Evaluate The View That The UK Constitution Should Be Codified And Entrenched. 

Introduction
Key Definitions
The UK Constitution is uncodified and unentrenched, meaning that it isnʼt written down in a
single document and can be changed with a simple Act of Parliament, rather than being
protected to a greater extent.
One of the key debates in relation to constitutional change is whether the UK constitution
should be codified in a single document and entrenched so that it couldnʼt be easily changed,
including a British Bill of Rights.

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Paragraph One ➡ Is The UKʼs Uncodified Constitution Too Easy
To Change?
For: Arguments That The UK Constitution Should Be Codified And Entrenched
One key argument in favour of the codification and entrenchment of the constitution is that the
British constitution is too easy to amend, as any statute can be repealed by Parliament,
whatever its constitutional implications, whilst significant constitutional changes can be
introduced by a simple majority in Parliament.
For example, the Fixed Term Parliament Act 2011 was arguably passed for short term
political reasons in relation to the coalition, but couldʼve had far reaching implications,
preventing a government with no majority from triggering an election.
Entrenchment would protect the constitution from political interference from an overarching
executive and make it more likely that potential reforms are fully debated before being enacted.
For example, supermajorities in both chambers of Parliament or a referendum could be
required before changing the constitution.
For example, it can be argued that in relation to Brexit it shouldn't have been so easy to change
a fundamental part of the constitution EU membership) on the basis of an advisory referendum
whose support commanded the support of less than 50% of the franchise.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The UK Constitution Shouldnʼt Be Codified And
Entrenched
On the other hand, it can be argued that the UKʼs constitution shouldnʼt be codified and
entrenched because the flexibility of Britain's uncodified constitution has allowed the British
political system to evolve gradually and adapt to unusual circumstances when necessary.
The constitution has evolved a great deal over the past two centuries, making Britain a
democratic system, and New Labour's programme demonstrates that major progressive
changes can be made.
For example, Labour removed the majority of hereditary peers from the House of Lords,
introduced the Human Rights Act 1998 to improve rights protection in the UK and
created the Supreme Court in the 2005 Constitutional Reform Act.
These delivered on New Labour programme of constitutional change that aimed to
democratise, modernise, decentralise and further the protection of rights.
If the UK constitution was codified and entrenched, these changes wouldʼve been a lot
more difficult and a key criticism of codified, entrenched constitutions is that they freeze
the balance of political power according to the prejudices of a particular moment, whereas
the unwritten British constitution continues to adapt to the changing needs of the nation.
The US constitution, for example, has been criticised for allowing thousands to die
through school shootings due to the 2nd amendment, which is seen by many to be no
longer relevant given its damaging effects and the huge power of the US military.
Further, codification would likely be a highly complex and controversial process that could take
years and distract politicians from governing the country, whilst there would likely be significant
disagreement over how the constitution should look.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Though the codification and entrenchment of the constitution would make the constitution more
difficult to change and ensure changes are more thought out, overall this isnʼt a strong
argument to codify and entrench the UK constitution, as its flexibility is a great advantage that
allows it to adapt and modernise.

Paragraph Two ➡ Does The Uncodified Constitution Offer
Sufficient Protections For Democracy?
For: Arguments That The UK Constitution Should Be Codified And Entrenched

Codification could enable parliament to better check and constrain the executive, making the
government more responsive and accountable to the people's representatives. It could do so by
clearly setting out the powers of different branches and giving them the ability to check the
power of each other, as is the case in the US.
For example, the House of Lords could become elected and therefore be given greater
powers, or parliament could have a veto on certain key decisions such as the appointment
of ministers.
The Supreme Court could also be given a key role in protecting the constitution, by giving it
the ability to strike down laws if they didnʼt comply with the constitution.
It can be argued that judges are neutral and arguably best placed to interpret the
constitution and make sure that it is applied fairly by public bodies, including parliament.
This would not only constrain the executive and lead to a greater separation of powers, it
would also reform an outdated, undemocratic aspect of the constitution.
This links in to a key criticism of the UKʼs current uncodified and unentrenched constitution;
that it could be easily exploited and changed by an authoritarian government that seeks to go
against democracy and extend its powers.
The fact that important parts of the constitution, such as the Representation Of The People
Act 1918, which gave the vote to women over 30 who met a property qualification and to all
men, are simply Acts of Parliament that can be removed/replaced with a simple majority,
illustrates this point.
For example, the Conservative government was able to easily prevent those without
relevant ID from voting in the Elections Act 2022, which many believe they did to restrict
those from voting who were less likely to vote for the Conservative Party.
The fact that the UKʼs current constitution relies of uncodified conventions has also been
criticised for this reason, as these conventions are unwritten and based on a common
understanding of best practice. As a consequence they can easily be denied and not
followed by a government.
This can be seen in Boris Johnsonʼs prorogation of Parliament in 2019. It had been a
convention that the governmentʼs power to prorogue Parliament shouldnʼt be used for
political reasons by the government, however Johnsonʼs government sought to use it to
prevent parliamentary scrutiny of their Brexit deal, which threatened the sovereignty of
Parliament.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The UK Constitution Shouldnʼt Be Codified And
Entrenched

On the other hand, it can be argued that the UK Constitution shouldnʼt be codified and
entrenched, because the current uncodified constitution can be seen as offering sufficient
protections for democracy.
Though conventions arenʼt entrenched, it can be argued they are very difficult to change
and the Supreme Court ensures they arenʼt exploited. Conventions clearly hold weight, as
shown by the backlash they generate when they are broken by the government, which
ensures democracy is protected.
When the government prorogued Parliament for political reasons in 2019, for example, it
generated significant public backlash and the Supreme Court used its powers of judicial
review to rule that the government had acted ultra vires and force it to reverse its decision.
Further, it can be argued that if greater checks and balances were introduced to the
constitution, it would make it very difficult to pass important legislation and to deal with
emergencies, which supporters of the UKʼs current uncodified constitution argue it is very
effective at doing so.
The uncodified nature of the constitution has allowed governments to quickly and
effectively deal with emergencies and deal with changed circumstances. This allows the
system to develop in a way that suits Britain and can be better kept up to date.
The emergency measures brought in to respond to COVID19, which significantly
impinged upon human rights, are a good example of this. As is the preparation for the
2010 coalition government, when a Cabinet Manual that adapted conventions such as
collective responsibility and the Fixed Term Parliaments Act was introduced.
The UKʼs current uncodified constitution also typically provides for strong governments that
can carry out their legislative programme without facing the same level of checks and
balances as in many codified systems.
British voters can be confident that governments elected with a good majority can
implement their manifesto pledges, unlike in countries such as the US, where it is very
difficult to pass legislation in important areas, such as climate change.
Further, one could argue that weaker governments with smaller majorities and lower
levels of public support face greater checks and balances, as Parliamentʼs ability to
check the executive and successfully rebel on bills increases.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, though there is no doubt that a codified and entrenched constitution would better
protect democracy in the UK, this isnʼt a sufficient argument for codification and entrenchment,
as greater checks and balances would come at the expense of preventing the government from
quickly dealing with emergencies and passing its key manifesto pledges, which are key benefits
of the current uncodified constitution.

Paragraph Three ➡ Would Codification And Entrenchment Better
Protect Human Rights?
For: Arguments That The UK Constitution Should Be Codified And Entrenched

A key argument for the codification and entrenchment of the constitution is that it would better
protect human rights in the UK, potentially by introducing a new British Bill of Rights that would
be entrenched and therefore couldnʼt be infringed upon by the government and other public
bodies.
It can be argued that this is necessary as the current Human Rights Act doesnʼt protect
rights effectively in the UK as parliamentary sovereignty means that judges canʼt legally
compel parliament to make changes to laws and the fact it is not entrenched means the
HRA could be repealed by a simple act of parliament.
The Illegal Migration Bill, for example, was introduced by Home Secretary Suella
Braverman on 7th March 2023 with a section 19 note stating that there was a likelihood
the provisions of the bill would be incompatible with the Human Rights Act and
international law The European Convention on Human Rights), but that the government
wanted to proceed with it nonetheless.
The government passed The Safety of Rwanda Asylum and Immigration) Act in April
2024. The act states that Rwanda must be considered a safe country for asylum
seekers and that international law must be put aside to allow for the deportation of
asylum seekers to Rwanda, therefore removing the individual right to judicial review on
these grounds.
It also gives ministers the power to ignore emergency rulings against the act by the
European Court Of Human Rights in Strasbourg.
This shows how Parliament and the government can threaten rights in the UK by
explicitly preventing the Supreme Court from using judicial review to challenge their
legislation.
An entrenched Bill of Rights would also prevents governments from overturning rights
legislation in order to remove rights, as the Conservative Party has often been criticised for
attempting to do in their proposals to replace the Human Rights Act with an unentrenched
British Bill of Rights.
It can be argued that a codified Bill of Rights would also give citizens a greater understanding
and attachment to their rights as citizens.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The UK Constitution Shouldnʼt Be Codified And
Entrenched
On the other hand, it can be argued that a codified constitution with an entrenched Bill of Rights
would simply increase the power of unelected, unaccountable and unrepresentative judges to
police the constitution and make difficult decisions about how rights should be applied,
dragging them into political matters and granting them too much political power
It is arguably better if Parliament resolves such issues and is accountable to the people, as
is currently the case.
Further, it can be argued that the Human Rights Act in fact currently provides a robust
framework for protecting the rights of British citizens. Since the HRA, it is often claimed that the
UK has developed a 'rights-based culture' as all new legislation must be compliant with the act
and judges can declare earlier acts of parliament incompatible with it, whilst public authorities
in the UK are under an obligation to abide by it.
Parliament usually acts to address any issues raised by the courts and parliament also has a
Joint Committee on Human Rights to scrutinise bills and ensure they are compatible,
demonstrating the 'persuasive influence' of the HRA.
For example, in A v Secretary of State for the Home Department, part of The Anti-terrorism,
Crime and Security Act 2001 was declared incompatible with Articles 5 and 14 of the human
rights act by permitting the detention of suspected international terrorists in a way that
discriminated on the grounds of nationality or immigration status. The Prevention of
Terrorism Act 2005 was amended by parliament as a result.
Further, the HRA already educates citizens about their rights and makes them more high
profile in the political system.
Further, Parliament can be seen as effective in preventing the government from encroaching
human rights by voting down bills, especially the House of Lords due to its lack of government
majority.
In 2005, for example, the House of Commons and House of Lords voted down Tony Blairʼs
proposal to allow the police to hold terrorist suspects for 90 days without charge, with a
compromise eventually passed at 28 days.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the argument that the UK constitution should be codified and entrenched in order to
better protect rights in unconvincing. The Human Rights Act and House of Lords currently
provide a robust framework for defending rights and do so in a way that doesnʼt give too much
power to elected, unaccountable judges, which codification likely would do.

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, the view that the UK constitution should be codified and entrenched is
unpersuasive. The UKʼs current constitution arguably provides sufficient protections for
democracy and rights.
Crucially, its uncodified and unentrenched nature also allows it to adapt with the times, whilst
allowing it to deal with emergencies and enabling governments to fulfil their manifesto pledges.
