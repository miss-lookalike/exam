Evaluate The View That Parliament Holds The Government To Account Effectively.

Introduction
Key Definitions
This essay looks at 3 key ways in which Parliament holds the government to account: select
committees, ministerial question time and legislative scrutiny

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Paragraph One ➡ Select Committees
For: Arguments That Select Committees Hold The Government To Account
Effectively
Select Committees look in depth into issues. Questioning is calm, measured and professional
and political answers aren't accepted. Scrutiny is therefore a lot more professional and less
partisan than PMQs. As a result it can be argued that Select Committees hold the government to
account effectively
Further, since the Wright Reforms, Select Committees are moire independent of the
government. Select Committee Chairs are no longer chosen by the whips, which has
allowed prominent backbench MPs who oppose the government on some issues to obtain
key roles in Select Committees.
High profile Labour Party backbencher and former shadow foreign secretary Emily
Thornberry, for example, is Chair of the Foreign Affairs Select Committee and has
criticised the governmentʼs approach to China, urging stronger action on human rights
abuses, including the treatment of Uyghur Muslims. She has also called for a more
assertive stance on Russian sanctions, arguing that loopholes still allow oligarchs to
shield assets in the UK.
The work of Select Committees is respected because it is evidence-based and the fact they
hold televised hearings increases their influence. As they air issues of public interest, they are
reported on in the media and therefore hold the government to account publicly.
In March 2023, for example, the Privileges Select Committee scrutinised former Prime
Minister Boris Johnson over his involvement in the ‘Partygateʼ scandal, which garnered a lot
of public interest and media attention.
The government has to publicly respond to their findings/reports in 8 weeks and they can
sometimes have a direct influence on government policy, ensuring that government
departments perform effectively.
In May 2023, the government accepted/partly accepted all of the Foreign Affairs Select
Committeeʼs recommendations in their report titled Refreshing our approach? Updating the
Integrated Review.
The government agreed with the committeeʼs assertion that an updated strategy was
needed and took on a number of recommendations, including appointing a Director of
Resilience in the Cabinet Office to focus on making the UK less dependent on other
international actors.
The Liaison Committee directly questions and scrutinises the Prime Minister twice a year,
therefore directly holding the most powerful figure in government to account.

**************************************************************************************************

For: Arguments That Select Committees Donʼt Hold The Government To
Account Effectively
A majority of Select Committee members are drawn from the governing party and there is a
tradition that the influential Treasury, foreign affairs and defence committees are chaired by
MPs of the governing party.
This limits their independence from the government and the likelihood they will hold the
government to account effectively.
In the current Parliament, 18/27 of the total Select Committee chairs are from the Labour
Party and there is a majority of Labour members in each committee, due to their huge 174
seat majority in the House of Commons.
Though available resources have increased, Select Committees can only cover a limited range
of topics in depth, therefore limiting their ability to hold the government to account across all
areas of policy and governance.
Select Committeesʼ power to summon witnesses is considerable but not unlimited
In March 2025, the government blocked the Joint Committee on National Securityʼs request
for the Government's National Security Advisor NSA Jonathan Powell to give evidence,
despite the fact that every previous NSA had given evidence since 2010 and the fact that
the committee is an influential one.
The government accepts about 40% of SC recommendations, but these are usually minor
changes. The actual influence of Select Committees on policy can therefore be seen as limited.
In the summer of 2023, the Education Select Committee, chaired by Conservative MP and
former Minister of State for School Standards Robin Walker MP, and with a majority of
Conservative Party MPs, made several recommendations aimed at enhancing support for
the early years sector.
These recommendations included additional funding and SEND support, the
reinstatement of previous staff-to-child ratios for two-year-olds, and exemptions from
business rates and VAT for early years settings.
The committee came to these recommendations through extensive inquiries involving
interviews with school and college leaders.
Despite the detailed recommendations, the government, in its response on October 17,
2023, chose not to implement them.
Boris Johnson twice cancelled appointments to attend the Liaison Committee and was therefore
able to avoid scrutiny.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, Select Committees are effective in holding the government to account.
Though they can only investigate a limited number of topics, the fact that they can draw on
significant expertise, their scrutiny is calm and professional results and their scrutiny can gain
significant coverage in the media means that their scrutiny is highly effective in relation to the
topics they do focus on.

Paragraph Two ➡ Ministerial Question Time (Especially Prime
Ministerʼs Questions)
For: Arguments That Ministerial Question Time Holds The Government To
Account Effectively
The Leader of the Opposition 6 questions) and the leader of the 3rd largest party 2 questions)
have the opportunity to get the government to respond directly to their questions in PMQs,
therefore giving them the ability to expose government failure and suggest why they would be
more effective.
The government is forced to address the concerns of the public, the Opposition and
backbenchers.
On 8 January 2025, the MP for Hackney North and Stoke Newington Dianne Abbott
raised concerns about the severe shortage of social housing in her constituency during
Prime Ministerʼs Questions PMQs) and demanded action, showing how MPs can force
the government to respond to the concerns of their constituents in PMQs.
Some Opposition leaders are highly effective in scrutinising the work of government. Before
becoming Prime Minister, Starmer was reasonably good, especially given his experience as
a prosecutor.
On 8 May 2024, Starmer used his first few questions in PMQs to effectively scrutinise
the reality of the impact of the Safety of Rwanda Bill and Rwanda plan, which he called a
‘gimmickʼ rather than effective policy.
He pointed out that it wasnʼt really effective as a deterrent, with 2400 small boats
crossings in the 16 days since it was passed, therefore publicly damaging Sunakʼs
government which had been trying to promote the effectiveness of the policy.
PMQs is televised every week and is the most watched aspect of politics among the public,
which can be seen as highly important to encouraging public engagement in politics and
exposing government failures to the public.
During a dramatic session of Prime Minister's Questions on 8 May 2024, Natalie Elphicke,
the MP for Dover, made headlines by defecting from the Conservative Party to the Labour
Party at the start of PMQs.
Her defection during PMQs was clearly designed to inflict maximum damage on the
Conservative Party and on Sunak, as it is televised and she knew it would gain major
media attention on a day when Starmer was criticising the governmentʼs immigration
policy anyway.
This shows how PMQs can have a significant impact in influencing voters, as MPs can use
them to make a political point, knowing that it will go viral and many voters will see it as it
dominates the media unlike politics during the rest of the week.
Misleading the House of Commons can lead to huge pressure to resign, which forces Ministers
and the Prime Minister to be competent and on top of their policy brief and the work of their
department.
This shows the accountability of the government to Parliament and decreases the likelihood
that incompetent party leaders who favour political demagoguery over rational argument
will lead a major party into an election, as they would embarrass and harm the partyʼs image
every Wednesday at PMQs.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That Ministerial Question Time Doesnʼt Hold The
Government To Account Effectively
It can be argued that PMQs in particular provides little effective scrutiny and is more focused on
partisan political point scoring than proper, detailed scrutiny of the government. Ministers and
Prime Ministers often give political answers, intended to deflect and get soundbites for social
media clips rather than to honestly answer scrutiny.
On the 13 November during PMQs, Prime Minister Keir Starmer was asked a question by
Reform UK Leader Nigel Farage, who questioned whether Starmer would prescribe Iran's
revolutionary guard as a terrorist organisation, following an assassination plot against
president-elect Donald Trump.
Rather than answering the question, Starmer responded by avoiding the question and
joking about Farage's frequent trips to America saying he would be "on the immigration
statistics".
This shows how PMQs often donʼt hold the government to account effectively, as PMs
often simply answer a different question to the one asked and resort to witty,
predetermined one-liners.
Further, many government backbenchers ask questions drafted by the whips, which are
intended to flatter rather than scrutinise the government.
For example, on the 13 November 2024, Labour MP for Southport, Patrick Hurley, asked
the Prime Minister if he could confirm that his budget would "provide much needed
investment" and properly support public services "unlike the previous government.ˮ
This was clearly planted by government whips in order to allow the Prime Minister to
condemn the Conservative's policy platform and tout the £1.3 billion investment into
local government announced in the budget.
PMQs is very boisterous and is more parliamentary theatre than effective scrutiny.
It can be argued this also presents a very negative view of politicians to the public,
therefore decreasing trust in politicians and the political process.
On May 24, 2023, during a particularly charged session of Prime Minister's Questions
PMQs), Speaker Sir Lindsay Hoyle ejected Conservative MP Paul Bristow from the chamber
for repeated heckling.
Effective scrutiny is very dependent on the abilities of backbenchers and the Leader of the
Opposition.
Kemi Badenoch has been criticised by many in the media for underperforming at PMQs and
failing to effectively hold the Prime Minister to account.
For example, in February 2025, she was criticised for failing to challenge the PM on the
Chagos Islands deal and whether the growing cost of the plan increasing to £18 billion is
compatible with his pledge not to return to austerity.
This meant questions on issues that are of high political importance went unanswered,
undermining the purpose of PMQs to forensically pin down the PM and provide scrutiny
on issues the Government would least like to talk about.
It can be argued that PMQs should be scrapped and replaced with greater use of other scrutiny,
such as the Liaison Committee, which provides far more in depth and meaningful questioning of
the Prime Minister.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
The effectiveness of the scrutiny that Ministerial Question Time and PMQs in particular provides
is in part dependent on the leader of the opposition.
Overall, even though the scrutiny of some questions is limited as it is partisan, Ministerial
Question Time and PMQs do effectively hold the government to account as they allow the
opposition to directly question key ministers, including the Prime Minister, publicly. This forces
them to defend the governmentʼs decisions and be on top of their policy brief.

Paragraph Three ➡ Legislative Scrutiny
For: Arguments That Parliamentʼs Legislative Scrutiny Holds The Government
To Account Effectively
It can be argued that Parliament is effective at holding the government to account through
legislative scrutiny, in particular due to the House of Lords.
Especially in recent years, when the government has increasingly rushed legislation through
the House of Commons, the House of Lords has played a crucial role in scrutinising and
revising legislation, as the government has less control over the Parliamentary timetable in
the Lords.
The Product Regulation And Meteorology Bill is a good example of how House of Lords
Amendments to improve bills are often accepted by the House of Commons.
When first introduced by the government, the bill contained huge delegated powers
giving UK ministers the power to unilaterally align the UK with EU regulations, as
part of Starmerʼs reset with the EU.
However, after criticism from the House of Lords Constitution Committee for how
this would have given too much control to the government and left MPs with little
ability to scrutinise the governmentʼs decisions, the government made changes to
the bill. These included introducing consultation requirements before ministers make
any regulations.
This shows how the House of Lords is highly effective at holding the
government to account and preventing executive overreach as it has the time
and expertise to scrutinise the complicated detail of legislation.
Further, though the House of Lords often backs down if its amendments are rejected by the
House of Commons, recognising its lack of democratic legitimacy, it sometimes doesnʼt and
takes a stand against a bill when it feels it is justified.
This is often to protect human rights or in relation to a bill that has garnered a significant
public backlash/has limited public support. This delaying power can be very significant
in influencing legislation and holding the government to account, especially in
emergencies when governments want to pass laws quickly.
For example, in 2001 ten defeats in the House Lords led the government to remove
incitement to religious hatred as an offence from its anti-terrorism legislation. The
House of Lords argued it wouldʼve threatened rights.
Parliament can also be seen as effective at holding the government to account in relation to
legislative scrutiny as the governmentʼs backbenchers can rebel to defeat government
legislation in the House of Commons, which has happened with increasing frequency in recent
years.
Theresa May was defeated thirty-three times when she had a minority government,
including suffering the worst defeat in modern political history 432202, with 118 Tories
voting against) on 15 January 2019, when her government tried to get Parliament to approve
its Brexit Withdrawal Agreement.
Parliament was therefore able to hold the government to account and tell it to renegotiate a
Brexit deal that would gain the support of a majority of Parliament.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That Parliamentʼs Legislative Scrutiny Doesnʼt Hold The
Government To Account Effectively
On the other hand, it can be argued that Parliamentʼs legislative scrutiny isnʼt effective in
holding the government to account, as the government has control over most of the
Parliamentary timetable and has in recent years increasingly rushed legislation through the
House of Commons, therefore limiting effective legislative scrutiny from backbenchers.
For example, the Passenger Railway Services Public Ownership) Act received just 1 day of
scrutiny in the Committee stage in the House of Commons and skipped the Report stage as
no amendments were accepted to the bill, therefore receiving little scrutiny despite being a
highly significant bill.
When the government has a large majority in the House of Commons, it is rarely defeated and
is able to pass almost all legislation through the chamber that it wants to.
Blair, for example, was defeated just four times in his 3 terms as Prime Minister.
Sunak was defeated just once as Prime Minister.
In the 2022/23 parliamentary session, the government introduced 56 bills. Of these, 43
(76% received royal assent by the end of the session.
Successful laws included the Illegal Migration Act and Online Safety Act.
Starmer has yet to face a significant defeat and has a large majority of 174 seats, making it
very unlikely he will be defeated in this Parliament.
Further, the legislative scrutiny of the House of Lords is limited by its lack of legislative powers.
The Parliaments Acts of 1911 and 1949 prevent the Lords from voting down any financial
bills and mean that the House of Lords can only delay legislation for up to a year, after
which the House of Commons (dominated by the government) can force through legislation.
This was used 3 times by the Blair government, including to pass the Hunting Act 2004,
which banned hunting with dogs.
The Salisbury Convention prevents the House of Lords from voting down any legislation that
fulfils a commitment in the governing partyʼs election manifesto.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the extent to which Parliamentʼs legislative scrutiny is effective in holding the
government to account is largely dependent on the size of the governmentʼs majority.
When the government has a large majority, the House of Commons has little power to hold it to
account. When it has a small majority, however, the House of Commons has much more power.
Even when the government does have a large majority, however, the House of Lords does
provide some effective legislative scrutiny, though its powers are limited by its lack of
legislative powers.

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, Parliament is effective at holding the government to account, though it is
certainly more effective at holding weak governments with small majorities to account than
strong governments with big majorities.
The House of Lords in particular is important in providing legislative scrutiny, whilst
ministerial question time is important in forcing the government to account for its decisions
and Select Committees provide effective calm and measured questioning.
