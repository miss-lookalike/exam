Evaluate The View That The Supreme Court Has Too Much Influence Over The Executive And Parliament. (30)

Paragraph One ➡ The Composition Of The Supreme Court
For: The Supreme Court Has Too Much Power Over The Executive and
Parliament

One key argument in favour of the Supreme Court having too much power over the executive
and parliament is that it shouldnʼt be able to have significant influence in the political process
because it is unelected and unrepresentative of the population.
The Supreme Court has been criticised for being highly unrepresentative in terms of race,
gender, class, education and age. Even the Times, the leading newspaper of the establishment,
used the phrase 'pale, male and stale' to head an article on the judiciary in 2011.
Of the current 12 justices, 11 are male, 12 are white, 11 have studied at Oxbridge and 12 are
over 60.
The fact that the court only had one female member was significant in the case of
'Radmacher v Granatino' 2010. It was a case involving a prenuptial agreement between
marriage partners, in which a majority of the justices upheld the principle that claims made
in the event of a divorce should be limited.
Lady Hale, the only female justice, was the only one of the 9 justices to dissent from the
majority verdict. She gave as her reason that the vast majority of people who would lose
out as a result of the precedent would be women and argued that it may be better for
Parliament to make such decisions where gender is a key element of the case, rather
than a very unrepresentative Supreme Court.
It can therefore be argued that the Supreme Court shouldnʼt exercise the significant power it
does in UK politics, because its narrow composition impedes its ability to uphold the principle of
judicial neutrality and gives old, privileged, unelected white men too much power over elected
governments and parliaments.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: The Supreme Court Doesnʼt Have Too Much Power Over The Executive
and Parliament
On the other had, it can be argued that the Supreme Court doesnʼt have too much power over
the executive and Parliament as even if it is unrepresentative, there are a number of rules and
protections to ensure the Supreme Court carries out its role with sufficient judicial neutrality.
Supreme Court justices, like civil servants, aren't allowed to campaign for political parties or
pressure groups.
Conflicts of interest are avoided by preventing Supreme Court judges from sitting on cases
that involve family, friends and others they know personally.
Judges have to base each decision in law and provide a full explanation of how they
reached it. Supreme Court decisions are published in full for people to read and scrutinise,
whilst cases are televised on YouTube.
This attempts to ensure that the Supreme Court is transparent and that it fulfils its role
without being influenced by personal political views.
To get onto the Supreme Court, a judge has to have served as a senior judge for 2
years, or been a qualified lawyer for at least 15 years and their elevation to the Supreme
Court is based on their past performance and experience, in which they have been
neutral.
The current President of the Supreme Court Lord Robert Reed, for example, has
served as a senior judge since 1998.
Further, an elected Supreme Court would likely lead to it becoming politicised and therefore
impede its ability to be independent, as can be seen in the United States.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, though the Supreme Court is unrepresentative of the population and this should
certainly be changed with future appointments, this shouldnʼt lead to a conclusion that the
Supreme Court has too much power over the executive and Parliament. It unelected nature is
important to it being able to hold the government to account and there are a number of
important protections that ensure it does so in a neutral way.

Paragraph Two ➡ The Supreme Courtʼs Role In Interpreting The
Human Rights Act
For: The Supreme Court Has Too Much Power Over The Executive and
Parliament

The Supreme Court plays an important role in ensuring that all legislation passed by Parliament
is compliant with the Human Rights Act (which is the European Convention on Human Rights
but in UK law).
Though the ECHR and HRA arenʼt legally superior to statute laws, the Supreme Court can
declare acts of parliament incompatible with the Human Rights Act and urge Parliament to
change them.
Parliament usually acts to address any declarations of incompatibility raised by the courts and
parliament also has a Joint Committee on Human Rights to scrutinise bills and ensure they are
compatible, therefore demonstrating the 'persuasive influence' of the HRA.
For example, in A v Secretary of State for the Home Department, part of The Anti-terrorism,
Crime and Security Act 2001 was declared incompatible with Articles 5 and 14 of the human
rights act by permitting the detention of suspected international terrorists in a way that
discriminated on the grounds of nationality or immigration status. The Prevention of
Terrorism Act 2005 was introduced by Parliament to amend the legislation as a result,
bringing UK legislation in line with the HRA.
More recently, in R Steinfeld and Keiden) v Secretary of State for International
Development in 2018, the Civil Partnership Act 2004 was declared incompatible with
articles 14 and 8 of the Human Rights Act as they precluded different sex couples from
entering civil partnerships. The legislation was then amended in 2019 in the Civil
Partnerships, Marriages and Deaths Act.
It can therefore be argued that the Supreme Court has too much power over the executive and
Parliament as they have significant influence over human rights law in the UK, despite being
unelected, and can usually compel a democratically elected Parliament and government to
change legislation.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: The Supreme Court Doesnʼt Have Too Much Power Over The Executive
and Parliament
On the other hand, it can be argued that the Supreme Court doesnʼt have too much power over
the executive and Parliament because Parliament and the executive retain the ultimate decision
making power and the Supreme Courtʼs role in seeking to ensure the UK has a rights based
culture is important and legitimate.
Whilst the Supreme Court can issue a declaration of incompatibility in relation to the Human
Rights Act, they have no legal powers to compel parliament and the government to change
the offending Act of Parliament due to Parliament being sovereign.
The Illegal Migration Bill, for example, was introduced by Home Secretary Suella Braverman
on 7th March 2023 with a section 19 note stating that there was a likelihood the provisions
of the bill would be incompatible with the Human Rights Act and international law The
European Convention on Human Rights), but that the government wanted to proceed with it
nonetheless.
Further, it can be argued that the Supreme Court doesnʼt have too much power as Parliament
could easily repeal/change the Human Rights Act if it wanted to.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the Supreme Court doesnʼt have too much power over the executive and Parliament in
relation to human rights. It plays a highly important, legitimate role in protecting rights in the UK,
yet does so in a way that maintains Parliamentary Sovereignty and leaves the ultimate decision
making power in the hands of the elected Parliament and government.

Paragraph Three ➡ The Supreme Courtʼs Powers Of Judicial
Review
For: The Supreme Court Has Too Much Power Over The Executive and
Parliament
The Supreme Court has the power to ensure that the government hasnʼt acted beyond the
authority given to them by the law in ‘ultra viresʼ cases. If the government is judged to have
acted ‘ultra viresʼ, the Supreme Court can sanction the government or force them to reverse
their action.
It can be argued that the Supreme Court has too much power over the executive and
Parliament because, despite being unelected, it can overturn the actions of a democratically
elected government with a national mandate.
In the 2019 Prorogation case, Boris Johnsonʼs government prorogued (temporarily suspended)
parliament, arguing that the prorogation was a routine one that ordinarily follows the selection
of a new Prime Minister, but the Supreme Court disagreed and unanimously ruled that the
prorogation was unlawful, reversing the prorogation and reinstating parliament.
It can be argued that the Supreme Court has too much power as they were able to reverse
the actions of a democratically elected government.
In the 2024 Jackdaw and Rosebank Oil and Gas Case, the Supreme Court ruled that the
previous Conservative Government had broken the law in 2023 and 2024, when it approved
new oil and gas projects Jackdaw and Rosebank without properly considering the full
environmental impact.
This decision revoked the approvals for the project until the new government approved
them again whilst properly considering the full environmental consequences of the projects.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: The Supreme Court Doesnʼt Have Too Much Power Over The Executive
and Parliament
On the other hand, it can be argued that the Supreme Court doesnʼt have too much power over
the executive and parliament, as it simply uses its power of judicial review to uphold the law and
prevent governments from acting ‘ultra viresʼ.
In the prorogation case, the Supreme Court simply prevented Boris Johnson from avoiding
parliamentary scrutiny through using the power to prorogue for the governmentʼs benefit,
therefore legitimately upholding the sovereignty of Parliament.
Further, the Supreme Courtʼs power of judicial review is sometimes used to protect the power of
the UK Parliament and government against devolved bodies.
In the 2022 Second Referendum on Scottish Independence Case, for example, the UK
Supreme Court ruled that the Scotland Act 1998 doesnʼt give the Scottish Parliament the
power to unilaterally legislate for an independence referendum.
Ruling against the Scottish Government, it sided with the UK Government and ruled that the
consent of the UK Parliament was necessary.
Crucially, the government and parliament can change the law if it disagrees with a Supreme
Courtʼs ruling and remove the courtʼs power of judicial review in certain cases. After the law has
been changed, the Supreme Court has to implement this new law. This has happened in 2 key
cases recently:
The Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, which was introduced to repeal the
Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011, also included an ouster clause, which explicitly removed
the Supreme Courtʼs power of judicial review in relation to the dissolution of Parliament.
The law states that a court cannot question the exercise of the Prime Ministerʼs power to
dissolve Parliament or the limit or extent of these powers. They are ‘non-justiciableʼ.
This can be seen as a direct response to the Supreme Courtʼs 2019 decision (in the
‘Miller 2ʼ case) to reverse the governmentʼs prorogation of Parliament, ensuring that
judicial review in relation to this power canʼt happen again.
This shows the limits of the Supreme Courtʼs power, as Parliament can pass laws to
limit the Supreme Courtʼs power and change the law if they donʼt like how it is being
implemented.
The government passed The Safety of Rwanda Asylum and Immigration) Act in April 2024.
The act states that Rwanda must be considered a safe country for asylum seekers and that
international law and the Human Rights Act must be put aside to allow for the deportation of
asylum seekers to Rwanda, therefore removing the individual right to judicial review on
these grounds.
This followed the courtʼs November 2023 ruling that the governmentʼs Rwanda plan was
illegal, citing risks of refoulement and inadequacies in Rwanda's asylum system as
major concerns. They argued that as a result, the policy was against the Human Rights
Act and the 1951 Refugee Convention, as Rwanda couldnʼt be defined as a ‘safe
countryʼ.
This shows that the Supreme Court can only protect rights if the law does and that
parliament can change the law to make their policy legal if the Supreme Court declares
it illegal.
Therefore the Supreme Courtʼs power over the executive and Parliament is limited

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the Supreme Court doesnʼt have too much power over the executive and parliament in
relation to judicial review. Instead, its power to stop a government from acting ‘ultra viresʼ is key
to the protection of democracy in the UK and often protects the power of Parliament against an
overarching executive.

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, the Supreme Court doesnʼt have too much power over the executive and
parliament, instead its power is highly legitimate and limited, yet important in protecting
democracy in the UK.
The Supreme Court plays a key role in protecting human rights in the UK whilst ensuring the
sovereignty of Parliament is still in tact. Its judicial review powers are also highly important in
protecting the sovereignty and democratic rights of Parliament against an overarching
executive. It does all this with sufficient judicial neutrality and independence due to a number of
important protections.
