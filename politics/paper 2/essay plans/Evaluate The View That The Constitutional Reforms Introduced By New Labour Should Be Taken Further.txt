Evaluate The View That The Constitutional Reforms Introduced By New Labour Should Be Taken Further.

Introduction
Key Definitions
‘New Labourʼs Constitutional Reformsʼ refers to a number of reforms introduced by Tony Blairʼs
governments, including the Supreme Court, Human Rights Act 1998, House of Lords Reform,
Electoral Reform and Devolution

Paragraph One ➡ House Of Lords
For: Arguments That The Constitutional Reforms Introduced By New Labour
Should Be Taken Further
One key way in which New Labourʼs constitutional reforms should be taken further is through
making the House of Lords elected.
New Labour attempted a two stage plan to reform the House of Lords. The first would
remove all hereditary peers and the second would replace the Lords with an elected second
chamber.
Not only was stage 1 not fully carried out as 92 hereditary peers remained, stage 2
didnʼt happen at all as many Labour MPs felt the key problems of hereditary peers and
Conservative dominance had been dealt with, whilst there was a lack of agreement on
what the new chamber should look like.
It can be argued that the House of Lords should be replaced with an elected second chamber
as is fundamentally undemocratic and in a democracy houses of Parliament should be based
on popular consent.
This is a view supported by Keir Starmer who in December 2022 announced plans to
abolish the House of Lords and replace it with a new democratically elected second
chamber. Starmer criticised recent Tory governments for undermining trust in politics by
handing peerages to ‘lackeys and donorsʼ, such as Boris Johnsonʼs friend and Evening
Standard owner Evgeny Lebedev and billionaire Conservative Party donor David Harding.
Labourʼs 2024 manifesto proposed reforming the chamber by removing hereditary peers,
introducing a mandatory retirement age of 80 and reforming the appointments process to
ensure higher quality peers and a better regional balance.
The manifesto also stated that Labour is committed to replacing the chamber with
alternative chamber that is more representative of the regions and nations of the UK.
Since taking office, the Labour government hasnʼt yet introduced any legislation on the
mandatory retirement age and hasnʼt set up a public consultation, but it has introduced
the House of Lords Hereditary Peers) Bill, which seeks to remove the remaining 92
hereditary peers who retain their seats in the Lords.
It can be argued that being elected would embolden the second chamber and result in more
effective scrutiny of government legislation.
The House of Lordsʼ current unelected nature limits its ability to scrutinise legislation, whilst
many members of the House of Lords also fail to turn up regularly or play an active,
professional role.
According to the Byline Times, 13% of peers rarely or never attend the House of Lords.
Lord Sugar has only cast votes in 1.96% of divisions since joining the House of Lords.
If a new chamber was elected through proportional representation, it likely wouldnʼt have a
majority and would therefore be able to effectively challenge the dominance of the House of
Commons and change the current system from being an ‘elective dictatorshipʼ.
If elected through proportional representation, a new second chamber would be much more
representative of the electorate, especially in comparison to the House of Lords which is very
unrepresentative in terms of demographics and the House of Commons which is
unrepresentative due to First Past The Post.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The Constitutional Reforms Introduced By New
Labour Shouldnʼt Be Taken Further
On the other hand, it can be argued that the House of Lords shouldnʼt be made elected, as if it
was it would be able to claim democratic legitimacy and be a lot more assertive. This would risk
causing gridlock in Parliament and prevent important legislation from being passed.
This would be especially likely if it was elected at a different time to the House of Commons
and if it was elected using proportional representation.
The current appointed nature of the House of Lords can be seen as an advantage, as life peers
have a lot of expertise in different areas and can therefore provide effective scrutiny.
Lord Mair, for example, is a geotechnical engineer and Professor of Civil Engineering at the
University of Cambridge and he is able to bring this expertise to his role as chair of the
House of Lords Science and Technology Committee.
The House of Lords plays a key role in providing legislative scrutiny, which the House of
Commons no longer has time for, as they are focused on representing their constituents and
getting elected.
The Levelling-Up and Regeneration Act, passed in October 2023, is a good example of
how House of Lords Amendments to improve bills are often accepted by the House of
Commons.
In total, the government accepted 64 House of Lords amendments to the bill.
The vast majority of these were ‘tecnical amendmentsʼ that fixed issues with the
bill and improved the wording so that it would function better, rather than
seeking major changes to the outcome of the bill itself.
For example, amendment 117 replaced the word “consultˮ with “obtain the
consent ofˮ in line 9 of the bill.
The current balance of the two chambers can be seen as working, with the House of Lords
being a less powerful revising chamber that can hold the government to account on key issues
using its delaying power when it needs to.
For example, in 2001 ten defeats in the House Lords led the government to remove
incitement to religious hatred as an offence from its anti-terrorism legislation. The House of
Lords was therefore able to defend democracy in the UK.
The fact that it defers to the House of Commons when there is strong government and
public support for a policy can be seen as a positive.
For example, in April 2024 after six rounds of parliamentary ping pong, the House of
Lords backed down on its amendments to the Safety of Rwanda Bill, recognising its lack
of legitimacy.
An elected second chamber would likely be more concerned with party politics than anything
else, even if it was intended to represent different regions.
This can be seen in the Senate in America, which is very partisan and divided between
Democrats and Republicans.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the more persuasive argument is that the House of Lords doesnʼt require further
reform. Though it is undemocratic, the current House of Lords is professional and effective in
scrutinising the government legislation, whilst providing an effective balance with the House of
Commons that favours the elected latter and allows legislation to be passed effectively. Making
the House of Lords elected would negatively disrupt this balance.

Paragraph Two ➡ Devolution
For: Arguments That The Constitutional Reforms Introduced By New Labour Should Be Taken Further

One key potential further reform to devolution is introducing further devolution to England. A
key problem with the current system of devolution is that it is asymmetric, with England lacking
the level of democratic representation that Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
An English Parliament would complete devolution within the UK, making it symmetrical and
granting the English population the same level of representation as the rest of the UK.
This would allow for the policy preferences of English people to be represented, such as
has been the case in Scotland. The Scottish population is more left wing than the rest of
the UK and the Scottish Parliament has been able to represent this in policies such as
free tuition fees and higher income tax.
If an English Parliament wasnʼt introduced, a greater use of city regions/regional assemblies
to cover the whole of England could be introduced instead.
This would bring decision making closer to the people and address the differing
interests of each English region, therefore improving democracy and representation.
The Greater Manchester City Region, which has the elected mayor Andy Burnham,
for example, has been effective in gaining significant administrative control,
including over healthcare.
Devolution could also be further reformed by giving further powers to existing devolved bodies,
which have shown they can run public services and decide policy effectively, including during
the COVID crisis. Giving them greater power would increase their ability to reflect local opinion
and address local issues.
Scotland, for example, could be granted further powers over transport, welfare policy and
tax policy to get closer towards ‘Devomaxʼ.
This would allow it to further reflect the more left wing views of the Scottish population in
policy.
The SNP for example, supports the devolution of immigration policy to Scotland.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The Constitutional Reforms Introduced By New
Labour Shouldnʼt Be Taken Further
On the other hand, it can be argued that devolution shouldnʼt be taken further by introducing
devolution to England, as there is little demand for it and reform would create more problems
than it would solve.
An English Parliament would only serve to create another layer of government and would
create tensions between the English parliament and government and the UK-wide
government, with the former challenging the latter.
These tensions would be greater and the relationship more fraught than the current
relationship between the UK Parliament and devolved bodies, as around 85% of the
UKʼs population is in England, which would lead to the English Parliament being very
powerful.
Federalism arguably works best when there is no dominant state or region, such as
in the US.
A greater use of regional assemblies/city regions would also create the potential for
tensions with local councils. As these assemblies would also cover large populations, there
would be the risk of urban interests dominating and drowning out rural interests.
Further, whilst areas such as Yorkshire and Cornwall have a strong sense of regional
identity, many others such as Berkshire donʼt and would likely attract very little
attention/support as a consequence.
There is also little public support for a regional layer of government in England.
A 2012 referendum on whether Birmingham should have an elected mayor received a
58% turnout on just a 27.67% turnout
It can also be argued that advances in recent years have meant that these reforms have
gone far enough. Devolved bodies including combined authorities and directly elected
metro mayors now cover 64% of the English population and over 90% of the population in
the North.
Finally, it can be argued that there shouldnʼt be further reforms to existing devolved bodies as
they have failed to prove their effectiveness and the economic and policy benefits of
devolution.
Scottish education has been drastically changed by the SNP, but Scotland has since fallen
behind England in the PISA Programme for International Student Assessment) rankings.
The Northern Irish Parliament is frequently suspended due to tensions and a lack of
compromise between Sinn Fein and the DUP. It therefore makes little sense to grant further
powers to a Parliament which rarely functions.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the more persuasive argument is that New Labourʼs devolution reforms shouldnʼt be
taken further. Neither and English Parliament nor greater use of regional assemblies/city
regions provides a popular and logical possible reform, whilst existing devolved bodies already
have significant powers and have failed to provide enough success in governing effectively to
be given further powers.

Paragraph Three ➡ Human Rights Act 1998
For: Arguments That The Constitutional Reforms Introduced By New Labour
Should Be Taken Further

One key constitutional reform introduced by New Labour was the Human Rights Act 1998,
which enshrined the European Convention on Human Rights into UK law, meaning that the
rights could be defended in UK courts rather than having to go to Strasbourg.
It can be argued that this reform should be taken further as it fails to effectively protect rights in
the UK. The HRA may be used to check other laws, but it is not entrenched and could still be
repealed by a simple act of parliament, whilst also not being binding on parliament.
Parliamentary sovereignty is therefore still in tact.
The Illegal Migration Bill, for example, was introduced by Home Secretary Suella Braverman
on 7th March 2023 with a section 19 note stating that there was a likelihood the provisions
of the bill would be incompatible with the Human Rights Act and international law The
European Convention on Human Rights), but that the government wanted to proceed with it
nonetheless.
The government passed The Safety of Rwanda Asylum and Immigration) Act in April 2024.
The act states that Rwanda must be considered a safe country for asylum seekers and that
international law and the Human Rights Act must be put aside to allow for the deportation of
asylum seekers to Rwanda, therefore removing the individual right to judicial review on
these grounds.
It also gives ministers the power to ignore emergency rulings against the act by the
European Court Of Human Rights in Strasbourg.
This shows how Parliament and the government can threaten rights in the UK by
explicitly preventing the Supreme Court from using judicial review to challenge their
legislation. The Human Rights Act can therefore be seen as not sufficiently protecting
rights in the UK.
It can therefore be argued that it should be taken further by introducing a British Bill of Rights
and entrenching it as a part of the constitution by giving the Supreme Court the power to strike
down laws passed by Parliament that break it.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The Constitutional Reforms Introduced By New
Labour Shouldnʼt Be Taken Further
On the other hand, it can be argued that an entrenched Bill of Rights would simply increase the
power of unelected, unaccountable and unrepresentative judges to police the constitution and
make difficult decisions about how rights should be applied, dragging them into political matters
and granting them too much political power
It is arguably better if Parliament resolves such issues and is accountable to the people, as
is currently the case.
Further, it can be argued that the Human Rights Act in fact currently provides a robust
framework for protecting the rights of British citizens. Since the HRA, it is often claimed that the
UK has developed a 'rights-based culture' as all new legislation must be compliant with the act
and judges can declare earlier acts of parliament incompatible with it, whilst public authorities
in the UK are under an obligation to abide by it.
Parliament usually acts to address any issues raised by the courts and parliament also has a
Joint Committee on Human Rights to scrutinise bills and ensure they are compatible,
demonstrating the 'persuasive influence' of the HRA.
For example, in A v Secretary of State for the Home Department, part of The Anti-terrorism,
Crime and Security Act 2001 was declared incompatible with Articles 5 and 14 of the human
rights act by permitting the detention of suspected international terrorists in a way that
discriminated on the grounds of nationality or immigration status. The Prevention of
Terrorism Act 2005 was amended by parliament as a result.
The Human Rights Act also currently maintains the sovereignty of Parliament, which is a key
part of the UK constitution.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the more persuasive argument is that the Human Rights Act 1998 shouldnʼt be taken
further, as it effectively protects rights in the UK whilst maintaining Parliamentary sovereignty.
An entrenched Bill of Rights would give too much power to unelected judges and decrease the
sovereignty of Parliament, which is elected.

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, the constitutional reforms introduced by New Labour shouldnʼt be taken further.
The Human Rights Act, House of Lords Act 1999 and Devolution reforms had a significant
positive impact on the UK constitution: they provided a robust framework for the defence of
rights, modernised Parliament and improved democracy in the UK.
The potential benefits of proposed further reforms would have many negative unintended
consequences and disrupt the current balance of the constitution, whilst there is little
support for them.
