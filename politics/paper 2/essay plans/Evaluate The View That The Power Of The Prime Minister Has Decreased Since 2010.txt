Evaluate The View That The Power Of The Prime Minister Has Decreased Since 2010.

Paragraph One ➡ The Extent To Which Prime Ministers Can
Control Their Cabinet
For: Arguments That The Power Of The Prime Minister Has Decreased Since
2010

One key argument that the power of the Prime Minister has decreased since 2010 is that they
have been unable to control their cabinets, with frequent leaking, breaking of collective
ministerial responsibility and Prime Ministers being brought down by cabinet resignations. This
is in large part due to the fact that multiple Prime Ministers since 2010 have become unpopular
with the public.
This compares in particular with Blair before 2010, who was a very strong Prime Minister
who had strong control over his party and cabinet.
Theresa May was unable to control her cabinet effectively, with a major breakdown in collective
ministerial responsibility.
Foreign Secretary Boris Johnson consistently leaked his dissatisfaction with government
policy and briefed against the Prime Minister, including writing critical weekly articles in the
Daily Telegraph. Due to his popularity within the Conservative Party, particularly with
Brexiteers, and Mayʼs weakness as a Prime Minister, she wasnʼt able to sack him, though.
During the Brexit process, multiple ministers not only spoke out against government policy,
but also voted against it in the House of Commons.
The decreasing power of Prime Ministers over their cabinets can also be seen under Liz Truss
and Boris Johnson, who were ultimately forced to resign after key members of their cabinets
challenged their leadership and resigned from the government.
Truss in particular sought to drive through policy herself in a similar way to how Blair was
able to, but became very unpopular and was removed after just 54 days as Prime Minister.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The Power Of The Prime Minister Hasnʼt Decreased
Since 2010
On the other hand, it can be argued that the power of the Prime Minister hasnʼt decreased since
2010 as both Rishi Sunak and now Keir Starmer have demonstrated more effective control of
their cabinets, with less leaking and collective ministerial responsibility being maintained.
One key explanation for this in relation to Sunak is that following Bravermannʼs sacking in
late 2023, there were no big beasts within the cabinet who could claim to represent/lead a
faction of the Conservative Party. They therefore lacked the power to challenge the Prime
Minister without easily being fired, despite Sunak being very unpopular.
Starmer too is extremely unpopular, with a net favourability rating of 40% in February
2025, but has also faced limited leaking and no real challenge to his power.
This is arguably down to three reasons. Firstly, very few members of the cabinet could
be considered ‘big beastsʼ, as almost none are household names. Secondly, Starmer has
a huge 174-seat majority in Parliament and is therefore able to largely pass what he
wants and drive through his legislative agenda. Thirdly, it is early on in his leadership
and the cabinet are likely giving him some time to turn around the governmentʼs
popularity.
Further, Prime Ministerʼs prior to 2010 certainly didnʼt have complete power over their cabinets.
Thatcher was forced out of power by her cabinet, whilst John Major struggled to preside over a
cabinet that was greatly divided over Europe.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, it is clear that the power of the Prime Minister over their cabinet has decreased since
2010. Prime Ministers have been weaker and less popular, which has emboldened the cabinet
to leak their dissatisfaction with the Prime Minister and remove a number of Prime Ministers.

Paragraph Two ➡ The Prerogative Powers Of The Prime Minister
For: Arguments That The Power Of The Prime Minister Has Decreased Since
2010

One key argument that the power of the Prime Minister has decreased since 2010 is in relation
to the PMʼs prerogative powers, which traditionally belonged to the monarch but were over time
transferred to the Prime Minister.
One of the key prerogative powers is in relation to declaring war and authorising the use of the
armed forces. It can be argued that this has decreased since 2010, with it now a convention that
House of Commons consent is required before enacting major military action.
In 2013, for example, David Cameron lost a vote on airstrikes in Syria in response to Assadʼs
use of chemical weapons and backed down.
Another key prerogative power that has been limited since 2010 is the Prime Ministerʼs power to
call a snap General Election when they wish.
This was removed by the 2011 Fixed Term Parliaments Act, which required a vote of no
confidence in the government or support from 2/3rds of MPs in order to trigger an election.
A final prerogative power that can be seen to have decreased since 2010 is the Prime Ministerʼs
power of patronage. In particular their ability to enforce collective ministerial responsibility.
From the outset of the 2010 coalition government, it was agreed that Liberal Democrat
ministers wouldn't be bound by collective responsibility on four key issues on which they
strongly disagreed with the Conservatives.
Liberal Democrat ministers were allowed to abstain in votes on: 1 the construction of
nuclear power stations 2 tax allowances for married couples 3 higher education
funding and 4 the renewal of Trident.
Collective Ministerial Responsibility also had to be suspended during the EU referendum,
with key ministers including Michael Gove speaking against the governmentʼs position of
remaining in the EU.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The Power Of The Prime Minister Hasnʼt Significantly
Decreased Since 2010

On the other hand, it can be argued that the power lost through these developments has in fact
been limited. In reality the Prime Minister is still able to exercise their prerogative powers
effectively.
The PMʼs continued power of patronage can be seen in the fact that David Cameron was
appointed as Foreign Secretary by Rishi Sunak in November 2023, marking his return to
frontline politics seven years after resigning as Prime Minister.
To assume the role, Cameron was made a peer as Lord Cameron of Chipping Norton,
breaking with the convention that the key offices of state are traditionally filled by members
of the House of Commons.
This highlighted the extent of the Prime Minister's powers of patronage, one of the Prime
Ministerʼs key prerogative powers, as he could bypass parliamentary norms to recruit a
trusted ally.
Despite the convention that Prime Ministers should ask for the House of Commonsʼ consent
before enacting major military action, in January 2024, Rishi Sunakʼs government carried out
joint air-strikes with the US against Houthi targets in response to the Yemeni group attacking
cargo ships in the Red Sea.
It did so without asking for parliamentary consent, breaking the convention established
since Blairʼs invasion of Iraq in 2003 that Parliament should be asked before the government
carried out significant military interventions.
The Fixed Term Parliaments Act was largely ineffective, as shown in the 2017 and 2019
elections, when the governments were able to shame the opposition into voting for an election,
which they are unlikely to block as it would make them seem very weak.
As a consequence, The Fixed Term Parliaments Act was repealed by Boris Johnsonʼs
government in 2022, restoring the prerogative power to call a snap election.
Finally, collective ministerial responsibility may have been suspended briefly on certain issues,
but it remains important the vast majority of the time and is key to maintaining the Prime
Ministerʼs power.
In November 2023, Sunak sacked Suella Braverman as Home Secretary following a
controversial opinion piece in the Times about the Metropolitan Police's handling of pro-
Palestinian protests, accusing the police of "playing favourites" and describing protesters
as "hate marchers."
Braverman's piece was published without being reviewed and approved by Downing
Street, therefore violating the ministerial code.
Her statements were seen as a direct challenge to Sunak's authority and were widely
criticised by members of the Conservative Party.
Collective ministerial responsibility has also been upheld under Starmer, with very limited
leaking.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, in relation to prerogative powers, there were some ways in which the power of the
Prime Minister decreased since 2010, but these should be seen as minor, especially since they
have mostly been reversed after being introduced.

Paragraph Three ➡ The Power Of The Prime Minister Over
Parliament
For: Arguments That The Power Of The Prime Minister Has Decreased Since
2010

A final way in which the power of the Prime Minister can be argued to have decreased since
2010 is in relation to their control over Parliament.
The Wright Reforms, introduced in 2010 to increase the power of Backbenchers, can be seen
as limiting the power of the Prime Minister over Parliament and increasing the effectiveness of
House of Commons scrutiny of the government and PM.
Prior to the Wright Reforms, the chairs of Select Committees used to be selected by party
whips, which limited the effectiveness of their government scrutiny.
Following the Wright Reforms in 2010, however, chairs are now elected by their fellow
MPs, which has increased their independence. Members are chosen by secret ballot
within party groups, which has allowed prominent backbench MPs who oppose the
government on some issues to obtain key roles in Select Committees.
High profile Labour Party backbencher and former shadow foreign secretary Emily
Thornberry, for example, is Chair of the Foreign Affairs Select Committee and has
criticised the governmentʼs approach to China, urging stronger action on human rights
abuses, including the treatment of Uyghur Muslims. She has also called for a more
assertive stance on Russian sanctions, arguing that loopholes still allow oligarchs to
shield assets in the UK.
The Wright Reforms also introduced the Backbench Business Committee in 2010, which
gave backbenchers more of a say on the agenda of Parliament, therefore reducing the
governmentʼs control.
The BBBC chooses the topic of debate for 35 days in each parliamentary session, which
equates to around 1 day per week. In February 2023, for example, the BBBC held a
debate in the House of Commons on the future of the NHS, including its funding and
staffing.
Due to the fact that Cameron, May and at first Johnson had relatively small/no majorities when
they were Prime Minister, their governments were also frequently defeated in the House of
Commons, which prevented them from passing key pieces of legislation.
Cameron was defeated seven times when in charge of the coalition government and three
times when he had a Conservative majority government.
Theresa May was defeated thirty-three times when she had a minority government. She
also suffered the worst defeat in modern political history 432202, with 118 Tories voting
against) on 15 January 2019, when her government tried to get Parliament to approve its
Brexit Withdrawal Agreement.
Boris Johnson was defeated twelve times in just 6 months when he had a minority
government and four times in 3 years when he had a majority government.
This compares with Tony Blair, who was defeated just four times in his 10 years in office, all
of which were in his third term.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The Power Of The Prime Minister Hasnʼt Decreased
Since 2010
On the other had, it can be argued that the power of the Prime Minister over Parliament hasnʼt
decreased since 2010, as the government is still able to pass most of the legislation it wants
and more recently governments with large majorities have been elected.
Despite Sunak and his government being very unpopular throughout his leadership, he
faced few problems passing legislation due to the significant majority secured by the
Conservative Party in the 2019 election.
He was defeated just once as Prime Minister and in the 2022/23 parliamentary session,
the government introduced 56 bills. Of these, 43 76% received royal assent by the end
of the session. Successful laws included the Illegal Migration Act and Online Safety Act.
With Starmer securing the biggest majority since 1997, with a huge 174 seat majority in the
2024 election, he has faced no problems passing legislation so far and will likely be able to
pass a number of significant legislative proposals
Therefore, even though electoral support for the two main parties has decreased, the FPTP
electoral system and government dominance of the legislative process mean that the
executive is still able to dominate Parliament and the power of the Prime Minister hasnʼt
decreased.
The Prime Minister also has significant control over secondary legislation and budget decisions
On 12 July 2024, the Labour Government allowed prisoners to be released after 40% of
their term, rather than the previous 50%, allowing 1000's of prisoners to be released in
order to tackle overcrowding.
This was implemented through a statutory instrument, rather than through primary
legislation being passed.A statutory instrument is a form of secondary legislation that
does not require a full vote in Parliament, thus allowing the government to make the
change without extensive parliamentary debate.
This shows the power of the government to make significant changes through
secondary legislation.
In February 2025, the government reduced the UKʼs international aid budget from 0.5% to
0.3% of Gross National Income GNI to help fund an increase in defence spending to 2.5%
of GDP by 2027.
This decision was made without passing new legislation, as the government has the
executive authority to reallocate departmental budgets during the annual spending
review.
Further, though Prime Ministers with small majorities are weaker than those with big majorities,
they are still able to pass most legislation they like as they can whip their MPs effectively most
of the time and the House of Lords has limited powers to block legislation.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the power of the Prime Minister over Parliament has reduced somewhat since 2010, in
large part due to the smaller majorities that some governments have had and the increased
independence of the House of Commons to scrutinise governments.
This has led to more government defeats and the Prime Minister being scrutinised and
exposed more effectively, both of which have played an important role in bringing down
recent Prime Ministers.
Though the current government has a huge majority, it is yet to be seen whether they will
reverse the trend and Starmerʼs power will be much greater than other recent Prime
Ministers, especially as he is much less popular than Blair was when he had a similar
majority.

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, the power of the Prime Minister has decreased significantly since 2010. Though
the formal powers of the Prime Minister havenʼt decreased much, their power over both the
Cabinet and Parliament has been undermined due to Prime Ministers having small majorities
and being unpopular.
Both the Cabinet and Parliament have challenged the power of Prime Ministers effectively
through defeating government legislation and replacing unpopular Prime Ministers.
Though Starmer now has a huge government majority, it is possible that his power will still be
far more limited than Blairʼs was due to him being much more unpopular with the public.
