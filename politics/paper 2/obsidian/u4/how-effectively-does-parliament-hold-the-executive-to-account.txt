The [[executive]] sits within [[parliament]]. This means the executive and [[legislature]] are fused. Due to parliamentary [[sovereignty]] the executive is accountable to the [[house-of-commons]] since it represents the will of the people.

Despite this many claim parliament fails to hold the executive to account. In 1976, Lord Hailsham claimed that the executive had enough power to be called an [[elective-dictatorship]].

The claim can be supported by:

- The government has a lot of control over parliamentary business which limits the oppositions ability to debate government [[legislation]].
- Since [[public-bill-committees]] are government majority and whipped it is is unlikely the opposition can change much at committee stage
- A government with a large majority would be able to pass whatever it wishes
- The government can change the law using [[secondary-legislation]] to change the law without parliament's scrutiny
- Patronage gives the government large amounts of control over their own party and ensure loyalty.
- The executive can use [[royal-prerogative]] powers to go to war without parliament such as when Theresa May approved air attacks on Syria.
- The [[salisbury-convention]] states that the [[house-of-lords]] should not block legislation from the government's manifesto

This relationship is not static. A minority government or a small majority may make it easier for parliament to hold the executive accountable. If the opposition has a strong leader thought to win the next general election it may also limit the government.
