# Prerogative 

In theory, the Monarch has 'prerogative powers' (e.g. being the Commander in Chief of the Armed Forces) because they are the Head of State, but these are exercised by the PM, symbolically handed to the PM when they are summoned to meet the Monarch after winning an election. These are not constrained, and are considered 'legitimate' because they have operated this way for a long time, hence 'traditional'.

---
# Party

The leader of the largest party in the Commons becomes the new leader becomes PM, because the party they lead won the support of the people in an election. If the party leader changes between elections (e.g. Margaret Thatcher > John Major 1990; Tony Blair > Gordon Brown 2007; David Cameron > Theresa May 2016; Theresa May > Boris Johnson 2019), the new leader automatically becomes PM and is summoned to meet the Monarch for the symbolic handing over of prerogative powers. For as long as they can keep support of their party, the PM can be their chief policy-maker.

---
# Patronage

This is the power of an individual to make important appointments to public offices. Being able to give people (and dismiss them from) prestigious and sought-after jobs enables the PM to generate loyalty, because people want to gain, and then keep, these prestigious roles. The PM enjoys patronage over hundreds of roles, including government ministers, peers and headship of various state bodies, so many MPs tend to show loyalty.

# [[Parliament]]

Each new parliament, including opposition parties, recognises the authority of the PM to lead the government. There is no formal procedure for this, or for confirming new PMs when there is a change of leadership between elections. However, the passing of the Budget informally 'approves' the PM's authority, and the option of a parliamentary 'vote of no confidence' allows Parliament to dismiss the whole government if needed. The PM's authority in parliament also stems from the fact of their party having the majority of seats in the Commons - the larger the majority, the greater the authority; PMs of minority governments have very weak authority and struggle to pass legislation. MPs of the governing party usually support their party in the Commons to ensure the survival of their party in government; e.g. John Major in 1995 resigned as party leader (but not as PM) due to the disloyalty of some of his backbench MPs. He was subsequently re-elected by as party leader, thereby reasserting his authority in Parliament.
