Reason for English Devolution

- Scotland, Wales and NI all now have their own legislatures
- [[west lothian question]]: Scottish, Welsh and NI MPs can vote on legislation that effects only England (e.g. education)

- England has 85% of the population, 95% of the GDP
- England is too big to have one ‘parliament’ like Scotland
- Regional devolution / city-based devolution might be democratically desirable
    - Opportunities for democratic [[participation]]
    - [[Accountability]] enhanced
    - [[representation]]: ‘closer to the people’
    - [[legitimacy]] enhanced
    - Guards against central government tyranny
    
---

Current English Devolution

- Regional authorities: larger, strategic or regional level governments, e.g. Greater Manchester or the Greater London Authority (GLA). Large scale issues like economic policy and transport links.
- Two-tier authorities: areas with two levels of council - one larger (Norfolk County Council) and then smaller levels (South Norfolk, Broadland, etc.). Split local government functions between them.
- Single-tier authorities: areas like Cornwall have just one council, responsible for every local government function.
- Parish or Town Councils: allotments, community buildings, parks, playgrounds and public toilets.

-----------------------------------------

**Devolution in England Timeline – what’s happened so far?**

* **1965–1980s**: *Creation of the Greater London Council (GLC) in 1965*, a **single authority** for London; in the 1980s some other areas moved to a single-tier of local government known as ‘unitary authorities’.

---

**THATCHER’S CONSERVATIVES:**

* **1986**: *Abolished all unitary authorities*; Thatcher argued they were a waste of money but also that they were not sufficiently accountable. This led to increased centralisation of power.

---

**BLAIR AND NEW LABOUR, 1997–2010:**

* After 1997 Labour governments devolved powers to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, but **England was largely left out** of this process.

* **1998–2000**: The exception was Greater London: the following a referendum yes vote (turnout 34.1%), an **elected mayor of London** (using FPTP) and **London assembly** (using AMS) were created in **2000**. Controls allocation of funds for use in London, distributed by an elected assembly of 25 members, who can also veto the mayor’s budgetary proposals. London’s mayor and assemblies have limited powers – **administrative, not legislative**.

* **2004**: *Planned elected assemblies in other English regions were abandoned* after a failed referendum in the North East in 2004 (result: ‘No’ 78%. Turnout 48%). Instead, the English regions were granted limited administrative decentralisation via **‘Regional Development Agencies’**. These had an **economic focus**, aiming to improve skills, employment opportunities, competitiveness and innovation – **but had no legislative powers**.

---

**CAMERON’S COALITION 2010–2015:**

* **2010**: *Abolition of the regional development agencies*, as part of Cameron’s programme of cuts. Their powers were given to local government or, in the case of London, the London mayor and Assembly.

* **2011**: The *Localism Act* permitted local governments to hold **referendums on having an elected mayor**, and the *Police Reform and Social Responsibility Act* introduced the ability to have elected crime and police commissioners.

* **2012**: Ten local referendums held on having an elected mayor. **Only Bristol voted in favour**.

* **2013**: The *McKay Commission* proposed that legislation which affects only England should require the support of **a majority of MPs representing English constituencies**.

---

**2015 onwards:**

* **2015**: *EVEL (English votes for English laws)*. Established that if a measure that concerns England (or England and Wales) and not Scotland comes before the House of Commons, it can only pass if the majority of English (or English and Welsh) MPs vote for it. Used for the first time in January 2016, but see 2021, below…

* **2016**: *Cities and Local Devolution Act* allows for the election of metro mayors to lead combined authorities. By 2022 the number had risen to 9, many in the North, to balance the sense the North of England is not well represented in Parliament. Metro-mayors have significant powers and budgets. In 2016, the Greater Manchester elected mayor became responsible for a £6 billion health and social care budget.
  **However, this is administrative, not legislative devolution.**

* **2021**: *EVEL abolished* by Parliament, with the government claiming this would remove unnecessary complication.

* **By 2022**: The electorate had rejected the opportunity to have an elected mayor in **37 out of the 54 referendums** that were called.
  **May 2022**: *Bristol voted to abolish its elected mayoral position* on a turnout of 29%.

* **March 2023**: Two *‘trailblazer’ devolution deals agreed* (**Greater Manchester and the West Midlands**). Will devolve further powers over transport, skills, retrofitting, and housing, and will reform and simplify funding.

---

**Current status, January 2024:**

* **10 areas have mayoral devolution in England**:
  *Greater London, West Midlands, Greater Manchester, Liverpool City Region, West Yorkshire, South Yorkshire, Cambridgeshire and Peterborough, Tees Valley, West of England, and North of Tyne.*

* **Mayoral devolution to be extended to three new areas in 2024** – *York and North Yorkshire, East Midlands*, and more of the North East – **and then to an additional four places in 2025**:
  *Suffolk, Norfolk, Greater Lincolnshire, and Hull and East Yorkshire*.
  In addition, new non-mayoral devolution deals will go live in 2025 in *Lancashire and Cornwall*.
