Codified constitution - definition	- A constitution that is contained in a single document that was created at a particular time - Term also implies that a codified constitution contains a set of laws on a higher plane as opposed to all other law and that cannot be amended expect by a special procedure which protects them
Functions of a constitution (6)	1. Distribution of power - They determine how political power should be distributed within the state - This includes federal states where the power is shared with a central government - Constitutions determine the balance of power between the government and the legislature too   2. Political processes - Also determine the political processes and how they work   3. Limited government - Also define where the limits in the government's power are - UK Constitution places no such limits on Parliament, it can do as it pleases   4. Rights - Asserts the rights of citizens - Most democratic countries have a so called 'bill of rights', which protects citizens from the government and eachother   5. Nationality - Constitutions determine what makes an individual eligable for citizenship and how outsiders may become so   6. Amendments - Constitutions need to be flexible to some degree - Therefore it is necessary for a constitution to outline how it can be amended - UK Constitution can be amended by the passing of a parliamentary statute
Stages in the development of the UK constitution (4)	1. Magna Carta 1215 - Established that the rule of law should apply and the monarch should operate within the framework of the law - An important staging post of the development of constitutionalism   2. Bill of Rights 1689 - Act of Parliament which effectively stated that Parliament was sovereign and would have the final word on legislation and the governemnt's finances - Feared an overbearing monarch   3. The Acts of Union 1707 - Abolished the seperate Scottish Parliament and so established the modern United Kingdom - Devolution of power to Scotland in 1998 brought back the Scottish Parliament, although it was still not sovereign   4. The European Communities Act 1972 - Act that brought the UK into the European community, which later became the EU - UK joined a year later, left in 2016 - It was a key part of the constitution for over 50 years
Codification   Is the UK constitution codified? 3 features a codified constitution should have Conclusive info	- The UK constitution is not codified, it is not contained in a single document - It's contained over many Constitutional statutes, the ECHR is a single document    To be codified a constitution needs to have 3 features: - contained in a single document - it has a single source and was therefore created at 1 point in history - the constitutional laws must be clearly distinguished from non-constitutional law   - Virtually all modern democracies have a codified constitution - US constitution is arguably one of the first ever, UK quite unusual for not having one - Main advantage is that there is a clear document with which all citizens can identify and which can be examined if in doubt of its meaning
Entrenchment   Definition Why is it imporant  name the 2 tests for constitutional changes An example	-> Entrenchment is a constitutional principle whereby constitutional rules are safeguarded against change by a future government or legislature. It means in practice that constitutional change requires special arrangements which are more difficult to make than the passing of traditional legislation   - Important because constitutional change makes a fundamental difference to the political system of a country - A country must be sure that any proposed constitutional changes meet the following 2 tests: 1. that there is widespread support for it 2. that it is in the long-term interests of the country   - An example of this principle is the guarentee of human rights protected within the constitution -> Bill of Rights -> it may be in the short term interests of a government to set aside such human rights but it is not in the long term intersts of the people
Why is entrenchment in the UK impossible?   How can the constitution be amended?   An example __	- It is not possible to entrench constitutional principles because Parliament is sovereign - The sovereignty of parliament asserts that each individual parliament cannot be bound by its predecessors, nor can it bind its successors -> this means that every new parliament is able to amend the constitution as it wishes   - All they have to do is pass a new parliamentary statute, the same as any other statute - Moreover, as the government practically dominates parliament and tends to hold a HOC majority -> they have supreme power over the constitution   - An example of executive power was demonstrated when parliament passed the Human Rights Act in 1998 (ECHR), no special procedures were needed
Constitutional changes - fixed term parliaments   What is it How does it relate to the constitution	- After 2010 the coalition government wished to change the constitution -> proposed a law stating that each new parliament should sit for a fixed term of 5 years before the general election - This would take away the unwritten convention that the PM could name the date of the next general election - Due to the sovereignty of parliament, this constitutional feature is not entrenched -> it could be removed, shortened or the time frame extended
Referendums - entrenchment   Examples	- Despite the inability to entrench law in the UK, it is becoming common practice to hold a referendum when constitutional changes are proposed - This was done for the devolution of power to Scotland and Wales, changing of FPTP to AVS, Good Friday Agreement and EU membership - When a 'no' result occurs, the government has no mandate and will not go against the will of the people - The effect of such referendums is to entrench constitutional developments as it is inconceivable as to what would happen if the government rejeted the wish of the electorate

The nature of devolution   Definition Is devolution entrenched? What seperates us from federalist systems? What type of federalist system do we have? Distinctions between federalism and devolution   4 corresponding differences	Federalism 1. Legal sovereignty is divided between central and regional governments 2. Federalism is entrenched in a constitution 3. The powers granted to regional governments are equal and symmetrical  4. Any powers not specified in the constitution are normally granted to regional governments   Devolution 1. Power, but not soverignty, is delegated from the central government to regional governments 2. Devolution is not entrenched and is therefore flexible 3. Powers may be delegated in unequal amounts to various regional governments 4. Any powers not specified in the devolution legislation are reserved for the central government
3 types of devolution	1. Legislative powers - This means that the devolved assemblies or parliaments can make laws that will be enforced within their territories   2. Administrative powers - These are powers that have been devolved to regional governments - It refers to their power to implement and administer the laws and to organise state services   3. Financial powers - Devolved governments have funds made avaiable to them by the central government so they can provide services - However, financial devolution takes this further it allows devolved governments to raise their own funds from taxation or other means, so that they become financially independent

The arguments for retaining an uncodified constitution (4)	1. Flexibility - The constitution can adapt to a changing world without major upheavals - Constitution described as 'organic' -> this means it is rooted in society, not seperate from it - Thus when society and its needs and values change, it can also change fluidly alongside - Parliament can pass a new Act relatively quick and new unwritten conventions can develop to take account of social and political change   Examples of 'organic' change: - After 9/11 the threat of terrorism became more acute. Had the UK had an entrenched constitution and codified constitution it would have been much more difficult to pass a wide range of anti-terrorist measures. Instead, Parliament was able to do as it wished and protect the UK - When the 2010 election failed to produce an outright majority, there was some confusion about what should happen in absence of codified rules. However the system was flexible enough to adapt and a new set of principles was quickly drawn up   2. Executive power - Because constitutional safeguards in the UK are weak or absent, government can be more powerful - This can be viewed as a positive or negative - Supporters of an uncodified constitution argue that it is better to have a government that can deal with problems and crises without too much inhibitation - USA is a good case study eg zika virus c.s - In the UK the relationship between the government and Parliament is flexible, in countries with a codified constitutions it tends to be fixed and can inhibit effective governance   3. Conservative pragmatism - The typical conservative attitude to the uncodified constitution is that it has served the country effectively for centuries  - Change has occured naturally when it has been necessary rather than when reformers have campaigned for it   4. The dangers of politicising the judiciary - A codified constitution would involve the courts, the Supreme Court in particular, in disputes over its precise meaning and application - As seen in the USA, many of these issues would be intensely political - Bringing judges into political conflict puts the independence of the judiciary at risk - Of course such disputes can still occur under an uncodified constitution but they are far more likely if codified

What are the 5 principles	- Governing in the UK is a collective exercise on the whole - While the PM has thier own prerogative powers, eg over foreign and security policy, decisions are taken collectively by the government - This means that all ministers are collectively responsible for the decisions made by the government, whether they are in the cabinet or not - There is a convention that all ministers will defend and publicly support all official policy, this is known as the doctrine of collective ministerial responsibility   The 5 main principles: 1. Ministers are collectively responsible for all government policy 2. All ministers must publicly support all government policy, even if they personally disagree with them 3. If a minister wishes to dissent publicly from a government policy, he or she is expected to resign first 4. If a minister dissents without, they can expect to be dismissed by the PM 5. As cabinet meetings are secret, any dissent within government is concealed

The nature of the senior judiciary   Which are the top 3 courts and what do they do?	- The political role of the judiciary does not become significant until we look at the higher levels, this mainly relates to the 3 top levels:   1. The Court of Appeal, Civil and Criminal Divisions - These courts hear appeals from lower courts, either questioning the outcome of cases or in order to clarify a difficult point of law   2. The High Court - This is a collection of courts dealing with civil, not criminal, law such as negligence cases and reviews of government decisions   3. The general role of the senior judiciary 1 (5)	1. Dispensing justice - Though not of direct importance, the lower courts do have a vital role in ensuring justice is delivered and that those who have broken the law see due punishment   2. 'Making' law - It may seem obvious that Parliament makes law in the UK - However not all law is clear, nor is it clear how they are to be applied to particular cases - A great deal of law is not made by parliament, this is common law which is unwritten law - The judges must declare the meaning of such law if they believe it exists    3. Interpretation of law - All lawyers and judges have to interpret the meaning of law, but it is the 3 top levels of the judiciary who focus on it - The precise meaning of law is not always clear, however well drafters have done their job, there will always be circumstances where those in court come into conflict over what the law is supposed to mean - Interpretation is like the last stage in the legislative process - Here, judicial precedents become important, once an interpretation is made it must be followed in future cases unless overruled by a higher court   4. Establishing case law - As with interpretations of statute law it is not always clear as to how the law should be applied to a specific case under specific circumstances - Eg there are laws relating to racial prejudice and discrimination where an interpretation needs to be made - Once that decision is made it is expected that other judges will follow suit in similair cases   5. Declaring common law - Not all law is made by parliament - Some is known as common law -> rules of behaviour that have developed solely on tradition, in other words, they are common ways of dealing with disputes of various kinds - Much common law is fairly well established and so easily applicable - 3rd example of 'judge made law'
The general role of the judiciary - Conducting judicial review   What is it What are typical examples When did the court increase in power	- Citizens may feel they have been mistreated by a public body - When this happens there is an opportunity to seek a judicial review by the courts - The review will examine whether the citizens claims are justified, the purpose of the review is to establish the wrongdoing and may involve either compensation or simply a reversal of a decision   - Typical examples of judicial review are cases where a minister or civil servant has not dealt equally with different citizens, or where there has been a clear injustice   - Judicial review is vital because it helps achieve 2 democratic objectives: -> ensure that government does not overstep its powers -> to assert the rights of citizens   - The courts were given a huge boost in this area when the Human Rights Act 1998 was introduced in 2000
The Constitutional Reform Act 2005   What did it aim to do What was the key component Why was it important	- This Act was designed to improve and guarentee the independence of the UK judiciary - Before the Act it had always been claimed that the senior judges in the UK were independent of political circumstances but there was mixed evidence as to if that was the case - The central feature of the act was the establishment of the Supreme Court - Before the Supreme Court was established it had been the HOL that was the highest court which was also apart of the legislature - This was clearly a conflict of interests and almost unheard of in the modern world

The establishment of the Supreme Court   Main provisions of the Constitutional Reform Act (7)	1. The Lord Chancellor was no longer head of the judiciary, this was now Lord Chief Justice who was a non-political figure   2. The position of Lord Chancellor still exists and the holder combines the position with that of justice secretary in the cabinet, however they are no longer a member of the judiciary   3. The Lord Chancellor was no longer to be Speaker of the HOL ans ceased to sit in the HOL   4. The Supreme Court was established, it contains 12 senior judges known as justices of the Supreme Court   5. When there is a vacancy in the court, a Selection Committee is established consisting of senior law officers from the whole of the UK, the commisssion recommends a candidate to the Lord Chancellor   6. In theory the Lord Chancellor can question the nomination but does not have an absolute veto   7. The Act reaffirmed the principle that a Supreme Court judge can only be removed by a vote in both houses of Parliament only for misconduct and not as a result of their decisions, salary is also guarenteed   - Thus the indpenedence of the judiciary and the Supreme Court were finally codified in law - Furthermore the Lord Chancellor was charged with the task of guarenteeing and maintaining the independence of the Supreme Court from political and public pressure

The government has an electoral mandate to carry out its manifesto commitments   - So when Parliament exercises its right of sovereignty, it is threatening the democratic legitimacy of government - This issue is normally resolved by the fact that the government almost always also enjoys a majority of the members in the HOC -> this means that Parliament does not need to exercise its sovereignty given that the government is operating within its electoral mandate - Therefore the sovereignty of Parliament effectively becomes the sovereignty of the government
3 main limitations on the HOL   Specific acts	1. Parliament Act 1911 - This prevented the HOL having any control over the governments finanical arrangements - The Act also stated that if a law is passed in 2 consecutive years the HOL cannot block it   2. Parliament Act 1949 - Stated that the delaying power of the Lords, as first specified in 1911, should be reduced to only 1 year   3. The Salisbury Convention - Developed in the 1940s and states that the Lords must not block any legislation that was contained in the government's last electoral manifesto
Parliament's checks on the executive (4)	1. Veto- If the government lacks an electoral mandate for a policy, the Commons may exercise a veto if the government fails to gather a majority to support them   2. Amendments  - Parliament, both houses, may amend legislation to change its character and to protect certain minorities   3. Calling ministers to account - Parliament calls government and its ministers to account, this means they are constantly aware that any wrong-doings will be exposed   4. Removal of a government - In very extreme circumstances, Parliament could dismiss a government through a vote of no confidence given they have a majority, forcing a GE
The current state of executive-Parliament relations (5)	1. No dominant majorities- Since 2010 there has not been a dominant government majority, this is partly due to the emergence of a multi-party system and the split in the conservative party   2. Military control  - In recent years Parliament has insisted in taking control of UK military intervention, this is largely a legacy of the failed policy in Iraq after 2003 -> since Parliament has demanded it approves military intervention    3. HOL - The HOL has become increasingly important and obstructive since 1999, measure which are not subject to the Salisbury convention are vunerable to problems in their passage through the Lords   4. DSCs - The Departmental Select Committees and the Public Accounts Committee in the HOC, led by powerful chairpersons have become increasinly aggressive and intrusive   5. Backbench business - The Backbench Business Committee now controls part of the government parliamentary agenda - The backbench members now control the membership of select committees, greatly improving their willingness to challenge government
The changing balance of power and influence between Parliament and the executive    The growing influence of Parliament (4)   Factors that retain executive power (5)	The growing influence of Parliament - Parliament now has partial control over military policy - Select committees are increasingly important - There has not been a decisive government majority in the commons since 2005 - The HOL has become increasingly obstructive   Factors that retain executive power - Governments still normally enjoy a commons majority - Government still relies on a largel 'payroll vote' where all ministers, numbering over a 100, vote in their favour due to collective responsibility - Government still controls the legislative programme and the Public Bill Committees which propose legislation - PM still has patronage which creates loyalty - Government still at a huge advantage in resources (advice and research) over Parliament
Factors emerging due to minority governance (3)	- Most proposals need to be negotiated individually with members of Parliament from all parties to secure a majority of support - The government is constantly facing the possibility of defeat - The government must survive both votes of no confidence and the passing of their annual budget
What is the EU? (4)	- A customs union - A single market - An economic union - A political union
The institutions of the EU (5)	1. The European Commission - This is the civil service of the EU, staffed by non-elected workers - Their main role is to develop and propose policy which will further the aims of the EU   2. The Council of Ministers - This is a number of councils, each one dealing with a part of the EU's business - Eg finance, agriculture, foreign policy etc - Ministers from the elected governments of member countries attend meetings - Their role is to negotiate final legislation and to ratify important decisions   3. The Council of the European Union - The heads of government of member states meet, normally twice a year to form this council - They ratify important decisions  - Along with the Council of Ministers, the 2 bodies are the sovereign body in the EU   4. The European Parliament - MEPs are elected from member states - Most represent parties, the Parliament has a veto on appointments to the European Commission and can amend or even block legislation in some circumstances  - It calls the commissio to account   5. The European Court of Justice - Staffed by judges from member states this is the highest court of appeal - It deals with disputes between member states and interprets EU law
The impacts of the UK's exit from the EU (2)	1. The constitutional impact - Upon leaving the EU Parliament regains all its sovereignty - EU laws are no longer part of UK law and the UK is no longer subject to EU treaties - ECourtHR has no jurisdiction in the UK   2. The political impact - The top level of the Conservative Party who had supported remain lost considerable power, notably David Cameron and Geogre Osborne - The Conservative Party became divided very quickly - The Labour Party is similarly divided - Because Scotland voted overwhelmingly to remain inside the EU, there are renewed demands for full independence - Those parties advocating for remain and a second referendum, such as the Greens and the LDs, have seen a considerable increase in their support at the May local elections
What are the principles within sovereignty (3)	- Sovereignty implies a power which cannot be overruled - It is the source of all other political power, a sovereign body can delegate power but always has the right to withdraw such delegation - Sovereignty can only be removed or transferred, at least in democracy, but a speacial procedure
Different forms of sovereignty	- The sovereignty of Parliament is a clear example of legal sovereignty, this is a constitutional reality which will be enforced with law   - It may not reflect political sovereignty however, this refers to where real power lies as opposed to theoretical power
Is sovereignty important? (3)	- Some argue that the location of legal sovereignty is largely pointless and that the only importance in terms of power is where it really lies   Examples which illustrate this philosophy: - The key decisions in regard to the constitution are now being decided through referendums, in this sense the people are sovereign - The devolution settlement is in reality a federal system, this is because it would be unthinkable as to what would happen if Parliament where to abruptly withdraw its sovereignty - It can be argued that through winning an election and a mandate for their manifesto, the government has been granted sovereignty
Different institutions which are effectively sharing the sovereignty (5)	- The people have sovereignty over key constitutional changes through referendums - The courts are sovereign when human rights and civil liberties are to be defined - The devolved administrations are sovereign when decisions and policies concern only Scotland, Wales and N Ireland - The PM is sovereign when determining who will form the government - The monarch would be sovereign if there was a major constitutional crisis which could not be resolved by elected politicians
How sovereign is the UK Parliament? (4/3)	Sovereignty retained: - There has been no challenge to the legal sovereignty of Parliament - The Miller case confirmed that Parliament remains sovereign over the government - UK's departure from the EU means full sovereignty will be returning - Devolution can be reversed   Sovereignty threatened: - The executive continues to claim political sovereignty given it has a mandate - Devolution has been described as 'quasi-federalism' in that it is unlikely that the powers will be returned - Though Parliament must confirm a referendum result it is very unlikely that they would go against a decision
Functions of the HOL (4)   2. 2 relevant examples	1. Scrutiny and revising legislation - The Lords doesn't really legitimise legislation, but it does need to pass through - They participate more in scrunity, giving their opinion on legislation, potentially amending proposals in order to improve them - The main way the Lords scrutinises is during the Committee stage of a bill, at this stage, members can debate the bill and propose amendments   2. Delaying - Lords can delay legislation for up to 1 year Examples: -> The War Powers Act 1991: allowing the UK government to prosecute war criminals even if the offences were committed outside of the UK -> The Hunting Act 2004: banning fox hunting with hounds - Commons prevailed on both   3. Scrutinising secondary legislation - Most legislation emerging from government is actually secondary legislation -> These are laws, regulations and orders which are mostly made by ministers. They require parliamentary approval but do not have to pass through the full procedure, most passes through unnoticed - The House of Lords Secondary Legislation Scrutiny Committee considers all secondary legislation and decides which proposals might cause concern   4. National debates - Like the HOC, the Lords occasionally holds debates on national issues - The Lords tends to specialise in issues that have a moral or ethical dimension - Can be useful in advising the Government on policy, especially considering the level of expertise in the chamber
