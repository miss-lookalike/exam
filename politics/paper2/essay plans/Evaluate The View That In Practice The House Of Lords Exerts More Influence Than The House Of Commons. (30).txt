Paragraph One ➡ Power Over Legislation
Against: Arguments That The House Of Lords Doesnʼt Exert More Influence
Than The House Of Commons In Practice

The power of the House of Lords in relation to legislation is significantly limited by both the
Salisbury Convention and the Parliaments Acts of 1911 and 1949.
If the House of Lords refuses to change its position and pass a bill supported by the House of
Commons, the House of Commons can as a last resort invoke the Parliament Act 1949 after a
year to force a bill through.
This has been used 7 times in total, including for equalising the age of consent to 16 for
homosexuals and heterosexuals, with the Sexual Offences Act 2000
The House of Lords is limited in the legislation it can vote down and play a role in, whilst the
House of Commons isnʼt.
The Salisbury Convention means that the House of Lords shouldnʼt block any bill that fulfils
a pledge in the governmentʼs election manifesto by voting against it in the second or third
reading.
The Lords can offer technical amendments but cannot introduce ‘wrecking
amendmentsʼ which change the Governmentʼs manifesto intention as proposed in the
bill.
This can be seen in the House Of Lords Hereditary Peers) Bill, which if passed would
remove the 92 hereditary peers in the upper chamber. As this was included in Labourʼs
2024 manifesto, the Lords will be unable to block the bill despite it making a significant
change to the upper chamber.
The Parliament Acts of 1911 and 1949 prevent the House of Lords from delaying financial
bills.
Even when the House of Lords votes against legislation/attempts to significantly amend it, it
often backs down when the House of Commons disagrees again/votes against these
amendments, recognising its lack of democratic legitimacy.
For example, in April 2024 after six rounds of parliamentary ping pong, the House of Lords
backed down on its amendments to the Safety of Rwanda Bill despite strong moral
opposition to the bill from much of the chamber, recognising its lack of legitimacy.
This is in comparison to the House of Commons, which plays a part in all legislation.

**************************************************************************************************

For: Arguments That The House Of Lords Does Exert More Influence Than The
House Of Commons In Practice
Though the House of Lords often backs down if its amendments are rejected by the House of
Commons, recognising its lack of democratic legitimacy, it sometimes doesnʼt and takes a stand
against a bill when it feels it is justified.
This is often to protect human rights or in relation to a bill that has garnered a significant
public backlash/has limited public support.
This delaying power can be very significant in influencing legislation, especially in emergencies
when governments want to pass laws quickly.
For example, in 2001 ten defeats in the House Lords led the government to remove
incitement to religious hatred as an offence from its anti-terrorism legislation. The House of
Lords argued it wouldʼve threatened rights.
When the government has a large majority in the House of Commons, it is very unlikely to be
defeated. By contrast, especially since New Labourʼs reforms, the House of Lords has become
more independent of the executive (with no majority in the chamber) and more assertive in
scrutinising the work of the government.
This leads to governments being defeated a great deal more in the House of Lords than
they are in the House of Commons, especially when the government has a massive
majority, in which case the Lords becomes the effective opposition.
Since the 2019 election, Boris Johnson was defeated just 4 times in the House of Commons
but 243 times in the House of Lords, which was an unprecedentedly large number given the
short amount of time he was in power.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the House of Commons clearly has more power over legislation in law. When the
government has a large majority, however, the House of Lords often plays a more significant
role than the House of Commons due to its lack of government majority and greater
independence,
In practice, therefore, the House of Lords does exert more power than the House of Commons
over legislation.

Paragraph Two ➡ Scrutiny Of The Government
For: Arguments That The House Of Lords Does Exert More Influence Than The
House Of Commons In Practice

Especially in recent years, when the government has increasingly rushed legislation through the
House of Commons, the House of Lords has played a crucial role in scrutinising and revising
legislation, as the government has less control over the Parliamentary timetable in the Lords.
The House of Lords frequently offers technical amendments to improve and fix bills and is
sometimes called an ‘amending chamberʼ, as it looks in detail at legislation.
The Product Regulation And Meteorology Bill is a good example of how House of Lords
Amendments to improve bills are often accepted by the House of Commons.
When first introduced by the government, the bill contained huge delegated powers
giving UK ministers the power to unilaterally align the UK with EU regulations, as
part of Starmerʼs reset with the EU.
However, after criticism from the House of Lords Constitution Committee for how
this would have given too much control to the government and left MPs with little
ability to scrutinise the governmentʼs decisions, the government made changes to
the bill. These included introducing consultation requirements before ministers make
any regulations.
This shows how the House of Lords exerts more influence that the Commons in
relation to scrutiny as it has the time and expertise to scrutinise the complicated
detail of legislation, whilst the House of Commons often doesnʼt.
An example of the government rushing legislation through the House of Commons and it
receiving little effective scrutiny as a result is the Passenger Railway Services Public
Ownership) Act, which received just 1 day of scrutiny in the Committee stage and skipped
the Report stage as no amendments were accepted to the bill.
By contrast, it received 3 full days of scrutiny in the Committee Stage in the House of
Lords.
Since New Labourʼs reforms, the House of Lords has also become a lot more professional and
has significant expertise, which allows highly effective scrutiny of government legislation.
Many life peers are ex professional politicians who are able to provide leadership in the
House of Lords and have experience in parliamentary scrutiny.
Lord Andrew Adonis, for example, is a Labour peer who served as Secretary of State for
transport under Gordon Brown.
Many other life peers are specialists from different fields, who can provide expertise on
specific areas.
Lord Mair, for example, is a geotechnical engineer and Professor of Civil Engineering at
the University of Cambridge and he is able to bring this expertise to his role as chair of
the House of Lords Science and Technology Committee.
The House of Lords also has more time and energy to devote to scrutinising government
policy and legislation line by line.
The House of Commons spends little time scrutinising government legislation, whilst the fact
that MPs are whipped and public bill committees reflect the balance of the house, and therefore
have a government majority, limit the effectiveness of House of Commons scrutiny.
MPs spent just 24% of their time in the chamber between 2006 and 2021, compared to
44% for Peers.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That The House Of Lords Doesnʼt Exert More Influence
Than The House Of Commons In Practice
The House of Commons can also be seen as providing effective non-legislative scrutiny in a
number of ways, including select committees, ministersʼ questions and debates.
PMQs, for example, enables the leader of the opposition, the leader of the third largest
party and backbenchers to direct question the minister every week.
On 8 May 2024, Starmer used his first few questions in PMQs to effectively scrutinise
the reality of the impact of the Safety of Rwanda Bill and Rwanda plan, which he called a
‘gimmickʼ rather than effective policy.
He pointed out that it wasnʼt really effective as a deterrent, with 2400 small boats
crossings in the 16 days since it was passed, therefore publicly damaging Sunakʼs
government which had been trying to promote the effectiveness of the policy.
Scrutiny from Select Committees is sometimes able to have a direct impact on government
policy.
The government has to publicly respond to their findings/reports in 8 weeks and they
can sometimes have a direct influence on government policy, ensuring that government
departments perform effectively.
In May 2023, the government accepted/partly accepted all of the Foreign Affairs
Select Committeeʼs recommendations in their report titled Refreshing our approach?
Updating the Integrated Review.
The government agreed with the committeeʼs assertion that an updated strategy
was needed and took on a number of recommendations, including appointing a
Director of Resilience in the Cabinet Office to focus on making the UK less
dependent on other international actors.
The democratic legitimacy of the House of Commonsʼ legislative scrutiny leads to it being more
effective, whilst it also has the exclusive power to vote down financial legislation and legislation
included in the governmentʼs manifesto.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the House of Lords exerts more power than the House of Commons in practice in terms
of legislative scrutiny, particularly due to its increased independence and expertise since New
Labourʼs removal of hereditary peers.
The House of Commons, however, exerts more power in terms of non legislative scrutiny,
particularly through Ministerʼs Questions and Select Committees.

Paragraph Three ➡ Debates and Other Powers
Against: Arguments That The House Of Lords Doesnʼt Exert More Influence Than The House Of Commons In Practice

One key power held exclusively by the House of Commons is that they can hold a vote of no
confidence and therefore bring down a government if the government loses, as they are forced
to resign.
The last time this happened was in 1979, when nationalist parties that were upholding a
minority Labour government in a confidence and supply agreement withdrew their support,
which brought down the Callaghan government and triggered the 1979 general election.
The House of Commons also has the exclusive power to uphold a minority government through
a confidence and supply agreement.
This is when one of the Conservatives/Labour doesnʼt have a majority and doesnʼt get a
majority through a formal coalition (such as in 2010, but instead through a limited
agreement with another party/parties that agree to keep them in government.
The supporting smaller party promises to support the government in a vote of no
confidence and to vote through the governmentʼs budget (supply). In return they receive
concessions.
In 2017, for example, the Conservative Minority Government used a confidence and supply
agreement with the DUP Democratic Unionist Party), in which the DUP agreed to support
the Conservatives in votes of confidence and over budgets, in return for £1 billion of money
pledged to Northern Ireland.
The House of Commons can also exert influence through debates, which put issues on the
political agenda and put pressure on the government to address them. There are a number of
different types of debates, including emergency debates and debates suggested by the
Backbench Business Committee, which chooses the topic of debate for 35 days in each
parliamentary session.
On 5 December 2024, Conservative MP Sir Iain Duncan Smith led a Backbench Business
Committee debate on the issue of detained British nationals abroad. His motion expressed
concern about the number of arbitrarily detained British nationals overseas, including about
the risk of human rights abuses and the perceived lack of active support for those detained.
Smith called on the Foreign Office to provide regular reports detailing when each case
was last raised and different cases were discussed.
The debate attracted media attention and shows how MPs in the House of Commons can
use debates to raise the importance of issues that are low on the political agenda and try to
get them addressed by the government.

**************************************************************************************************

For: Arguments That The House Of Lords Does Exert More Influence Than The
House Of Commons In Practice
The House of Lords can also play a key role in raising the importance of issues through
debates. These debates are often insightful and considered due to the significant expertise
within the chamber.
They can have influence by considering issues that are not focused on my the House of
Commons due to the greater time that the House of Lords has. This can help to put them on the
political agenda.
For example, on January 18 2024, the House of Lords held a number of key debates on
important issues including climate change, refugee integration, religious education in
schools, the adoption of new vaccines, and creating parity between health and social care
to address dementia.
This format of short debates allows the Lords to cover a range of important topics,
leveraging their expertise to bring attention to and influence public policy on these issues.
The House of Lords also has the legal power to force a general election if the government tries
to stay on beyond 5 years. This is given to it in the Parliament Act 1911.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the House of Commons has more power than the House of Lords in practice when it
comes the these other non-legislative powers, as it has on a number of occasions brought
down a government through a vote of no confidence/upheld a government through a
Confidence and Supply agreement.

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, the House of Commons certainly has more legal powers than the House of Lords
and exerts more influence in practice when there is a weak government with a weak/small
majority.
When the government has a large majority in the House of Commons, however, which is usually
the case, the House of Lords arguably exerts more influence in practice, due to its greater
independence and professionalism since New Labourʼs reforms, which enable it to defeat the
government more frequently and provide more effective legislative scrutiny.
