Evaluate the view that the UK has an ‘elective dictatorship’.
(30)

Paragraph One - House of Lords
+ The House of Lords has limited powers to hold the government to account, due to the
Parliaments Acts and Salisbury Convention, allowing the government to drive policy
through Parliament.
- The House of Lords provides an important check on government power, by scrutinising
legislation and using its 1 year delaying power effectively

Paragraph Two - House of Commons
+ The House of Commons doesn’t stand up to the government at all due to the government’s
large majority in the HoC. It can pass what it wants and faces limited dissent due to strong
whipping.
- The House of Commons is highly effective in standing up to the government when it is
weak/has a small majority, through rebellions. The Wright Reforms have made Parliament
& Select Committees more independent.

Paragraph Three - Supreme Court
+ Parliament is sovereign. The Supreme Court can’t strike down laws passed by Parliament,
including in relation to the HRA. The executive can ignore declarations of incompatibility.
- Declaration of incompatibility put pressure on the executive and holds them to account. The
Supreme Court can declare actions of the government ‘ultra vires’ and reverse them.

Evaluate The View That The Roles and Powers Of The
Executive Allow It To Dominate Parliament. (30)

Paragraph One - Legislative Powers
+ The executive controls the legislative timetable and proposes the majority of
bills. This allows it to drive legislation through Parliament and ensure its key
policies are fulfilled. E.g. The Steel Industry (Special Measures) Act
+ Due to its large majority, it is very rare for the government to lose a vote in
the House of Commons. Blair was defeated just four times in his 10 years in
office, all of which were in his third term.
+ The government has major control over secondary legislation, which
Parliament performs little scrutiny over. Around ⅔ of statutory instruments
become law without being debated by MPs.
+ In July 2024, the government used a SI to allow the early release of
prisoners (100s released after serving 40% of their term. It was
debated for just 90 minutes in the House of Commons and wasn’t put
to a vote.
- When the government has a small/no majority, it is certainly not able to
dominate Parliament. Theresa May was defeated thirty-three times when she
had a minority government.
- There are 20 opposition days and 13 days for PMBs, which can be highly
significant changes.

Paragraph Two - Prerogative Powers
+ The power of patronage allows the PM to control their party in Parliament
through carrot and stick.
+ The executive has the power to declare war and authorise the use of the
armed forces. Sunak authorised air strikes against the Houthis in January
2024 without parliamentary approval.
+ The executive has the power to sign treaties and negotiate with other
countries without asking giving Parliament a vote (e.g. Chagos Islands deal
and UK-India trade agreement).
- Parliament is still able to debate, scrutinise and criticise treaties and the
government’s decisions to use the armed forces. The executive is still
therefore influenced by Parliament and public opinion and can’t completely
ignore them.
- CMR has broken down significantly under weak government, stopping them
from dominate Parliament and controlling their party’s MPs.
- There is a convention that House of Commons consent is required before
enacting major military action. Blair 2003 - Iraq. Cameron backed down on
Syria airstrikes after Commons voted down in 2013.

Paragraph Three - Making Policy and Budget Decisions
+ The executive makes key decisions on economic policy and proposes a budget.
This allows it major control over economic policy. E.g. in Oct 2024 Reeves
announces tax rises (NIs for employers), increase in minimum wage to £12.21
and revenue cuts to 7 departments.
+ In February, Starmer cut international aid budget from 0.5% of GNI to 0.3%.
+ The executive also sets the policy direction of the county and makes key policy
decisions. E.g. In October 2023, Sunak’s government cancelled the northern leg
of HS2, even though it had been introduced through parliamentary legislation
- Parliament can have a major influence on the government’s budget and policy
decisions by representing public opinion and criticising the government in
Parliament.
- E.g. in May 2025, the government u-turned on winter fuel payments to
pensioners after over 6 months of criticism from a lot of backbenchers and the
opposition.
- If Parliament no longer supports the government’s agenda, they can vote them
out in a vote of no confidence, as happened to Jim Callaghan.

Evaluate the view that Prime Minister’s Questions should be
scrapped. (30)

Paragraph One - PMQs is highly theatrical BUT it is highly popular
+ PMQs is very boisterous and is more parliamentary theatre than effective scrutiny -
Cameron labelled it ‘punch and judy’ politics.
+ Should be scrapped and replaced with greater use of other scrutiny, such as the
Liaison Committee, which provides far more in depth and meaningful questioning of
the Prime Minister.
+ Boris Johnson, for instance, was far less able to evade scrutiny in the liaison
committee and the embarrassment he faced there was even arguably
something which contributed to his downfall
- PMQs is televised every week and is the most watched aspect of politics among
the public, far more than other forms of scrutiny, which can be seen as highly
important to encouraging public engagement in politics and exposing government
failures to the public.
- On 8 May 2024, Starmer used his first few questions in PMQs to effectively
scrutinise the reality of the impact of the Safety of Rwanda Bill and Rwanda
plan, which he called a ‘gimmick’ rather than effective policy.
- This shows how PMQs can be effective in scrutinising recent
government policy in front of the electorate and how the six questions
that the leader of the opposition gets can be very powerful.
- Contributes to a more informed electorate and consequently helps to
remove the danger of a democratic deficit.

Paragraph Two - PMQs is highly partisan BUT it provides genuine political
debate
+ It can be argued that PMQs in particular provides little effective scrutiny and is
more focused on partisan political point scoring.
+ Many government backbenchers ask questions drafted by the whips, which
are intended to flatter rather than scrutinise the government.
+ This shows how PMQs often delivers poor scrutiny from government
backbenchers, with questions often drafted by the whips, and is more focused on
political point scoring than actual scrutiny.
+ On 13/11/2024, Labour MP Patrick Hurley, asked the Prime Minister if he
agreed that his budget would "provide much needed investment" and
properly support public services "unlike the previous government.”
+ Rather than actually scrutinising the government, he praised them and
criticised the opposition.
- Even if PMQs may sometimes not be effective scrutiny as it is highly partisan, it
can be seen as important in providing genuine political debate between parties and
MPs, therefore presenting voters with a clear choice, which is important to
democracy, especially in the run up to elections.
- The Leader of the Opposition (6 questions) and the leader of the 3rd largest
party (2 questions) have the opportunity to get the government to respond
directly to their questions in PMQs, therefore giving them the ability to
expose government failure and suggest why they would be more effective.

Paragraph Three - PMQs Forces PM to be competent BUT its effectiveness
depends on Opposition leader
+ Misleading the House of Commons can lead to huge pressure to resign, which forces the
Prime Minister to be competent and on top of their policy
+ This decreases the likelihood that incompetent party leaders who favour political
demagoguery over rational argument will lead a major party into an election, as they
would embarrass and harm the party’s image every Wednesday at PMQs.
+ Some Opposition leaders are highly effective in scrutinising the work of government.
Starmer was reasonably good, especially given his experience as a prosecutor.
- Effective scrutiny is very dependent on the abilities of backbenchers and the Leader of the
Opposition.
- Since becoming the leader of the Official Opposition and the Conservative Party in
2024, Kemi Badenoch has been criticised for underperforming at PMQs.
- For example, in February 2025, she was criticised for failing to challenge the
PM on the Chagos Islands deal and whether the growing cost of the plan
increasing to £18 billion is compatible with his pledge not to return to
austerity.
- This meant questions on issues that are of high political importance went
unanswered, undermining the purpose of PMQs to forensically pin down the
PM and provide scrutiny on issues the Government would least like to talk
about.

Evaluate the view that the legislative process requires reform.
(30)

Paragraph One - The Government’s Dominance Of The Legislative Process
+ The government dominates the legislative process and can force legislation through
Parliament even when it is unpopular/has limited support from the population, as
governments can win a majority under FPTP without a majority of the popular vote
+ This includes forcing through legislation that wasn’t in the government’s manifesto
+ PMBs have very little chance of succeeding without government support
- Government dominance is key in preventing gridlock and allowing manifesto commitments
to be fulfilled.
- It also allows legislation to be pushed through Parliament quickly in emergencies
(E.g. Coronavirus Act or The Steel Industry (Special Measures) Act)
- A number of PMBs have been successful and have introduced significant changes.

Paragraph Two - Scrutiny Of Legislation in the House of Commons
+ Public Bill Committees have a majority of MPs from the government’s party and very rarely
make amendments not supported by the government.
+ Over 99% of ministerial amendments succeed at this stage, whilst less than 1% of
non government amendments do.
+ The government dominates secondary legislation which has little scrutiny from Parliament.
+ Legislation is increasingly rushed through Parliament with limited scrutiny from the House
of Commons.
- Legislation is effectively scrutinised through public bill committees in the House of
Commons
- On important pieces of legislation, this is done through detailed, considered debate in a
Committee of the Whole House

Paragraph Three - The Role Of The House of Lords
- The House of Lords plays a highly important role in scrutinising legislation, providing
important line-by-line scrutiny including in Select Committees
- It often proposes technical amendments to improve bills.
- This is aided by the expertise in the House of Lords chamber, due to the fact that the
majority of members are life peers.
+ The House of Lords provides little real scrutiny as it lacks democratic legitimacy or the
power to veto legislation
+ This means that it often backs down when faced by a government determined to
pass a piece of legislation.
+ Therefore the government is an ‘elective dictatorship’ that faces few checks on its power
over the legislative process.
