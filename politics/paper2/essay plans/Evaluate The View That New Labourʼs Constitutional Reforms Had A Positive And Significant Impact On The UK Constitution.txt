Evaluate The View That New Labourʼs Constitutional Reforms Had A Positive And Significant Impact On The UK Constitution. 



Introduction
Key Definitions
‘New Labourʼs Constitutional Reformsʼ refers to a number of reforms introduced by Tony Blairʼs
governments, including the Supreme Court, Human Rights Act 1998, House of Lords Reform,
Electoral Reform and Devolution

Paragraph One ➡ House Of Lords
For: Arguments That New Labourʼs Constitutional Reforms Had A Positive And
Significant Impact On The UKʼs Constitution
It can be argued that New Labourʼs reforms to the House of Lords had a major positive impact
on the UK Constitution. The House of Lords Act 1999 removed all but 92 hereditary peers and
replaced them with life peers.
This can be seen as significant as it undermined the hereditary basis of the House of Lords and
removed its in-built Conservative majority, with crossbenchers ensuring no single party can get
a majority. As a consequence, the House of Lords is now more independent of the executive
and better able to limit the power of the government.
Tony Blair was defeated just 4 times in the House of Commons, all of which were in his last
term, but was defeated 353 times in the House of Lords.
As the reforms meant the House of Lords had greater legitimacy, it also emboldened them
to stand up to the government on key issues, such as defending Human Rights. For
example, in 2001 ten defeats in the House Lords led the government to remove incitement
to religious hatred as an offence from its anti-terrorism legislation.
New Labourʼs reforms to the House of Lords can also be seen as having a positive and
significant impact on the UKʼs constitution as they made the chamber more professional and
therefore effective at scrutiny of the government as a consequence.
Many life peers are ex professional politicians who are able to provide leadership in the
House of Lords and have experience in parliamentary scrutiny.
Lord Andrew Adonis, for example, is a Labour peer who served as Secretary of State for
transport under Gordon Brown.
Many other life peers are specialists from different fields, who can provide expertise on
specific areas.
James Timpson, for example, pioneered a progressive employment model as CEO of
the Timpson Group by hiring ex-offenders to help them reintegrate into society.
His first-hand experience in reducing reoffending through employment and
rehabilitation provides the House of Lords with significant expertise and he has also
been able to support the government since being made Minister of State for Prisons.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That New Labourʼs Constitutional Reforms Didnʼt Have A
Positive And Significant Impact On The UKʼs Constitution
On the other hand, New Labourʼs reforms to the House of Lords can be seen as insignificant as
the House of Lords continues to be unelected and therefore undemocratic.
New Labour attempted a two stage plan to reform the House of Lords. The first would
remove all hereditary peers and the second would replace the Lords with an elected second
chamber.
Not only was stage 1 not fully carried out as 92 hereditary peers remained, stage 2
didnʼt happen at all as many Labour MPs felt the key problems of hereditary peers and
Conservative dominance had been dealt with, whilst there was a lack of agreement on
what the new chamber should look like.
New Labourʼs reforms can therefore be seen as having a limited impact on the constitution
as the House of Lords remains a key undemocratic feature of the UK constitution.
New Labourʼs reforms can also be seen as limited as they failed to change the balance of power
between the House of Commons and House of Lords. The former remains very dominant,
enabling a government with a large majority to force through legislation with little opposition, in
large part due to the House of Lordsʼ limited legislative powers.
The Parliaments Acts of 1911 and 1949 prevent the Lords from voting down any financial
bills and mean that the House of Lords can only delay legislation for up to a year, after
which the House of Commons (dominated by the government) can force through legislation.
This was used 3 times by the Blair government, including to pass the Hunting Act 2004,
which banned hunting with dogs.
The Salisbury Convention prevents the House of Lords from voting down any legislation that
fulfils a commitment in the governing partyʼs election manifesto.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, New Labourʼs reforms to the House of Lords certainly had a positive impact on the UKʼs
constitution, as they removed the in-built Conservative majority in the chamber and undermined
its hereditary element. Even though they didnʼt make the chamber democratic or more
powerful, these reforms should still be seen as significant, as they improved and modernised
the chamber.

Paragraph Two ➡ Devolution
For: Arguments That New Labourʼs Constitutional Reforms Had A Positive And Significant Impact On The UKʼs Constitution

One of New Labourʼs key constitutional reforms was to devolve significant power to Scotland,
Wales, Northern Ireland and the Greater London Authority.
Scotland was given the most powers out of all of the devolved bodies, due to its strong
nationalist movement. Including control over most public services; health, education,
justice, agriculture and most transport. It can therefore be seen as ‘service devolutionʼ.
The powers reserved for Westminster were listed (including defence, macroeconomic
policy, foreign policy, constitutional matter etc.), with the left reserved for Scotland.
In 1998, Northern Ireland was (like Scotland) given primary legislative control over areas not
reserved for Westminster.
These reforms can be seen as having a positive and significant impact on the constitution first
because they created governments closer to the people and therefore improved democracy.
This can be seen in particular in Scotland, in which the population is more left wing than in
the rest of the UK. This has led to the election of a left wing SNP government that has
introduced policies including free prescription charges, free tuition fees and a higher top
rate of income tax than in the rest of the UK.
They can also be seen as having a significant impact on the constitution as they reduced the
sovereignty of Parliament over Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That New Labourʼs Constitutional Reforms Didnʼt Have A
Positive And Significant Impact On The UKʼs Constitution
On the other hand, devolution can be seen as having an insignificant impact on the UK
constitution as it was asymmetric. Crucially, no English Parliament or substantial representation
of England was introduced, which can be seen as creating a democratic deficit and providing
no effective outlet for English Identity.
The asymmetric nature of devolution, with different powers and policies for different parts
of the UK, can be seen as undermining equal citizenship, as different citizens have different
levels of representation, are subjected to different laws and to different policies.
This can be seen as threatening the unity of the UK, as it leads to its citizens having less
in common with each other.
For example, the Scottish and Northern Irish Parliaments control policing, whilst policing
in Wales and England is controlled by the UK Parliament.
Further, whilst Scotland and Northern Ireland were given significant control over legislation,
Wales wasnʼt. As a consequence, further reforms had to be introduced by Conservative
governments following 2010 to devolve further power.
Wales started with far fewer powers than Scotland or Northern Ireland, as there was little
nationalist sentiment in Wales and the 1997 referendum on Welsh devolution was very
close; a majority of 50.5% on a 50% turnout.
At first, Wales only had the Welsh Assembly that had no legislative powers or 1st minister. It
was only granted administrative devolution.
Finally, devolution can be seen as having a negative impact on the UK Constitution as it created
the West Lothian Question and can be seen as creating tensions between differing parts of the
UK, whilst failing to stop a growing independence movement in Scotland.
The West Lothian Question asks why Scottish MPs should be able to vote on English
matters in the UK Parliament that donʼt affect their constituents, when English MPs canʼt do
the same in the Scottish Parliament.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the devolution reforms introduced by New Labour should be seen as having a
significant impact on the UK constitution, as they significant improved democracy in Scotland,
Wales and Northern Ireland and reduced the sovereignty of Parliament.
These reforms should also be seen as positive. Though devolution was asymmetric, this is
because an English Parliament wouldʼve created more problems than it wouldʼve solved and
there was little demand for it, and devolution ultimately improved the representation of citizens
in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

Paragraph Three ➡ Human Rights Act 1998
For: Arguments That New Labourʼs Constitutional Reforms Had A Positive And
Significant Impact On The UKʼs Constitution

One key constitutional reform introduced by New Labour was the Human Rights Act 1998,
which enshrined the European Convention on Human Rights into UK law, in effect from 2000,
meaning that the rights could be defended in UK courts rather than having to go to Strasbourg.
The rights protected by the Act included the right to life, right to a fair trial, right to privacy
and a family life, prohibition of torture.
It can be argued that this had a positive and significant impact on the UK constitution as since
the HRA, it is often claimed that the UK has developed a 'rights-based culture' as all new
legislation must be compliant with the act and judges can declare earlier acts of parliament
incompatible with it.
Parliament usually acts to address any issues raised by the courts and parliament also has a
Joint Committee on Human Rights to scrutinise bills and ensure they are compatible,
demonstrating the 'persuasive influence' of the HRA.
For example, in A v Secretary of State for the Home Department, part of The Anti-terrorism,
Crime and Security Act 2001 was declared incompatible with Articles 5 and 14 of the human
rights act by permitting the detention of suspected international terrorists in a way that
discriminated on the grounds of nationality or immigration status. The Prevention of
Terrorism Act 2005 was amended by parliament as a result.
The Human Rights Act can therefore be seen as significant as it limits Parliamentary
Sovereignty and furthers the Rule of Law in the UK.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That New Labourʼs Constitutional Reforms Didnʼt Have A
Positive And Significant Impact On The UKʼs Constitution
On the other hand, the significance of the Human Rights Act can be over stated. The HRA may
be used to check other laws, but it is not entrenched and could still be repealed by a simple act
of parliament, whilst also not being binding on parliament. Parliamentary sovereignty is
therefore still in tact.
The Illegal Migration Bill, for example, was introduced by Home Secretary Suella Braverman
on 7th March 2023 with a section 19 note stating that there was a likelihood the provisions
of the bill would be incompatible with the Human Rights Act and international law The
European Convention on Human Rights), but that the government wanted to proceed with it
nonetheless.
The government passed The Safety of Rwanda Asylum and Immigration) Act in April 2024.
The act states that Rwanda must be considered a safe country for asylum seekers and that
international law and the Human Rights Act must be put aside to allow for the deportation of
asylum seekers to Rwanda, therefore removing the individual right to judicial review on
these grounds.
It also gives ministers the power to ignore emergency rulings against the act by the
European Court Of Human Rights in Strasbourg.
This shows how Parliament and the government can threaten rights in the UK by
explicitly preventing the Supreme Court from using judicial review to challenge their
legislation. The Human Rights Act can therefore be seen as not having a major positive
impact in terms of rights protection in the UK constitution.
Many critics on the right also criticise the HRA for favouring undeserving individuals, including
illegal migrants and terror suspects, rather than protecting the legitimate freedoms of UK
citizens and protecting society as a whole.
For example, Abu Qatada, a radical Muslim cleric who had advocated the use of violence to
promote the Islamist cause and had spent some time in British jails, was wanted by the
security services to be deported to Jordan, where he was wanted for trial.
However his legal advisers managed to delay this for 8 years on the grounds that he
might be tried using evidence obtained through torture, a breach of the HRA.
Legal challenges in relation to the HRA have also prevented the deportation of illegal
immigrants to Rwanda.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the Human Rights Act has arguably had a positive impact on the protection of rights in
the UK, however its impact on the UK constitution should be seen as limited. This is because
Parliamentary Sovereignty is still in tact and it is only a simple act of parliament, meaning it
could be easily repealed at any time.

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, New Labourʼs constitutional reforms did have a positive and significant impact on
the UK Constitution. They improved the protection of rights in the UK, made democracy closer
to the people and reformed an outdated aspect of the political system. Collectively, they
decreased the powers of the UK government and the sovereignty of Parliament.
Though there were certainly possible further reforms that couldʼve been introduced, the
reforms that were introduced were nonetheless positive and significant.
