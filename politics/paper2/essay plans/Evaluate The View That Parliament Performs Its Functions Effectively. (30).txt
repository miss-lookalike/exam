Evaluate The View That Parliament Performs Its Functions Effectively. (30)

Introduction
Key Definitions
This essay considers 3 key functions of Parliament and looks at how effectively it performs
each of them: passing legislation, scrutinising the executive and representing the electorate.

**************************************************************************************************

Paragraph One ➡ Passing Legislation
For: Arguments That Parliament Passes Legislation Effectively
Perhaps Parliamentʼs most important function is to pass laws. It is the supreme legislative body
in the UK and has the authority to pass or amend laws on any subject.
Due to its majority and the power of the whips, the government rarely has a problem passing
bills through the House of Commons and legislation is almost always passed effectively.
In the 2022/23 parliamentary session, the government introduced 56 bills. Of these, 43
(76% received royal assent by the end of the session.
Successful laws included the Illegal Migration Act and Online Safety Act.
The House of Lords does offer amendments but rarely halts the passage of legislation,
recognising its limited democratic legitimacy.
Further, if the House of Lords does block legislation supported by the House of
Commons, then the Commons can use the Parliaments Acts to override their veto after
a year.
This has been used 7 times in total, including for equalising the age of consent to 16 for
homosexuals and heterosexuals, with the Sexual Offences Act 2000
The House of Lords especially is important in scrutinising legislation and offering technical
amendments to ensure laws are workable and effective.
The Levelling-Up and Regeneration Act, passed in October 2023, is a good example of how
House of Lords Amendments to improve bills are often accepted by the House of
Commons.
The Act aimed at reducing regional disparities and changing the planning system to
facilitate the building of new homes.
In total, the government accepted 64 House of Lords amendments to the bill.
The vast majority of these were ‘tecnical amendmentsʼ that fixed issues with the bill and
improved the wording so that it would function better, rather than seeking major
changes to the outcome of the bill itself.
For example, amendment 117 replaced the word “consultˮ with “obtain the consent
ofˮ in line 9 of the bill.
In emergencies, Parliament is also able to push through legislation in a short amount of time.
For example, on 12 April 2025, Parliament was recalled on a Saturday in order to pass an
emergency law giving Business Secretary Jonathon Reynolds the power to direct British
Steel to prevent a Scunthorpe plant from shutting down; the last blast furnace in the UK.
The bill passed Parliament in just six-and-a-half hours, receiving royal assent the same
day.
Some Private Membersʼ Bills are also passed if they get the support of the governing party.
The Protection from Sex-based Harassment in Public Act 2023 is an example of a Private
Memberʼs Bill that was successfully passed on 18 September 2023, after receiving support
from the government and opposition.
It was introduced by ballot by Greg Clark, the Conservative MP for Tunbridge Wells.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That Parliament Is Ineffective At Passing Legislation
The vast majority of Private Membersʼ Bills arenʼt passed, as if bills donʼt receive government
support, it is very difficult to pass them through Parliament.
In the 2022/23 parliamentary session, 297 Private Memberʼs Bills were introduced, of which
just 24 8% received royal assent by the end of the session.
Though the House of Lords only have a 1 year delaying power, this can be significant in
delaying important legislation/forcing the House of Commons to compromise and amend
legislation.
Especially when the government has a small/no majority, rebellions can be disruptive in
preventing important legislation being passed.
Both Theresa May and Boris Johnsonʼs governments failed to pass their Brexit Withdrawal
Agreements a number of times in 2019 when their Conservative governments relied on a
Confidence and Supply Agreement with the Democratic Unionist Party DUP for their
majority.
Legislation being rushed through Parliament can lead to a lack of scrutiny and to poorly drafted
and unworkable laws as a consequence.
For example, The 1991 Dangerous Dogs Act, passed in response to a series of tabloid
stories about dog attacks, prohibited certain breeds of dogs instead of targeting
irresponsible owners, as many critics argued it should have. A review by the RSPCA much
later showed that of the 30 deaths caused in that period by dogs, 21 involved breeds that
were not specified in the act.
It is increasingly common for legislation to be rushed through the House of Commons by
the government.
For example, the Passenger Railway Services Public Ownership) Act received just 1
day of scrutiny in the Committee stage in the House of Commons and skipped the
Report stage as no amendments were accepted to the bill, therefore receiving little
scrutiny despite being a highly significant bill.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, Parliament is largely effective at passing legislation. Due to the governmentʼs
dominance in the House of Commons and the House of Commonsʼ supremacy over the House
of Lords, it rarely has any problem passing the legislation it wishes to and fulfilling its mandate.
The fact that legislation is increasingly rushed through the House of Commons threatens the
effectiveness of passing legislation, however the House of Lords currently makes up for this
through effective legislative scrutiny.

Paragraph Two ➡ Scrutinising The Government
For: Arguments That Parliament Is Effective At Scrutinising The Government

The government comes from Parliament and is held to account by Parliament, which plays a
key role in scrutinising the government in a number of ways.
One key way in which Parliament scrutinises the executive is through Select Committees. These
look in depth into issues. Questioning is calm, measured and professional and political answers
aren't accepted. Scrutiny is therefore a lot more professional and less partisan than PMQs. As a
result it can be argued that Select Committees hold the government to account effectively.
The government has to publicly respond to their findings/reports in 8 weeks and they can
sometimes have a direct influence on government policy, ensuring that government
departments perform effectively.
In May 2023, the government accepted/partly accepted all of the Foreign Affairs Select
Committeeʼs recommendations in their report titled Refreshing our approach? Updating
the Integrated Review.
The government agreed with the committeeʼs assertion that an updated strategy
was needed and took on a number of recommendations, including appointing a
Director of Resilience in the Cabinet Office to focus on making the UK less
dependent on other international actors.
Ministerial Question Time, especially PMQs, is also important in allowing Parliament to
scrutinise the executive
The Leader of the Opposition 6 questions) and the leader of the 3rd largest party 2
questions) have the opportunity to get the government to respond directly to their questions
in PMQs, therefore giving them the ability to expose government failure and suggest why
they would be more effective. The government is forced to address the concerns of the
public, the Opposition and backbenchers.
On 8 January 2025, the MP for Hackney North and Stoke Newington Dianne Abbott
raised concerns about the severe shortage of social housing in her constituency during
Prime Ministerʼs Questions PMQs) and demanded action, showing how MPs can force
the government to respond to the concerns of their constituents in PMQs.
Misleading the House of Commons can lead to huge pressure to resign, which forces
Ministers and the Prime Minister to be competent and on top of their policy brief and the
work of their department.
This shows the effectiveness of Parliament in holding the government to account and
decreases the likelihood that incompetent party leaders who favour political
demagoguery over rational argument will lead a major party into an election, as they
would embarrass and harm the partyʼs image every Wednesday at PMQs.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That Parliament Is Ineffective At Scrutinising The
Government
On the other hand, it can be argued that Select Committees are an ineffective method of
scrutinising the government.
A majority of Select Committee members are drawn from the governing party and there is a
tradition that the influential Treasury, foreign affairs and defence committees are chaired by
MPs of the governing party.
This limits their independence from the government and the likelihood they will hold the
government to account effectively.
In the current Parliament, 18/27 of the total Select Committee chairs are from the Labour
Party and there is a majority of Labour members in each committee, due to their huge
174 seat majority in the House of Commons.
Further, the government accepts about 40% of SC recommendations, but these are usually
minor changes. The actual influence of Select Committees on policy can therefore be seen
as limited.
The effectiveness of PMQs in scrutinising the executive has also be questioned. It can be
argued that PMQs in particular provides little effective scrutiny and is more focused on partisan
political point scoring than proper, detailed scrutiny of the government. Ministers and Prime
Ministers often give political answers, intended to deflect and get soundbites for social media
clips rather than to honestly answer scrutiny.
On the 13 November during PMQs, Prime Minister Keir Starmer was asked a question by
Reform UK Leader Nigel Farage, who questioned whether Starmer would prescribe Iran's
revolutionary guard as a terrorist organisation, following an assassination plot against
president-elect Donald Trump.
Rather than answering the question, Starmer responded by avoiding the question and
joking about Farage's frequent trips to America saying he would be "on the immigration
statistics".
This shows how PMQs often donʼt hold the government to account effectively, as PMs
often simply answer a different question to the one asked and resort to witty,
predetermined one-liners.
Further, many government backbenchers ask questions drafted by the whips, which are
intended to flatter rather than scrutinise the government.
For example, on the 13 November 2024, Labour MP for Southport, Patrick Hurley, asked
the Prime Minister if he could confirm that his budget would "provide much needed
investment" and properly support public services "unlike the previous government.ˮ
This was clearly planted by government whips in order to allow the Prime Minister to
condemn the Conservative's policy platform and tout the £1.3 billion investment into
local government announced in the budget.
Finally, effective scrutiny is very dependent on the abilities of backbenchers and the Leader
of the Opposition.
Kemi Badenoch has been criticised by many in the media for underperforming at PMQs and
failing to effectively hold the Prime Minister to account.
For example, in February 2025, she was criticised for failing to challenge the PM on the
Chagos Islands deal and whether the growing cost of the plan increasing to £18 billion is
compatible with his pledge not to return to austerity.
This meant questions on issues that are of high political importance went unanswered,
undermining the purpose of PMQs to forensically pin down the PM and provide scrutiny on
issues the Government would least like to talk about.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Though there are certainly flaws to the scrutiny that Select Committees and Ministerial Question
Time provide, overall Parliament performs its role of scrutinising the government effectively,
particularly as it is able to directly question members of government in a number of different
ways.

Paragraph Three ➡ Representing The Electorate
For: Arguments That Parliament Is Effective At Representing The Electorate

The House of Commons can be seen as representative as it is democratically elected on a
regular basis, with each MP representing a relatively small number of voters.
This means that voters are able to hold their representatives to account and remove them if
they judge them to have not represented them effectively, by voting for a different
party/candidate.
After Conservative MP Scott Benton was suspended from the Commons for breaching
lobbying rules, after being exposed by the Times for offering to leak documents and ask
questions for a fake gambling company, he was replaced by a Labour candidate in the
Blackpool South By-Election on May 2, 2024.
The relatively small size of most constituencies, and the fact that a single MP is responsible for
those who live within the constituency, result in effective representation of local interests and a
strong link between the constituency and MP.
MPs handle correspondence from their constituents and problems their constituents have
and hold weekly constituency surgeries.
MPs also represent their constituentsʼ interests in Parliament.
For example, in October 2022, 33 Conservative MPs rebelled against Liz Trussʼ
government and abstained in a vote on banning fracking, despite the government
ordering them to oppose it, including many MP Mark Fletcher of Bolsover which had a
strong local movement against fracking in the area.
There have also been attempts by parties and the House of Lords to improve direct/descriptive
representation in Parliament.
Parties have used a range of methods to try and improve direct representation, including
using all-women shortlists and headhunting/mentoring promising candidates from minority
backgrounds.
This has resulted in some improvements in direct representation in the House of
Commons. After the 1997 election, just 18% of MPs were women and there were just 9
MPs from minority ethnic backgrounds.
Further, some argue that Parliament isnʼt required to be a microcosm of society and that
minority groups can be listened to and represented by Parliament even if theyʼre not directly
represented.

**************************************************************************************************

Against: Arguments That Parliament Is Ineffective At Representing The
Electorate
The House of Lords is unelected, yet still holds significant powers to influence policy, scrutinise
the government and delay legislation.
Rather than being elected, most members of the House of Lords are appointed by the
House of Lords Appointments Commission or by party leaders at the end of a
Parliament/Prime Ministers after leaving office.
Further, there are still 92 hereditary peers and 26 ‘Lords Spiritualʼ, who are bishops from
the Church of England.
This can be seen as fundamentally unrepresentative, as those who arenʼt elected shouldnʼt
have major power in a democracy.
Further, The House of Commons is elected using the First Past The Post FPTP voting
system, which is very unproportional and unrepresentative in comparison to other voting
systems.
MPs often donʼt vote according to their constituentsʼ wishes, instead they almost always vote
according to how they are whipped to do so, in order to keep in favour with their party and
climb the party ladder, therefore giving themselves a chance of becoming a minister.
In the 20192024 Parliament, the vast majority of MPs rebelled against the party line less
than 2% of the time, with just 8 MPs rebelling more than 5% of the time and the highest
percentage of rebellions being David Davis Conservative) at 9.2% of the time.
Parliament can also be seen as ineffective at representing the electorate in terms of direct
representation. Parliament remains highly unrepresentative in terms of gender, race and
educational background, amongst other ways.
After the 2024 election, just 37% of the House of Commons and 29% of the House of Lords
are women, compared to over half of the population.
After the 2024 election, just 13% of the House of Commons and 7.3% of the House of Lords
are from minority ethnic backgrounds, compared to over 18% of the general population.
This lack of direct representation can be seen as a big problem, not only because it is unjust
and a representative parliament would have symbolic value, but also because it would lead
to representatives who are better able to understand the wishes of the population and who
can broaden the political agenda.
For example, a male dominated parliament is unlikely to fully understand or seek to
address a lot of the discrimination women face in society.

**************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, Parliament is largely ineffective at representing the electorate. The House of Commons
is elected and MPs do seek to represent the interests of their constituents, however this is
outweighed by the fact that the House of Lords is fundamentally undemocratic, MPs usually
vote according to the party whip and the fact that both houses of Parliament under-represent
women, ethnic minorities and those who didnʼt go to university.

**************************************************************************************************
**************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, Parliament largely performs its functions effectively. Though it is ineffective in
representing the electorate, especially in relation to how descriptively representative it is,
Parliament does performs its functions of passing legislation and scrutinising the executive
relatively effectively.
