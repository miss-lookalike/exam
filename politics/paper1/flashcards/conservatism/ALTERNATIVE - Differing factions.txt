Traditional conservatism - Intoduction and Origins   When did it originate and reaction to __ Strand described in what? Defends what based on commitments to (3 things)?	- Origionated in late 1700s as a reaction of Enlightenment and French rev - Strand clearly set out in Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France - Defends the established order of society based on a commitment to organicism, hierarchy and paternalism
Traditional conservatism - organic society   Small description What will destabilise thus ___ needed Founded on ___ that convey ___ held in place ___ Any changes must ___	- Regard society as an organic entity with complex interrelationships  - Radical changes will undermine its sturcture and destabilize -> natural evolution through pragmatism needed - O.S founded on tried and tested institutions that convey authority, stability and obligations -> held in place by tradition  - Any changes must preserve best features -> pragmatic reform drawing on tradition & experience - Also argue implementation of ideological blueprints to bring about an ideal society can only lead to social breakdown and disaster
Traditional conservatism - society should be hierarchical for 3 reasons:	1. People do not have the same abilities so it is 'natural' that society should reflect this -> 'artificial' that all humans should be considered as equals 2. Hierarchy is a functional necessity as people have different jobs, pay depending on contribution made -> essential for co-operation to maintain overall health of the body 3. Good economic incentive
Definition - Noblesse oblige	French phrase that encapsulates the idea that nobility and priviledge bring with them a social responsibility to those less fortunate
Traditional conservatism - noblesse oblige   When was leadership assumed by ___ Traditionals justified it using ___ Also justified with ___ Viewed as ___	- During late 18th/ early 19th century leadership was assumed by the aristocracy - Justified by traditionals -> the aristocracy have ruled for countless generations and are raised with values of social obligation - Also justified with notion of noblesse oblige (paternalism) -> elite rulers could draw on class, leadership and duty in order to make decisions on behalf of society - They viewed this as soft paternalism for they believed society accepted them as the most fit to govern (consent)
One-nation conservatism - Introduction   Updated Responded to what? Who was a central figure who had what opinion?	- Updated traditional conservatism - Response to development of laissez-faire capitalism & 19th century industrialisation - Central figure, B. Disraeli, felt capitalism encouraged self-interested individualism -> undermining social responsibility and threatening to split Britain into 2 nations (rich/poor) - If left unaddressed would result in class conflict and maybe revolution
One-nation conservatism - Disraeli's outlook   What did Disraeli call for? What effect would ___ have ? What else would increase? Organ society dependant on ___ Conservative paternalism should?	- To reunite Britain, Disraeli called for renewed commitment to social obligation -> motives were pragmatic and principled  - Reforms to improve working class conditions would preserve the positions of upper class - Increase working class party support too - Organic society was dependent on elite's acceptence of social responsibility  - Conservative paternalism should embrace social reform to strengthen national unity and thus presereve the 'one-nation'
One-nation conservatism - Disraeli's policies	Social reform: - Sale of Food and Drugs Act (1875) - Second Reform Act (1867) Aimed to forge allience between elite/working class    Maintainance of traditional institutions: - Disraeli was determined to uphold national unity through the preservation of the monarchy and Church of England -> believed such institutions nurtured stability and a national identity
One-nation conservatism - modern day	- Disraeli's ideas have had a profound affect on the development of conservative thinking  - Dominant after WW2 where successive conserv. govs held a one-nation perspective -> adopted Keynesian style intervention to maintain high employment and supported an expanded welfare state and NHS - Cameron drew on this legacy arguing a new 'compassionate conservatism' would uperpin his gov
The New Right - Introduction and origins   - When did it gather momentum? - Founded on which ideologies?	- Gathered momentum from the mid-1970s as a rival to one-nation - Founded on 2 distinct but seemingly opposing ideologies: 1. neo-liberalism / liberal New Right -> modernised version of classical liberalism, based on free market, individual liberty and minimal state 2. neo-conservatism / conservative New Right -> updated form of traditional conservative social thinking based on order and traditional values
The New Right - Core   - What mixture of elements? - Why was it considered radical - What is stressed and what was failing in the 19__ - Where was thinking becoming prominent and thanks to whom?	- Through combining neo-liberal/conservative ideas New Right contains mixture of reactionary and traditional elements - Radical because its determination to abandon gov interventionism in economic / social affairs - At the same time stress traditional views - During 1970s traditional Keynesian interventionist strategies were failing to combat the persistent unemployment and rising inflation - New Right thinking became prominent in US/UK where it became associated with Regan and Thatcher leading to 'Thatcherism' and 'Reaganism'
The New Right - Neo-liberal elements of Thatcherism   What was controlled? What 3 different economic policies were used?	- Tight control over money supply through monetarist policy to control inflation / economic activity - Privitisation of key industries (gas,water,electric) to promte comp and efficiency - Promotion of free market policies through deregulation affecting London finance - Belief trade union power was endangering UK competitiveness so reduced power through legislation
The New Right - neo-conservative elements of Thatcherism   What was defended? Strong ___ based on ___ Opposition of ___	- Defence of traditional values including support for hetrosexual marriage and the nuclear family - Strong law-and-order policies based on support for police - Opposition to permissive attitudes eg Section 28 (law prohibiting the promotion of homosexuality)
Neo-liberalism   Why and when did it emerge? 4 core ideas	Liberal New Right ideas emerging out of the economic problems in the west (1970s) which Keynesianism was not fixing: 1. minimal state 2. self-reliant individuals making their own decisions 3. rejection of collectivism 4. elimination of gov intervention
Neo-liberalism - Introduction   Promoted in the work of who? Sees free market as what? 3 main threats	- Promoted by the work of economists such as Milton Friedman and Friedrich Hayek -> free market - Sees free market as only mechanism that can allocation resources and create prosperity efficiently - Maintains that the operation of the free market must be protected from 3 main threats: monopolies, gov intervention and inflation - Monopolies restrict comp and therefore distort price signals and consumer choice -> inflation the 'great evil' as it reduces the real value of savings, causes reduced investment/consumption - Controlling inflation should be the only goal of gov in the economy
Neo-liberalism - More economics    What did Friedman think about Keynesian policy? What was his alternative? Who utilised it	- Friedman asserted that Keynesian policy to create high employment resulted in inflation - He instead opted for monetarism -> controlling inflation through reductions in gov spending  - Utilised by Thatcher and Reagan in 80s to reduce inflation - Gov intervention seen as most potent threat to economy -> Thatcher embarked on extensive privitisation policy in 80s
Neo-liberalism - human nature   - Advocate ___ ___ - View freedom in ? - Reject what? - Humans may ___	- Advocate atomistic individualism -> individuals rational, self-interested and self-sufficient which links to their belief in free market - View freedom in negative terms -> free when external constraints removed, opposing collectivism and favouring minimal state - Reject excessive welfare as creating dependency culture - Although humans may be selfish, they are rational and entitled to pursue their own self interests
