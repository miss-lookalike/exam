What is Representative Democracy?
?
A form of democracy in which the people select individuals, often sorted into political
parties, to act on their behalf to exercise political choice and make decisions.
The usual form of democracy in the modern world.
These representatives aren't delegates and don't merely take instructions from voters; they
are expected to exercise their own judgement.
?
?
?
They are held to account through regular elections.
What Is Direct Democracy?
?
A direct democratic political system in which individuals express their opinions and vote on
laws and policies themselves and not through representatives acting on their behalf.
Associated with and originated in ancient Athens, where adult male citizens had the right to
take part in decision-making at public meetings. In these public meetings, demagogues
could win over the votes of the people.
?
?
Such a system would not be practical in a large modern state.
How is Direct Democracy Used Today?
?
?
No country in the world has an entirely direct democratic system as it would be impractical
for large nation states in the modern day.
Many countries, however, use referendums.
?
This is the key way direct democracy is in use in the UK today, with infrequent
elections including the Brexit Referendum and Scottish Independence Referendum.
Another example of direct democracy in the UK is The 2015 Recall of MPs Act,
which allows a petition to be triggered if an MP is sentenced to be imprisoned or is
suspended from the Commons for more than 21 days. If 10% of eligible voters in a
constituency sign the petition, a by-election is called. Direct democracy therefore
holds representatives to account.
?
¦
The Wellingborough By-Election on February 15, 2024 followed the recall of
Peter Bone after he was suspended from Parliament for six weeks due to
allegations of bullying and sexual harassment.
¦
In the by-election, Labour recorded the biggest swing from the
Conservatives to Labour since 1994, with Labour overturning a 18,000 Tory
majority.
?
Switzerland is an example of a country that uses direct democracy a lot, with around 10
referendum style votes each year, giving the Swiss people a direct say over many issues.

Rupert Lowe's Vote On Assisted Dying As An Example Of Representative/Direct Democracy:
?
Reform UK MP for Great Yarmouth, Rupert Lowe, voted in favour of the landmark
Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill 2024 after hosting a mini-referendum in his
constituency.
?
He promised to vote according to the majority of the wishes of people in his
constituency, after 75% of 1181 constituents voted in favour of assisted dying in a
poll. He cited wanting to consult his constituents and hand them the power as the
reason to do so.
?
This is an example of a representative trying to introduce an element of direct democracy in
how they represent their constituents.
?
As FPTP creates single-member constituencies, MPs can represent their interests
in Parliament and voters are able to hold their MP to account and vote them out if
they don't do so effectively.
How Has the Use of Direct Democracy in the UK Raised Problems?
?
The UK is a representative democratic system based on Parliamentary Sovereignty. The
direct democracy of recent referendums, particularly Brexit, has conflicted with this system.
In the Brexit referendum, the public disagreed significantly with their representatives with no
major party officially supporting Leave, showing the disconnect between representative and
direct democracy.
?
?
This, combined with the fact that referendums in the UK are not legally binding led to a
second referendum campaign and Brexit taking a long time to complete, eventually needing
an election in 2019.
What Is A Pluralist Democracy?
?
?
A type of democracy in which a government makes decisions as a result of the interplay of
various ideas and contrasting arguments from competing groups and organisations.
For many, pluralist democracy should be encouraged as it ensures all voices in society are
heard and it is less likely for minority groups to be neglected. It also promotes debate and
political engagement within society.

What are the Protective and Developmental Perspectives On Democracy?
?
The protective perspective focuses on the working of democracy as a protection for
individual freedom and doesn't expect large-scale citizen participation in politics but rather
just enough to grant the system legitimacy.
?
Democracy isn't a means of mass participation but a system where a competitive group of
leaders vie for electoral support in the context of a broader respect for individual freedoms
and liberties.
?
?
The developmental perspective of politics believes that for democracy to be successful,
citizens need to engage on an active basis.
Greater citizen engagement would achieve a more open and engaging political system, but
also a more equal society that addresses gender and class inequalities in access to politics.

Similarities and Differences Between Direct and Representative
Democracy
Similarities:
?
Both are types of democracy designed to implement the will of the people and based on the
concept of majority rule, with the people voting playing an important role.
?
Citizens still vote on policy in representative democracy, by voting for
representatives who align with their policy positions.
?
?
They can both be implemented at different levels of government, including local, regional
and national.
In both systems, the people can be swayed by powerful and clever individuals, including
politicians and those in the media. This can happen in all votes in direct democracy and at
election times in representative democracies.
?
Direct democracy plays an important role in representative democracy, for example through
petitions and pressure groups which put forward the views of the public.
Differences:
?
?
?
?
?
?
In direct democracy, individuals express opinions themselves, whereas in representative
democracy citizens elect representatives to make decisions on their behalf.
Citizens are much more involved in decision making on a regular basis in direct democracy,
requiring a lot more of citizens.
In representative democracy, there are political parties, whereas there aren't in direct
democracy, though there are campaigns on each side of the debate.
In representative democracy, a government is elected which can be held to account by the
people.
There are more protections and representation for minorities in representative democracy,
whereas direct democracy acts through rule of the majority.
Representative democracy can be seen as being able to handle complex and technical
political decisions whilst direct democracy can't.
Advantages and Disadvantages Of Direct and Representative Democracy
Direct Democracy:
Advantages
?
Gives equal weight to all votes.
?
This contrasts with representative democracy, where the electoral system and
constituencies will mean that votes are of unequal value.
?
?
Encourages participation in politics.
Removes the need for trusted representatives and minimises the possibility for
corruption/the will of the people not being followed.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
6


 
?
Develops a sense of community and encourages genuine debate, with people feeling like
they have a real stake in the political system.
Disadvantages:
?
?
Impractical in large, heavily populated modern states; complicated decision making.
Many people will not feel qualified to take part in decision making or want to make
important political decisions on a regular basis
?
?
Open to manipulation by the cleverest and most articulate speakers.
Minority viewpoints disregarded, as it is a majoritarian system and the views of the majority
aren't mediated through parliament.
?
It is often criticised for encouraging 'tyranny of the majority'
Representative Democracy:
Advantages
?
Representative democracy is the only practical system in a large country with complex
problems needing rapid responses.
?
This is especially important during crises such as COVID.
?
Parties represent the public and give people a real choice of representatives (though
arguably not that wide a choice).
?
?
?
Reduces the chances of tyranny of the majority, by giving minorities a voice in parliament.
Elections allow representatives to be held to account, ensuring responsible behaviour.
Politicians are (in theory) better informed than the average citizen and less likely to be
swayed by emotional appeals.
Disadvantages:
?
?
?
?
May lead to reduced participation as responsibility is handed to politicians.
Parties and politicians don't necessarily represent the best interests of the people.
Minorities are still underrepresented as they don't hold much electoral weight.
Politicians are skilful in avoiding accountability, especially as elections are relatively
infrequent and politicians seek to shape their own image.
?
Politicians may put their interests and their party's interests before those of the people and
betray election promises.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
7


 
?
For example in 2010 when the Liberal Democrats didn't follow manifesto promises
to scrap tuition fees for students when in coalition government with the
Conservatives.
?
In the UK's FPTP electoral system, representatives are often elected with less than 50% of
the vote and it's therefore likely that more people disagree than agree with them.
?
The same is true of governments, which almost always don't have majority support
from the population, even before turnout is taken into account.

Participation Crisis?
Arguments That UK Democracy Has A Participation Crisis
Elections:
?
Voter turnout in recent UK General Elections has been a great deal less than the average
between 1945 and 1997, which was 76%.
?
In the 2024 general election it was just 59.8%.
?
Turnout is even lower in so-called 'second order' elections and referendums such as those
for devolved bodies and councils.
?
In the 2023 Local Elections turnout was only 32%
?
?
Low turnout means that governments are elected on a reduced share of the popular vote,
therefore calling the strength of their mandate into question.
One explanation for lower voter turnout is political apathy; a lack of awareness or interest in
political issues that affect society.
Party Membership:
?
?
Only 1.6% of the electorate now belongs to a political party, compared to 3.8% in 1983,
however this differs party-to-party.
?
The Conservative Party has an estimated 100,000 members today, a significant
drop from an estimated 400,000 in the mid 1990s.
The Labour Party's membership has fallen from 500,000 under Corbyn to 370,000
today.
?
Less traditional political involvement could be associated with a negative perception of
MPs, due to various scandals and promises being broken.

Other Methods Of Political Participation:
?
Other forms of political involvement are certainly important, but are arguably less impactful
and important than voting and party membership, whilst also suggesting less confidence in
the political system.
?
Many of these methods of participation, particularly online methods such as e-petititons,
can be argued to be a form of "slacktivism"; participation that takes little real effort, has little
real impact and is not a replacement for traditional forms of participation.
?
For example, On the 24th of November 2024, a Petition to call an immediate
general election received 2.9 million signatures, despite the fact that it is highly
unlikely that a general election will be called just months after the electorate
delivered a government with a 170 seat majority.
?
The legitimacy of this form of participation was further undermined by the fact that
some X users urged people internationally, including from China and Russia, to sign
the petition pretending to be UK voters.
Arguments That UK Democracy Doesn't Have A Participation Crisis
Elections:
?
The 2014 Scottish independence referendum and the 2016 EU referendum had turnouts of
4.6% and 72.2% respectively, showing there is still significant engagement surrounding
critical issues.
This perhaps suggests that the population is still very engaged on important political
8
?
issues on which they feel they can have significant control, but vote less in elections
as they have a distrust of professional politicians, in light of the 2010 Expenses

?
An alternative viewpoint is the idea of 'hapathy' (happiness and apathy) suggesting people
are generally content and therefore don't need to push for change. This may explain the low
voter turnout in the 2001 and 2005 elections, when the UK economy was booming, but not
the low turnout in 2010 and 2024.

Proposed Reforms To Improve Participation In UK Elections
?
Some recent reform proposals have focused on increasing turnout by making it easier to
vote by: changing the day of elections from Thursday to the weekend, allowing people to
vote anywhere in their constituency as opposed to a particular polling station, allowing
voting to take place over several days.
?
Two further suggestions are to encourage a wider use of postal voting and to introduce
e-voting (online voting), however these have been criticised as they increase the chances
of cyberattack as well as increasing voter fraud and intimidation.

What Is A Democratic Deficit?
?
'A perceived deficiency in the way a particular democratic body works, especially in terms
of accountability and control over policy-making.'
?
Some commentators argue the UK is suffering from a democratic deficit, as decisions are
taken too often by those with little democratic basis and accountability.

Michelle Mone And The Medpro PPE Scandal:
The PPE Medpro scandal, involving Conservative peer Michelle Mone, has drawn significant public
and legal scrutiny due to allegations of cronyism and mismanagement in the procurement of
personal protective equipment (PPE) during the COVID-19 pandemic.
?
?
?
The controversy began when PPE Medpro, a company linked to Mone's husband, Doug
Barrowman, was fast-tracked through a government 'VIP lane' and awarded contracts
worth over £200 million for supplying PPE to the NHS.
?
These contracts were secured just 44 days after the company was established, and
the equipment supplied, which included face masks and sterilised gowns, was later
found to be unusable.
It was revealed that Baroness Mone had personally referred the company to government
officials without disclosing her financial interests. This led to accusations that she used her
position and connections for personal gain.
?
Subsequent investigations have shown significant transfers of funds, including at
least £65 million, from the profits of these contracts to offshore accounts associated
with Barrowman, some of which benefitted Mone directly.
There are therefore major suspicions of corruption, which has contributed to continued
mistrust of politicians among the public.

What Are Some Arguments That The UK Has A Democratic Deficit?
?
First-past-the-post system - unlike the proportional representation used in many European
countries - means little representation for minority viewpoints in parliament. It is also very
unrepresentative in general, resulting in representatives and governments with limited
legitimacy.
?
General elections produce unrepresentative results; in 2024 the Labour Party
received just 33.7% of the vote.
?
?
The House of Lords is unelected and can still exercise power and halt bills proposed by the
Commons.
Lack of protection for citizens' rights. The European Convention on Human Rights
(incorporated into UK law in 1998) arguably provides inadequate guarantees for the rights
of citizens in relationship with the state as governments can officially state that parts of the
act have no legal authority in certain situations.
?
?
Control of sections of the media by wealthy, unaccountable business interests - Rupert
Murdoch.
Many members of the House of Commons can be seen as looking out for themselves and
their careers rather than caring about representation, as seen by many MPs having second
jobs and going into lobbying after leaving politics.

Who Can Vote Today?
Who can vote in UK Elections?
?
?
?
All adults over 18 who are registered are allowed to vote.
British and Irish citizens have reciprocal rights to vote in each others' countries.
Commonwealth citizens are allowed to vote, as are UK nationals who live abroad.
Who can't vote in UK elections?
?
?
?
?
?

Brits Living Abroad For More Than 15 Years Given The Vote:
?
?
In early 2022, the UK government passed legislation to remove the 15-year limit on the
voting rights of British citizens living abroad. Previously, Britons who had lived outside the
UK for more than 15 years were ineligible to vote in parliamentary elections.
This change was implemented as part of the Elections Act 2022, which aimed to ensure
that all British citizens can participate in its democratic processes.
Voter ID Introduced For Elections Sees 50,000 Turned Away From Voting In 2024:
?
The Conservative government introduced voter ID laws which were implemented starting
with the May 2023 local elections in England. They require voters to present specific forms
of photo identification at polling stations before they could vote.
?
This move was purportedly a means to combat electoral fraud, but this explanation has
been highly criticised given the extremely low instances of such fraud in the UK historically
(just 4 convictions after the 2019 election).
?
This has led to concerns about voter suppression and the undermining of
democracy and rights in the UK, especially since the demographic groups least
likely to have ID most are statistically less likely to vote for the Conservative Party.
?
?
In the 2024 General Election, 0.25% of voters (50,000) were turned away from voting, with
a final number of 16,000 were prevented from exercising the right to vote.
Furthermore, the Electoral Commission highlighted that 750,000 voters lack accepted ID,
and that 10% of those who did not turnout said voter ID was their reason for non-voting.
?
This highlights concerns about the new rules' effect on democracy - the 2024
General Election had historically low turnout at just 59%. This means that voter ID
was a potential barrier to participation, therefore harming the legitimacy of the
government's mandate and the overall health of UK democracy.
Summary Of Key Events In Widening The Franchise
?
The 1832 Great Reform Act abolished rotten boroughs and gave the vote to middle class
men. 8% of the population.
?
?
In 1858, any man over 21 could become an MP whether or not they owned property.
The 1867 Second Reform Act gave many working men the vote. 2.5M could vote (1/3
men).
?
?
The 1872 Ballot Act introduced the secret ballot and therefore decreased the bribery and
bullying involved in voting.
The 1884 Third Reform Act gave most working men the vote. 18% of the population and
2/3 men.
?
?
By 1885, constituencies had to have an equal number of votes.
The 1918 Representation of the People Act granted all men and women over 30 the vote.
75% of the population.
?
?
In 1928, the women's voting age was reduced to 21.
By 1969, everyone 18 and over could vote.
Pre-1832
What were the two types of constituency pre-1832 and who could vote in them?
?
There were two types of constituency: boroughs (towns) and counties, which varied
considerably in size.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
15


 
?
?
In the counties the right to vote was only granted to those who freehold property worth at
least 40 shillings.
Voting rights in boroughs varied according to a range of local rules & traditions. In some
boroughs, all freemen were allowed to vote, whereas in others it depended on property
ownership or the payment of local tax.
What were the problems with the pre-1832 UK political system?
?
The distribution of seats had not kept up with economic growth and population movement,
so some very small boroughs still retained a historic right to vote in MPs while large
emerging industrial towns had no representation of their own.
Plural voting allowed wealthy mean who owned property in more than one constituency to
vote more than once.
?
?
?
Women were excluded from voting.
This meant that by the early 19th century the electorate totalled 400,000 (all men),
compared to a population of around 10,000,000.
The Reform Acts
What did the 1832 Great Reform Act do?
?
?
?
Abolished the separate representation of underpopulated boroughs and created seats for
urban boroughs such as Manchester.
Extended the vote to more people in the counties; including tenant farmers and smaller
property holders.
Created a standard qualification for the franchise in the boroughs, so it now applied to all
male householders living in properties who paid a yearly rental of £10 or more (the middle
classes were growing in importance thanks to the industrial revolution).
The vote increased to an estimated 650,000 - an estimated 5% of the adult population -
compared to around 400,000 in the early 1800s.
?
Who were the Chartists?
?
The Chartist movement grew out of the failure of the 1832 Great Reform Act to extend the
vote to those who didn't own property. It was a mass movement driven by the working
classes and continued for 2 decades.
?
They campaigned for the franchise to be extended to all men over 21, a secret ballot,
payment of MPs and annual parliamentary elections, among other things.
The Campaign For Extending The Right To Vote To Women
Where could and couldn't women vote pre-1918 and why?
?
Until the late 19th century, the exclusion of women from the franchise was largely
unchallenged, as it was assumed that married women were represented by votes cast by
their husbands.
?
Women were, however, allowed to vote in local council elections. Yet, society/those in
power were of the view that only men should have a say in issues of national and imperial
importance.

The Suffragists
?
The situation began to change in 1897, with the establishment of the National Union of
Women's Suffrage Societies (NUWSS) under the leadership of Millicent Fawcett. Members,
who became known as the 'suffragists', were mainly middle-class Women.
They believed in non-violent methods of persuasion such as peaceful demonstrations,
petitions and lobbying MPs.
There was evidence that opinion was changing, however for more radical campaigners
progress was too slow.
?

The Suffragettes
Who were the Suffragettes?
?
The Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) - dubbed the 'suffragettes' by the popular
press - was set up in 1903 by Emmeline Pankhurst; a former suffragist.
They attracted both working-class and middle-class support and used more militant tactics;
aiming to attract publicity and pressure on parliament through attacks on well-known
institutions and the disruption of political meetings and other prominent male-dominated
activities.
?
?
For example, in 1913, suffragette Emily Davison threw herself under the king's
horse at the Epsom Derby.
What was the public attitude towards the Suffragettes and how effective were their methods?
?
?
The suffragettes' strength and endurance attracted hostility but also a degree of sympathy.
Suffragettes who were imprisoned resorted to hunger strikes, leading the authorities to
resort to force-feeding.
?
?
This gave the movement valuable publicity and depicted the Liberal government of the day
as unreasonably harsh.
Even so, the suffragettes showed moderation when they suspended their campaign
following the outbreak of WW1.
How Effective was the Suffrage Movement?
?
?
The effectiveness of the two female suffrage movements remains controversial.
Some historians argue that the quiet, undramatic work of the suffragists has not been
recognised and the violent methods of the suffragettes alienated potential supporters.
Others argue that the suffragette movement was vital in keeping the issue of voting rights
on the agenda in the decade before WW1 and made politicians in 1918 conclude that, if
they did not grant the vote to some women, the campaign would restart and likely be more
successful in a society more favourable to equality.
?
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
17


 
?
Another argument is that the role of Women in WW1 proved to the government their fitness
for the vote. Though the vast majority of female workers were young and unmarried,
therefore not directly benefiting from the 1918 legislation.
1918 Representation of the People Act
?
The act granted all men over 21 and all women over 30 who were householders or wives of
householders the vote.
?
?
75% of the adult population could now vote.
It was passed as there was growing pressure to give all working class men the vote,
considering the fact that they had no stake in the political system they were expected to lay
down their lives for.

Votes At 16
How has the issue of Votes at 16 developed in recent times?
?
The votes at 16 Coalition was formed in 2003 and found early success as they secured a
study of the issue by the Electoral Commission.
?
Though the report came down on the side of no change, several individual Labour, Liberal
Democrat and SNP MPs kept the issue alive in the House of Commons. Labour, the Lib
Dems, the SNP and the Electoral Reform Society now all support votes at 16.
In 2014, the campaign received a significant boost when 16 and 17 year olds were allowed
to vote in the 2014 Scottish Independence Referendum, and the Scottish parliament voted
a year later to allow them to vote in their elections.
?
Labour Support Votes At 16 In Their 2024 Election Manifesto, But Has Yet To Introduce
Legislation:
?
In its 2024 manifesto, Labour pledged to lower the voting age to 16, arguing that this would
empower young people and enhance democracy.
?
They also justified this by highlighting that 16- and 17-year-olds already assume
significant societal responsibilities, such as joining the workforce, and pointing to the
successful implementation of votes at 16 in Scotland and Wales, where it was
credited with increasing political awareness and participation among young people.
?
?
Despite this commitment, the policy has not yet been introduced as a bill in Parliament.
Starmer stated that it would not be implemented in time for the 2025 local elections, citing
the need for thorough planning and debate.
The proposal has been met with strong opposition from both the Conservative Party and
Reform UK. The Conservative Party argues that 16- and 17-year-olds lack the maturity and
life experience necessary to make informed voting decisions, and they have expressed
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/

concerns that expanding the electorate to younger age groups could disproportionately
benefit Labour, as younger voters tend to lean left politically.
?
Nigel Farage, has been particularly vocal in his criticism, accusing Labour of
attempting to "rig future elections" by strategically targeting a demographic
perceived to be more supportive of progressive policies.
?
Farage argued that the policy is politically motivated rather than genuinely
democratic, aimed at securing long-term electoral gains for Labour.

What are digital/e-democracy and what do they achieve?

The terms e-democracy and digital democracy relate to the way in which the development
of social media and the internet have impacted the way in which democracy operates in the
UK.
?
?
?
Digital democracy also refers more specifically to the use of social media by groups that
seek to spread influence.
They help to inform the general public more effectively than traditional media about political
issues.
They allow organisations with modest financial and administrative resources to mount
political campaigns and spread their views.

They allow people to participate more readily and effectively in political action: by becoming

How are e-petitions used in UK democracy and what are some prominent
examples?
?
In the UK, if an e-petition receives 10,000 signatures it will get a response from the
government and if it receives 100,000 and is backed by an MP, it is considered for debate in
parliament.
?
?
The Revoke Article 50 petition of March 2019 received over 6M signatures.
The Ban all ISIS Members from Returning to the UK petition received around 600,000
signatures and was debated in parliament.
?
?
In 2011, a petition calling for the release of all documents relating to the Hillsborough
disaster of 1989 resulted in a debate in parliament, the release of the papers and the
launch of a new inquest.
Websites other than government's, such as 38 Degrees, also have e-petitions and while
they can't force parliament to debate anything, they can pressure the government and
opposition and provide evidence of public support.
E-Petition On Maternity Pay Leads To Policy Change:
?
In June 2023, an e-petition titled "Increase statutory maternity pay in line with cost of living
crisis" received over 38,000 signatures.for an increase in statutory maternity pay to better
support new mothers during economically challenging times.
?
Garnering substantial public support, the petition reached the required threshold of
signatures and was subsequently debated by the Petitions Committee on June 19,
2023.
?
The debate emphasised the financial struggles faced by families with new babies
and the increased costs associated with childcare and living expenses.
?
?
As a result, the government responded and announced an enhancement to maternity pay,
increasing the flat rate of statutory maternity pay from £156.66 to £172.48 per week, a
10.1% increase. This change was implemented in the Social Security Benefits Up-rating
Order of April 2023, a statutory instrument.
This shows how e-petitions allow voters to have an impact on policy between elections and
how this generates significant involvement.

What arguments are there for e-democracy and digital democracy enhancing and

Arguments that e-democracy/digital democracy enhance democracy
?
They increase political participation by bringing in people who don't have the inclination,
ability or time to participate in more conventional ways such as party or pressure group
membership.

?
They make a better informed electorate.
They communicate directly between the government and the governed.
They enhance pluralism by preventing powerful, elitist groups who have concentrated
power from having exclusive access to government.

They give the public greater access to extremist groups, such as those who are racist and

Disinformation is a big problem, with lies having the ability to affect election results.
The government may be influenced by short-term, 'populist' campaigns that don't reflect
wider, national public opinion.

Should Prisoners Get The Vote?
Arguments For
?
Voting is a fundamental right that shouldn't be removed.

should be given voting rights, but that the UK needed to justify its departure from
universal suffrage.
