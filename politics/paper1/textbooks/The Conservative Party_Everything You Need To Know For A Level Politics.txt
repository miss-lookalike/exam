The Origins and Historical Development Of The
Conservative Party
Traditional Conservatives and the Origins of the Conservative Party
?
The party originated in the late 17th century Tory Party, an aristocratic group that first came
together in defence of the historic privileges of the crown and the Church as powerful
landowning institutions.
?
By the 1830s, under the leadership of Sir Robert Peel, it was evolving into a party
dedicated to the defence of property and traditional authority against the threat of
revolution.
?
?
Peel stressed the importance of gradual reform in order to protect and conserve
established institutions, hence 'Conservative'.
By appealing to both the middle class and aristocracy, the Conservatives broadened their
support and were very successful in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.
One Nation Conservatives
?
Traditional conservatism developed into one-nation conservatism, originally associated with
party leader and Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli.
?
'One-nation' conservatism sought to bridge the gulf between the classes through a
paternalistic social policy, in which the 'natural leaders' would act benevolently
towards the disadvantaged in return for acceptance of their right to rule.

?
Disraeli and later leaders sought to win popular support through social reform and a
patriotic' foreign policy, with the aim of strengthening national unity.
The ideology peaked in the years after WW2, when the party broadly accepted the changes
introduced by the 1945-51 Atlee government (welfare state, NHS and government action to
maintain high employment). They balanced free enterprise with state intervention in
economic and social policy.
'
?
They prided themselves on a pragmatic, non-ideological approach. They maintained
the party contest between themselves and the Labour Party but undid few of their
policies when they took office.
?
This period was known as the 'post-war consensus'.
Thatcher and the New Right
What was Thatcherism?
?
Thatcher gave her name to a more ideological form of conservatism; 'Thatcherism' was
linked intellectually to the rise of the 'New Right' school of thought.
?
It sought to reduce state intervention in the economy, while restoring order to society in the
face of militant trade unions and other groups on the left.
?
In the UK, this New Right approach was slightly limited by the popularity of the NHS,
though.
?
?
Thatcher embodied strong conviction politics and her radical policy agenda rejected the
instinct of one-nation conservatives to seek compromise.
?
As a result, she was and is an extremely divisive figure in UK politics.
She was ultimately very successful, winning 3 elections and massively changing
government policy in a range of areas.
What were the key themes of Thatcherism?
?
Neoliberal economics
?
Limited public spending, tax cuts for businesses and the rich to stimulate economic
growth.
?
Privatisation of previously state-held industries and services to promote
improvement, innovation and consumer choice through competition.
?
?
Legally limiting the power of trade unions to deter strikes.
A tough approach to law and order.

After Thatcher To Cameron
John Major
?
?
Following Thatcher's departure in November 1990, the party struggled for a decade and a
half to find an identity independent of her.
?
John Major (1990-97) in a way represented the continuation of Thatcherism, with
the privatisation of Coal and Railways, however he was a less confrontational figure.
He narrowly won the 1992 general election, but his premiership was troubled by divisions
over Europe. These divisions, along with a series of scandals and a growing sense of
exhaustion on the part of the government, led to a huge defeat to Tony Blair in 1997.
?
Major was a moderate pro-European who unsuccessfully tried to reconcile two
opposing factions; hard-line Eurosceptics who wanted stronger resistance vs the
encroaching power of the EU and a smaller group of pro-Europeans who wanted
Britain to remain influential over a rapidly integrating continent.
The Conservative Party During The Blair Years
?
The next three leaders of the party, William Hague, Ian Duncan Smith and Michael Howard,
failed to unseat Tony Blair who successfully held the centre ground of UK politics and won
two subsequent elections in 2001 and 2005.
?
?
All three leaders failed to distance themselves from the now discredited Thatcherism and
move the party beyond an association with Europe, immigration and law & order.
The party's ageing membership and outdated policies failed to appeal to an increasingly
diverse society.

Cameron
How Did Cameron 'Detoxify' The Conservative Party?
?
Cameron's election as party leader in December 2005 brought the fresh thinking of a new
generation to the party; respectful to Thatcher but aware Britain had considerably changed
since she left office.
?
Cameron learnt from the way Blair had reinvented the Labour Party to win support
beyond its traditional core vote. He identified himself as a 'liberal conservative' who
was tolerant of minority groups and different lifestyles.
?
He showed an interest in the environment, an issue gaining more and more importance,
and demonstrated that he valued public services such as the NHS.
?
He therefore attempted to shift the party somewhat back to a moderate one-nation
conservatism.
?
His premiership was ultimately defined by austerity (spending cuts to reduce
government deficits) and divisions over Europe, though, which arguably went
against this one-nation shift.
How Did Cameron Seek To Move Away From Thatcherism?
?
Both Cameron and May maintained that they stood on the side of ordinary people rather
than just the elite and tried to move the party away from its image as the 'nasty party'.
Whilst Thatcher presented the Conservatives as the party for individualism and
self-determination, Cameron emphasised the bonds between people, arguing for
co-operation between the state and voluntary sector in building the 'Big Society', in many
ways it seemed like an updated version of one-nation conservatism.
?
?
Thatcher's morally authoritarian tone was replaced by, for example, support for the
legalisation of gay marriage.

election.

?
Her reign was defined by chaos and division within the party over Brexit, eventually
leading to Boris Johnson replacing her after she'd lost the support of the
Brexit-supporting wing of the party.

The Policies And Ideas Of The Conservative Party
The Policies Of The Conservative Party Under Thatcher
Economic Policy
?
Thatcher greatly reduced taxes and public spending in an attempt to encourage
individualism.
?
The top rate of income tax was reduced from 83% when she came to power to 40%
in her last budget. Corporation tax reduced from 52% to 34%.
?
In her first few years, spending was cut by £1 billion including cuts in housing,
energy, education, employment, industrial subsidies, transport and foreign aid. She
didn't, however, reduce funding for the police and armed forces.
?
Thatcher supported privatisation and the removing the state from the market and
introduced a series of massive privatisations starting in 1981 with British Telecom.
?
Her government also introduced the 'right-to-buy' scheme to allow people to
purchase and own their own council houses.
Law and Order Policy
?
Thatcher took on the unions. She called them the 'enemy within' and successfully reduced
their powers, by making striking more difficult and using the police against striking workers,
often violently.
?
?
Despite cutting the budgets of other departments, she increased funding for the police and
armed forces, representing a strong law and order policy.
Thatcher was generally very tough on crime.
Welfare Policy
?
With the 1988 Education Reform Act, Thatcher removed the control over education from
local politicians, making schools self-governing and the curriculum decided by the UK
Government.
?
Thatcher cut the budgets of many government departments and reduced some benefits,
but didn't privatise the NHS due to the strong public support for it.

Foreign Policy
?
Thatcher had a very strong, assertive foreign policy leading to her gaining the reputation of
being the 'iron lady'
?
She strongly projected Britain's interests abroad, including against the USSR and in
the Falklands War, and developed a strong relationship with Reagan in the US.
?
?
She was also eurosceptic and sought to protect Britain's sovereignty against a growing EU.
She did support the economic community and liberalisation of the EU, though.
She also refused to impose sanctions on apartheid South Africa and described Nelson
Mandela as a 'terrorist'.
Other Policy
?
Section 28 of The Local Government Act of 1988 introduced a ban on promoting
homosexuality in schools, which was extremely homophobic and has been strongly
condemned. It represented Thatcher supporting 'Christian values'.
?
Near the end of her premiership, Thatcher caused great controversy by introducing the poll
tax to replace council tax, which would mean all individuals paid the same amount rather
than payment being based on the value of one's property.
Overall
?
Overall, Thatcher had a huge impact on the policy of the Conservative Party and the politics
of the UK.
?
She completely rejected one-nation conservative ideas of compromise, instead being a
'conviction politician' and successfully implementing her New Right agenda.
?
She continues to be a very divisive figure today.

Cameron's priority was to reduce the budget deficit he inherited from Labour and introduce

The budgets of government departments were cut by up to 25%.

In opposition, Cameron seemed to take a more liberal attitude towards law and order,

In office he tried to take a balanced approach. He supported tough sentencing for

Welfare Policy
?
The policies of the coalition were intended to cut costs and encourage those receiving

The coalition also implemented radical overhaul of the NHS, allowing the private sector to

Foreign Policy
?
Cameron's approach was similar to Thatcher's in that he promoted the 'special relationship'

Cameron tried to fight Britain's corner in the EU, attempting to renegotiate the UK's terms of

Other Policy
?
Cameron sought to strongly tackle climate change, saying his government would be the

?
Partly influenced by being in power with the Liberal Democrats in the coalition, Cameron

?
Overall, Cameron sought to liberalise the party somewhat and did in some important ways
with the legalisation of same-sex marriage and 'rehabilitation revolution', moving closer to a
one-nation approach.
?
His strong foreign policy and economic policy, which became defined by austerity, remained
very reminiscent of Thatcher, though.

The Unions and Protests Under Sunak:
?
Sunak also took a very tough approach to striking workers and protests. He introduced
Strikes (Minimum Service Levels) Act 2023, which requires minimum service levels in key
public sectors and therefore limits the power of strikes.
?
The Public Order Act (2023) introduced new offenses such as "locking-on" and interfering

It was enacted in response to the UK government's concerns over highly disruptive

The Passage Of Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Act 2024:
?
The Conservative Party finally formalised its Rwanda immigration policy by passing the
Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Act in April 2024. The Act allowed for the
deportation of asylum seekers who enter the UK illegally to Rwanda for processing of their
asylum cases.
?
The legislation emphasises treating Rwanda as a generally safe country for these
deportations and restricts domestic courts from challenging this designation on
broad grounds.
?
Sunak has announced that an additional 150 judges will be recruited to fast track
appeals against deportation to Rwanda.
?
?
The policy was intended to deter illegal immigration to the UK, particularly through unsafe
and unauthorised routes including crossing the channel. The government argued that by
removing asylum seekers to a third country deemed safe, it would disrupt human trafficking
and smuggling networks, thereby reducing the flow of unauthorised entries into the UK.
It represents a very strong stance against immigration and illegal immigration in particular
and was introduced despite the extremely high cost and strong criticisms from human rights
groups that Rwanda was a safe country and that the asylum process would ensure fair and
humane treatment.
?
The cost included an initial payment to Rwanda of £290M and an additional £11,000
per asylum seeker for flights, and nearly £151,000 per person for processing and
integration over five years.
?
?
The Conservative Party pledged to continue the policy in the 2024 election, seeing it as a
deterrent against illegal immigration.
In their 2024 manifesto, they also pledged to introduce a legal cap on migration by getting
Parliament to fix an annual number of work and family visas and decrease this number
each year.

The Conservative Government's Strong Support For Ukraine and Israel
?
In 2024, the Conservative government's foreign policy continued to demonstrate strong
support for both Israel and Ukraine, reflecting a commitment to the UK's allies and the
promotion of UK interests abroad.

funding to Ukraine, making the UK one of the largest providers of military aid to Ukraine in

Foreign Secretary David Cameron also sought to persuade the US House of

The Conservative Party's Culture War In Relation To Gender
?
?
?
An important aspect of Conservative Party messaging and policy in 2023 and 2024 was in
relation to gender and identity politics, with the party pushing back against transgender
rights in particular.

back against calls for transgender rights and recognition, in particular through stating that a

Additionally, the Conservative faced criticism for its decision not to fully ban conversion
therapy. While the government initially promised to ban the practice for all LGBTQ+

and the wider public, who argued that the failure to ban all forms of conversion
therapy constitutes a significant oversight in protecting the rights and well-being of
