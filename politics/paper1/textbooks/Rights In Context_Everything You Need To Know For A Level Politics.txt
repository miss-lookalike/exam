Introduction To Rights In The UK
Rights
What are rights?
?
Rights are legally protected freedoms (also known as civil liberties).
?
They are absolute, universal and fundamental. This means they can't be
compromised, everyone has them and they can't be removed.
?
?
In the UK they were historically unwritten and there was just an understanding of people's
entitlements. However they are now guaranteed by the 1998 Human Rights Act.
It was and arguably still is broadly accepted that rights might have to be limited in times of
war or other major national emergencies, such as freedom of movement was during
COVID.
What do rights in the UK include?
?
Fair and equal treatment under the law. Including fair trials, peaceful possession of property
and freedom from arbitrary detention.
?
?
?
?
?
Freedom of expression (speech and writing).
Freedom of conscience (including religion).
The right to vote, stand for election and to join a party or pressure group.
The right to belong to an association such as a trade union.
Freedom of movement.

2


 
?
More contentious are 'social rights' Including the right to education, employment, healthcare
and welfare support.
What are civic responsibilities and obligations?
?
?
?
Legal obligations such as obeying the law, paying taxes and performing jury service.
These are seen as complementary to rights.
Moral responsibilities such as voting in elections and playing a part in protecting the
environment.
?
Ideas of 'active citizenship' go further and include offering voluntary community service.
How were rights prior to the Human Rights Act 1998?
?
Until 1998, there was no single document positively setting out citizens' rights. Instead there
were 'negative rights' (things people could do providing the law didn't explicitly prohibit
them).
?
Negative rights still play a role for areas not covered by the Human Rights Act.
?
?
For example, people had the right to freedom of expression as long as it complied with laws
against defamation and blasphemy.
Some rights were protected by acts of parliament, while others derived from custom or
common law.
How is the UK said to have a rights-based culture?
?
Since the Human Rights Act, it is often claimed that the UK has developed a 'rights-based
culture' as all new legislation must be compliant with the act and judges can declare earlier
acts of parliament incompatible with it (though they can't legally compel parliament to make
changes due to the concept of parliamentary sovereignty).
How are rights protected in the UK?
?
As the UK doesn't have a codified constitution, the rights of citizens aren't protected by an
entrenched bill of rights as they are in the US
?
Instead, they are protected by:
?
?
?
?
Parliamentary legislation such as the Equality Act and Human Rights Act.
Pressure groups.
The House of Lords, parties and other groups in parliament.
Common Law, such as the presumption of innocence.

Key Milestones In The Development Of Rights
Magna Carta (1215)
?
Usually regarded as the oldest statement of rights in the UK. Its original purpose was to
limit royal power, due to King John's tyrannical rule. It has been widely hailed as the
foundation of the rights of the citizen and is regarded as establishing the right to trial by jury.
The Bill Of Rights (1689)
?
Parliament further restricted the power of the monarch and increased its power.
The European Convention on Human Rights (1950)
?
?
Drawn up by the council of Europe, with the UK as one of its signatories. It was very similar
to the United Nations Declaration of Human Rights drawn up after the violations of WW2.
The European Court of Human Rights was set up in Strasbourg to hear cases where
people felt their rights had been infringed upon in their own countries. UK citizens could
appeal to the court, but it was time-consuming and expensive, leading to inequality in the
protection of rights for different citizens.
The Human Rights Act (1998)

What is the Human Rights Act?
?
Passed by New Labour in 1998. It enshrined the European Convention on Human Rights
into UK law, in effect from 2000, meaning that the rights could be defended in UK courts
rather than having to go to Strasbourg.
?
Including the right to life, right to a fair trial, right to privacy and a family life, prohibition of
torture.
Arguments for the Human Rights Act
?
Since the HRA, it is often claimed that the UK has developed a 'rights-based culture' as all
new legislation must be compliant with the act and judges can declare earlier acts of
parliament incompatible with it (though they can't legally compel parliament to make
changes due to the concept of parliamentary sovereignty).
?
Parliament usually acts to address any issues raised by the courts and parliament
also has a Joint Committee on Human Rights to scrutinise bills and ensure they are
compatible, demonstrating the 'persuasive influence' of the HRA.
?
For example, in A v Secretary of State for the Home Department, part of The
Anti-terrorism, Crime and Security Act 2001 was declared incompatible with Articles
5
and 14 of the human rights act by permitting the detention of suspected
international terrorists in a way that discriminated on the grounds of nationality or
immigration status.The Prevention of Terrorism Act 2005 was amended by
parliament as a result.
?
?
?
Its defenders argue that the HRA places public authorities in the UK under an obligation to
treat everyone with fairness, equality and dignity.
The HRA educates citizens about their rights and makes them more high profile in the
political system.
Article 2 of the ECHR protected in the HRA (the right to life), which places a duty on the
state to investigate deaths where agents of the state such as the police were involved, was
used to obtain a new inquest into the Hillsborough disaster.
Arguments against the Human Rights Act
?
Many critics on the right say the HRA stops terror suspects from being deported and that
the HRA therefore favours undeserving individuals rather than protecting the legitimate
freedoms of UK citizens and protecting society as a whole.
?
For example, Abu Qatada, a radical Muslim cleric who had advocated the use of
violence to promote the Islamist cause and had spent some time in British jails, was
wanted by the security services to be deported to Jordan where he was wanted for
trial.
?
However his legal advisers managed to delay this for 8 years on the grounds that he
might be tried using evidence obtained through torture, a breach of the HRA.
?
?
It has also been criticised for giving unelected judges too much power.
The HRA may be used to check other laws, but it is not entrenched and could still be
repealed by a simple act of parliament, whilst also not being binding on parliament.
?
The Illegal Migration Bill, for example, was introduced by Home Secretary Suella
Braverman on 7th March 2023 with a section 19 note stating that there was a
likelihood the provisions of the bill would be incompatible with the Human Rights Act
and international law (The European Convention on Human Rights), but that the
government wanted to proceed with it nonetheless.
?
The UK government ignored a ruling by the European Court of Human Rights
(ECHR) in 2005 (Hirst v United Kingdom), which stated that the blanket ban on

Freedom Of Information Act (2000)
?
The Freedom of Information Act made it easier for opposition MPs, journalists and voters to
ask for information about the government. Members of the public also gained the right to
access any information held about them by public bodies.
Equality Act (2010)
?
?
Brought together earlier pieces of legislation that had sought to outlaw discrimination and
unfair treatment (including the 1970 Equal Pay Act and the 1976 Race Relations Act).
It identified nine 'protected characteristics' and made it illegal for public bodies, employers,
service providers and other organisations and individuals to discriminate against people on
any of these grounds, in the workplace or in wider society.
?
These are age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy
and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex, and sexual orientation.

The Judiciary
Judicial Review
What is judicial review?
?
?
?
Judicial review is a type of court proceeding in which a judge reviews the lawfulness of a
decision or action made by a public body. Courts have the power to ensure those in power
have not acted beyond the authority given to them by law in what are called ultra vires
cases.
?
'ultra vires' means beyond the law
This can be in relation to a number of things
?
Going beyond the authority given to them within laws passed (this can be whether
they're stretching secondary legislation too far or doing something that is against
primary legislation
?
Checking whether a law or action is in line with the Human Rights Act.
Most applications concern local authorities and not ministers, only a quarter are allowed to
go forward and only 10% against the government manage to win.
Arguments for judicial review
?
Defenders argue that judicial review is a vital means of defending citizens' rights, enabling
the legality of govt actions to be scrutinised.
?
Pressure groups often fund and instigate legal challenges on behalf of individuals.
Arguments against judicial review
?
Critics argue it places too much power in the hands of unelected and unaccountable
judges, who are able to 'make law'.
The Safety Of Rwanda Act:
Initial Act and Agreement
The UK government under Sunak originally formed a migration partnership with Rwanda in April
2022, intending to relocate asylum seekers who arrived in the UK illegally to Rwanda where their
asylum claims would be processed. This was part of a broader effort to deter illegal crossings and
dismantle human trafficking networks
Supreme Court Ruling
This arrangement faced significant legal hurdles, culminating in a November 2023 ruling by the UK
Supreme Court. The court found the policy unlawful, citing risks of refoulement (the act of forcibly
returning refugees or asylum seekers to a country where they are likely to face persecution) and
inadequacies in Rwanda's asylum system as major concerns. They argued that as a result, the
policy was against the Human Rights Act and the 1951 Refugee Convention, as Rwanda couldn't
be defined as a 'safe country'.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
9


 
New Agreement and Legislative Response
In response to the Supreme Court's concerns, the UK and Rwanda drafted a new treaty, signed in
December 2023, which included more robust guarantees regarding the treatment of transferred
individuals. This treaty aimed to enhance the procedural safeguards within Rwanda's asylum
process, including better training for decision-makers and a stronger monitoring framework.
Safety of Rwanda Bill
To solidify the new treaty and address the legal issues identified by the Supreme Court, the UK
government introduced the Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Bill. This legislation, which
received Royal Assent in April 2024, states that Rwanda must be considered a safe country for
asylum seekers and that international law must be put aside to allow for the deportation of asylum
seekers to Rwanda.
?
?
On the one hand, it can be argued that this shows how the Supreme Court can protect
rights effectively in the UK, as its ruling led the government to secure additional guarantees
and a new treaty with Rwanda.
On the other hand, it can be argued that they have limited power if the government and
parliament is intent on an action/policy that threatens rights in the UK. This is because the
government, rather than changing to a different policy that was legal, changed the law to
make their policy consistent with domestic law (rather than the policy itself), whilst stating in
that law that international law should not be implemented in this case.
?
This shows that the Supreme Court can only protect rights if the law does.

Legislation Passed By Recent Governments That Threaten Rights
The Policing, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022
?
?
The Policing, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act, introduced in April 2022 gives greater
powers to the police and criminal justice system to impose restrictions on 'unacceptable
protests', among other things.
It gives the Home Secretary significant powers to decide the type of protest deemed
unacceptable and acceptable by the state. The Act gives power to limit protests on the
basis of them being in the buffer zone of parliament, too loud etc.
?
The day after the Act came into force, anti-Brexit activist Steve Bray had his
amplification equipment seized by the police.
?
?
It can be seen as in part in response to direct action campaigns by pressure groups and
social movements in the UK, including Extinction Rebellion and the Black Lives Matter
movement.
It is an extremely controversial law that was widely criticised by human rights groups,
politicians and journalists who argued it significantly impeded freedom of speech and the
right to protest in the UK.
The Public Order Act 2023:
?
The Public Order Act, which received Royal Assent on May 2, 2023, was enacted in
response to the UK government's concerns over highly disruptive protest tactics employed
by groups like Extinction Rebellion and Just Stop Oil.
?
The Act introduced new offenses such as "locking-on" (attaching oneself to objects
or other people to cause serious disruption) and interfering with national
infrastructure (including the roads around Parliament), which are punishable by
imprisonment or unlimited fines.
?
This legislation was designed specifically to limit the effectiveness of pressure groups that
use disruptive tactics to draw attention to their causes, by imposing stricter penalties and
giving law enforcement new tools to manage and respond to protests.

Voter ID Introduced For Elections:
?
?
The Conservative government introduced voter ID laws which were implemented starting
with the May 2023 local elections in England. They require voters to present specific forms
of photo identification at polling stations before they could vote.
This move was purportedly a means to combat electoral fraud, but this explanation has
been highly criticised given the extremely low instances of such fraud in the UK historically
(just 4 convictions after the 2019 election).
?
Critics argue that these new requirements disproportionately affect younger voters
and minority groups who are less likely to have the specified forms of identification.
Older and disabled individuals can use bus and travel passes as IDs, which are not
permitted for younger people's equivalents.
?
This has led to concerns about voter suppression and the undermining of democracy and
rights in the UK, especially since these demographic groups are statistically less likely to
vote for the Conservative Party.
?
In the 2024 General Election, 0.25% of voters (50,000) were turned away from
voting, with a final number of 16,000 were prevented from exercising the right to
vote.
?
Furthermore, the Electoral Commission highlighted that 750,000 voters lack
accepted ID, and that 10% of those who did not turnout said voter ID was their
reason for non-voting.

Proposed Changes To The Human Rights Act By The Conservative Government
?
At various points, the Conservative Party has backed pulling out of the European
Convention on Human Rights and replacing the Human Rights Act with a British Bill of
Rights, which has been criticised by hundreds of human rights groups for seeking to
remove rights in the UK.
?
The proposed Bill of Rights would make it easier to deport foreign criminals by
elevating public safety over the right to family life. It would also increase freedom of
the press by elevating the right to freedom of expression over the right to privacy.
This British Bill Of Rights would make the UK Supreme Court the final court of
appeal, rather than the European Court of Human Rights.
It would also weaken the protection of rights in the UK, as the British Bill of Rights
would be a simple Act of Parliament, whilst the European Convention of Human
Rights is an international treaty.
?
?
?
?
During the 2024 Conservative Party leadership election, Robert Jenrick advocated for the
UK's withdrawal from the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), arguing that it
was necessary to address illegal immigration and bolster national security.
?
He argued that the ECHR's jurisdiction impeded the UK's ability to deport individuals
with criminal records and manage its borders, whilst departing from the ECHR
would restore parliamentary sovereignty and enable the UK to implement
immigration policies without external interference.
Since assuming leadership, Kemi Badenoch has not dismissed the possibility of leaving the
ECHR. She has expressed concerns that international bodies, including the ECHR, have
overstepped their original mandates and undermine UK sovereignty.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/

?
Badenoch has indicated that if these institutions do not undergo necessary reforms,
the UK should consider disengaging from them.
?
Leaving the ECHR (and by impliction removing the Human Rights Act) would not only
significantly reduce the protection of individual rights in the UK, it would also be a significant
change to the UK Constitution.
?
The ECHR and its copying into UK law as the Human Rights Act has been the basis
of rights protection in the UK Constitution for decades.
Key Debates Over Rights
Collective vs Individual Rights
How can collective and individual rights be seen to conflict?
?
Individual and collective/group rights can often be seen to conflict, especially in relation to
public safety and security, where the protection of the population is often used to justify the
encroachment of the rights of individuals.
Terrorism as an example
How Blair's government clashed with the House of Lords following terrorist attacks in the
2000s:
?
The high profile terrorist attacks of the 2000s led to government measures that limited civil
liberties in the interests of protecting the wider community. Ministers argued they had the
right to detain terror suspects without trial on the grounds that a national emergency
existed.
?
?
In December 2004, the law lords ruled that indefinite detention of foreign nationals on
suspicion of terrorist charges was discriminatory.
Faced with this legal challenge the govt passed a law to introduce a system of control
orders that enabled suspects to be closely monitored, e.g. through electronic tagging, a
requirement to report to the police and the removal of phones + internet access.
Despite adverse ruling from judges, control orders were kept in place until 2011, when the
coalition govt replaced them with a modified version known as 'Terrorism Prevention and
Investigation Measures'.
?
How does counter terrorism clash with individual rights?
?
There has not been an unremitting erosion of human rights due to counter terrorism
measures. Though some restrictive measures - notably Blair's proposal to extend the
period a suspect could be held for and not charged from 14 to 90 days - have been
defeated in the Commons, with this one reaching a compromise of 28 days.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
14


 
?
Governments have tended to place the safety of society above the protection of individual
rights, which is why pro-human rights pressure groups such as Liberty have had little
success in deflecting government policy.
?
E.g. in 2013, the organisation failed to stop the introduction of so-called 'secret
courts', used for major criminals and terrorist suspects, where evidence against
them doesn't need to be disclosed in full. Nor did they arrest the passage of the
Investigatory Powers Bill, which increases the power of the intelligence agencies by
obliging internet companies to store info about customers' browsing history.
COVID as an example
?
?
The COVID-19 pandemic showed how Parliament can remove the rights of the population
very quickly in response to emergencies, with freedom of movement and organisation being
seriously restricted during lockdowns in order to prevent the spread of the virus.
There are fears that lockdowns set a precedent of the erosion of rights that could be
repeated.
Are Rights Well Protected In The UK
Arguments that rights are well protected in the UK
?
?
?
?
Everyone is equal before the law and has a wide range of rights.
The Equality Act ensures that everyone is treated fairly and criminalises discrimination.
The Human Rights Act can be seen as effective in protecting rights in the UK.
Civil Liberties Pressure Groups are effective in protecting and campaigning for the
extension of human rights in the UK.
?
?
?
The House of Lords especially is important in upholding rights.
The judiciary is important in limiting the government and protecting rights in the UK.
There have been various extensions of rights in the UK in the past couple of decades,
particularly for LGBT individuals.
Arguments that rights aren't well protected in the UK/they're being eroded
?
COVID and counter-terrorism measures show how human rights can be eroded in favour of
protecting the collective.
?
Recent Conservative governments have introduced legislation that contravenes human
rights (especially the Policing, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act), whilst the planned British
Bill of Rights can be seen as worrying.
?
The new Labour Government has continued this trend by using the Public Order Act
in relation to protests and removing the right to citizenship for asylum seekers who
enter the UK illegally.
?
Judicial review can be argued to give too much power to unelected, unaccountable judges.
