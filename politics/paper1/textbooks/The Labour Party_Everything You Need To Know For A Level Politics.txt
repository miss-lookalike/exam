The Origins and Historical Development Of The Labour Party
Origins Of The Labour Party
?
The Labour Party was founded in 1900 by a group of socialist societies and trade unions,
with the original purpose of getting more working class MPs into parliament, so that they
could push their interests. This is why the party was known until 1906 as the Labour
Representation Committee.
?
?
Traditionally, the politically active working class had supported the Liberal Party, however by
the 20th century it felt it needed a party specifically concerned with its interests.
Within the party's 1918 constitution, Clause IV committed it to campaign for the 'common
ownership of the means of production, distribution and exchange'.
?
In simpler terms this meant socialism: for the state to nationalise key industries and
run them in the interests of the community rather than just for profit.
?
After the expansion of the franchise to include working class men in the 1918
Representation of the People Act, the Labour Party was able to have more electoral
success and formed two short-lived minority governments in 1924 and 1929-31 under the
leadership of Ramsay Macdonald.

Old Labour (Social Democracy)
Labour's Post-War Social Democratic Governments
?
?
Clement Atlee's 1945 government was the first one in which Labour had a parliamentary
majority and the first one that was able to make important changes.
These included nationalisation of coal, railways, power, steel and civil aviation; a
comprehensive system of social security (inspired by the wartime Beveridge Report) and
the NHS, free at the point of need.
?
Labour governments between 1945 and 1979 described themselves as socialist but were a
moderate version known as Social Democracy.
?
This did emphasise the importance of redistributing the wealth and creating a fairer
society, but did not try to abolish capitalism. Instead, it aimed to manage it (through
Keynesian economic management) so that it didn't exploit the workforce and so that
there was full employment and equality of opportunity.
The Breakdown Of Old Labour
?
The last 'Old Labour' Prime Minister James Callaghan was defeated in the 1979 general
election, leading to a division between moderate social democrats and more left-wing
elements, who then captured the party under the leadership of Michael Foot.
Labour lost the 1983 election on a hardline socialist programme of further nationalisation,
increased tax and spending, the abolition of the UK's nuclear weapons and withdrawal from
the 'capitalist' EEC.
?
?
This hardline manifesto is often referred to as 'the longest suicide note in history'.
?
Following this catastrophic defeat, Neil Kinnock, drawn from the party's centre-left was
elected and the slow work of rebuilding began.

How Did New Labour Follow On From Old Labour?
?
In the early 1980s, the Labour Party began to move away from its hard-left position, so that
it could broaden its support.
?
The party gradually recognised that as the old industrial base of the country disappeared
and people became more affluent, a party that only appealed to the working class wouldn't
be able to win an election.
?
It took two more election defeats in 1987 and 1992, as well as a forceful leader in Tony
Blair, to complete this process.
How The Labour Party Changed Under New Labour
?
The party was rebranded 'New Labour' and aimed to find a third way between free-market
capitalism and old-style socialism (and its key principle of equality), under the influence of
progressive thinker Anthony Giddens.
?
The party dropped unpopular policy proposals, crucially revising clause IV of its constitution
in 1995 so that it was no longer committed to nationalisation and the redistribution of
wealth.
?
The significance and power of the trade unions in the party was also downgraded
and the leadership developed links with the business community, moving away from
high taxes on corporations and the rich.
?
This stopped the party being a purely working-class party
?
?
?
Labour became more pro-European as the EU began to adopt policies that protected
workers' rights, such as the 'Social Chapter'.
The party emphasised managing the media to project a modern image and tried to
demonstrate party unity and discipline.
The party also emphasised modernisation and democratisation (embracing key parts of
liberalism), introducing big changes to the constitution such as devolution and reforming the
House of Lords.
?
Blair won by a landslide in May 1997, helped by the disintegration of John Major's
Conservative government. He then went on to be re-elected twice, though he became
unpopular due to the Iraq War, before standing down in 2007 to be replaced by his
long-serving chancellor Gordon Brown, who was defeated in the 2010 election.
Brown and the Response To The 2008 Financial Crash
?
In response to the 2008 financial crisis, the treasury pumped money into the banking
system in order to boost economic activity and the government nationalised or
part-nationalised the most vulnerable banks to restore confidence.

How The Move To New Labour Was Divisive
?
Many traditional socialists rejected New Labour's modernising efforts as a betrayal of their
heritage and felt that Blair was too connected with business leaders and too positive about
the values of the free market.
?
?
His building of close links with the US government, leading to the 2003 Iraq war, further
damaged his credentials as a progressive figure.
Blair's supporters, however, argued that New Labour was a necessary adaptation to a

Milliband
Labour Party Policy Under Ed Miliband
?
?
?
The period of opposition under Miliband between 2010 and 2015 saw the party take up a
not always coherent position.It maintained some of New Labour's policies while shifting
slightly to the left.
?
He called for the restoration of the 50% top tax rate, reduced 5% by the coalition, as
well as a temporary energy price freeze.
One shouldn't exaggerate, however, the extent to which Miliband broke away from Blair and
Brown. They were keen to re-establish Labour's reputation as competent managers of the
economy and didn't reject the coalition's whole economic strategy.
?
Instead they concentrated their attacks on the most controversial aspects of
austerity, such as the bedroom tax, and called for a crackdown on tax avoidance.
Miliband tried to combine New Labour's support for business with the defence of the
working class (still Labour's core constituency) by distinguishing between what he called
'predatory' and 'responsible' capitalism.
How Milliband Was Presented In The Media
?
Irrespective of the actual moderation of many of Miliband's policy proposals, he was
dubbed 'Red Ed', with many media commentators ascribing his defeat in the 2015 May
election to his perceived hostility to the private sector and willingness to return to 'tax and
spend' policies.

Corbyn
How Corbyn Came To Power
?
By 2015, there was significant pressure from the left of the Labour Party to take a more
radical approach, with some party members attributing them being nearly wiped out in
Scotland by the SNP to the party being insufficiently left wing.
?
?
This was the background to the victory of Jeremy Corbyn, a staunchly socialist
backbencher, in the September 2015 leadership election following Miliband's resignation.
Due to his left wing agenda, Corbyn struggled to form a united shadow cabinet and
opposing MPs forced a 2nd leadership contest in the autumn of 2016, which he won
triumphantly.
?
Never before had there been such a party divide in the Labour Party between MPs who
favoured a more cautious, centrist approach and its grassroots (including Momentum), who
approved of Corbyn's unconventional, anti-establishment and left-wing style.
How Corbyn's Return To Old Labour Policies Failed
?
Corbyn moved the Labour Party to the left to a significant extent under the slogan 'for the
many not the few', reembracing the policies of Old Labour.
?
Corbyn and his Chancellor John Mcdonnell long defined themselves as socialist, as
did many of the new MPs elected in 2017 and much of the wider membership.
?
Corbyn marginally lost in the 2017 election and was destroyed in the 2019 election, with
Labour having their worst electoral performance since 1935, representing a decisive
rejection of Corbyn's left-wing manifesto and himself as a weak leader who had
inadequately handled the rise of antisemitism in the Labour Party since he came to power.

How Starmer Came To Power
?
Following the 2019 election defeat, Keir Starmer was elected as Labour leader instead of
the more left-wing, Corbynite candidate Rebecca Long-Bailey.
How Starmer Gradually Rejected Corbynism And Moved The Party Closer To New
Labour Again
?
Recognising that Corbyn's left wing programme was conclusively rejected by the electorate
in 2019, Starmer has progressively moved the Labour Party closer to the centre ground
since coming to power.
?
Following Partygate, the failure of Liz Truss' administration and general
dissatisfaction with the Tories, Starmer's Labour Party took advantage of the
Conservative Party's unpopularity and won a landslide in the 2024 election with
centre-left programme that promised not to raise income tax, national insurance and
corporation tax and promised to increase defence spending.
?
Starmer also suspended Corbyn from the Labour Party in October 2020, after the UK's
Human Rights Watchdog concluded that Labour broke the law by failing to stamp out
antisemitism in the party when Corbyn was leader, and dealt with antisemitism in the party
a lot more effectively and decisively.
Starmer's Increased Party Discipline:
?
In the past couple of years, as Labour's poll ratings and Starmer's stature has grown, he
has imposed strong party discipline, by forcing MPs to publicly support and defend party
policy and taking decisive action against those on the left of the party who don't.
?
In November 2023, Starmer ordered Labour MPs to abstain from voting on a motion
calling for a ceasefire in Gaza. Defying this order, 56 Labour MPs voted in favor of
the ceasefire, including 10 frontbenchers. These frontbenchers, including high
profile MP Jess Phillips, were forced to resign as a result.
?
Starmer's intent on reducing the power of left-wing elements within the party can also be
seen in 2023, when the party leadership sought to deselect several left-wing MPs, including
Sam Tarry, Apsana Begum, and Ian Byrne, from standing as Labour MPs at the next
election.
?
Corbyn's deselection as a Labour Party candidate for the next general election,
announced in July 2023, also clearly showed Starmer's intent to distance the party
from its reputation under Corbyn.
¦
This move followed the earlier decision to suspend Corbyn from the party
due to his reaction to the report on antisemitism by the Equality and Human
Rights Commission. Corbyn denied complicity in the clear rise in
antisemitism in the party under his leadership.
?
In July 2024, just after winning the 2024 election, Starmer suspended 7 Labour MPs from
the party for 6 months in response to them voting against the government on an
amendment to scrap the two-child benefit cap.

The Policies Of The Labour Party Under New Labour
Economic Policy
?
New Labour aimed to separate themselves from earlier social democratic administrations
by not increasing taxes and conserving resources before investing more in key public
services.
?
A major turning point was in 2002, when increases in National Insurance
contributions led to the largest ever rise in spending on the NHS.
?
New Labour governments aimed to reduce poverty, but didn't make wiping out inequality a
priority.
?
For example, they introduced a national minimum wage, but at a less generous
level (£3.60 per hour) than trade unions wanted.
?
They were also happy for individuals to get rich through hard work, emphasising
social justice and equality of opportunity rather than equality of outcome.
?
New Labour supported capitalism, privatisation and the use of Private Finance Initiatives
(PFIs), which used the private sector in the provision of public services, such as in building
new schools and hospitals.
Law and Order Policy
?
Blair famously said that Labour must be 'tough on crime and tough on the causes of crime',
setting out his policy of punishing criminal behaviour, whilst continuing to tackle poor social
conditions.
?
Blair imposed conditions on the receipt of welfare benefits and brought in ASBOs to
deal with anti-social behaviour.
?
?
Some of Labour's measures to fight terrorism promoted the safety of the community over
human rights.
New Labour broadly encouraged and facilitated immigration, seeing it as economically
beneficial, and net migration to the UK quadrupled to around 200,000 per year when they
were in office.

Welfare Policy
?
Labour consistently increased spending on the NHS and education.
?
Between 1997 and 2007, the core spend per pupil rose 48% and there were record
results in schools. When asked his 3 most important priorities in 1997, Blair said
'education, education, education'.
?
Under New Labour, there were the shortest waiting times in NHS history.
?
?
?
The winter fuel payment was introduced for pensioners and there was free off peak travel
for over 65s.
They introduced the minimum wage and the Working Families Tax Credit as part of policies
aimed at promoting work.
New Labour attempted to get people into work through welfare-to-work programmes and
increasing means testing of benefits to ensure that the benefits system was a "hand-up, not
a handout".
Foreign Policy
?
Tony Blair's premiership became largely defined by foreign policy, especially his decision to
support George Bush's invasion of Iraq in 2003.
?
New Labour also doubled foreign aid spending when in power.
Other Policy
?
New Labour also introduced a comprehensive set of constitutional reforms aimed at
modernising and democratising politics in the United Kingdom.
?
This included devolution, reforming the House of Lords, introducing the Supreme
Court and passing the 1998 Human Rights Act.
?
New Labour also sought to improve female representation in parliament through all-female
shortlists.
Overall
?
Overall, New Labour moved significantly away from Old Labour policies and were more
centrist, allowing them to get the support of many middle class voters.
They shouldn't be seen as very left wing, but they did combine prioritising national finances
with significant spending on the NHS, education and other services, as well as significant
constitutional reforms.
?

The Policies Of The Labour Party Under Corbyn
Economic Policy
?
In 2019 especially, Corbyn pledged to significantly increase taxes by introducing a new 50%
tax rate for those earning over £125,000 and raising corporation tax from 21% to 26%.
In 2019, Labour also pledged 6 big nationalisations: rail, mail, water, energy, broadband
relevant parts of BT and ending Private Finance Initiatives.
?
?
This would've led to an over £200B increase in the assets held by the public sector.
Law and Order Policy
?
Corbyn opposed cuts to police numbers, finding ground with Blairites, as he believed it
jeopardised public safety.
?
But did focus more on dealing with poverty and inequality, rejecting New Labour's
tough stance on crime.
?
?
In 2019 and 2017, Labour also pledged to end immigration targets.
Corbyn's premiership was marred by accusations of being close to terrorist groups in the
past.
Welfare Policy
?
?
Corbyn strongly opposed benefit cuts and also pledge in 2019 to replace universal credit.
He also opposed the use of the private sector to deliver public services, a central aspect of

In 2019, Labour also pledged an average annual increase of 4.3% in overall health
and social care spending.
?
?
In 2019, Labour also pledged to freeze the state pension age at 66.
In 2017 and 2019, Labour pledged to scrap tuition fees, end the charitable status of private
schools and introduce a lifelong adult learning service.
Foreign Policy
?
As a backbencher, Corbyn was generally anti-capitalist and anti-Western and consistently
voted against the use of force, wanted the UK out of NATO's military structure and wanted

Labour's 2024 manifesto emphasised fiscal responsibility and providing a stable economic

Law and Order Policy
Crime:
Like New Labour in 1997, Labour's 2024 manifesto pledged to increase police numbers to tackle
crime, whilst also seeking to tackle the causes of crime by preventing reoffending.
?
?
?
In their 2024 manifesto, Labour pledged to recruit 13,000 extra neighbourhood police and
PCSOs, showing a strong approach to tackling crime.
The manifesto also pledged to crack down on anti-social behaviour and recreational drug
use, which it said hits communities hard and leads to more serious offending.
The manifesto also pledged to tackle the prisons overcrowding crisis by building more
prisons and preventing them from becoming breeding grounds for more reoffending.
?
Labour pledged to provide prisoners and prison leavers with purposeful activity,
such as learning, so that they are helped into work and don't fall back into crime.
?
Labour also pledged to halve violence against women and girls in a decade by fast-tracking
rape cases and relentlessly targetting sexual predators using tactics normally reserved for
terrorists and organised crime, including covert operations and electronic tagging.
Immigration:
The Labour Party's 2024 manifesto wasn't supportive of more immigration, but pledged to make
the asylum system work and secure Britain's borders to stop illegal migration. Since coming to
power, they have shifted to the right on immigration.

After winning the election, Labour scrapped the Conservative Party's policy of handling the
claims of asylum seekers in Rwanda. They saw the approach as an inhumane gimmick and
a waste of money.
In January 2025, however, the Government published the Border Security, Asylum and
Immigration Bill. This landmark legislation introduces counter terror style powers to "smash
the people smuggling gangs" including measures to seize and search phones, expand
detention powers, and create new offences against gangs selling small boats parts.
?
Further, in February 2025, the Government announced that it would make it
impossible for a refugee who arrives in the UK on a small boat to become a British
citizen, which attracted significant criticism from the Refugee Council and some
Labour MPs.
?
Together, this shows a shift in Labour Party Policy towards a stricter, more right wing
approach to immigration, having previously based their immigration policy around a
firm opposition to the Illegal Migration Act and the Safety of Rwanda Act, which they
scrapped after winning the 2024 election.

The Spring Statement Welfare Cuts As An Example Of The Labour Party Being More Aligned
With New Labour Than Old Labour:
?
?
?
In late March 2025, Chancellor of the Exchequer Rachel Reeves made her Spring
Statement, something that was not meant to be a 'fiscal event', but turned into one, with
mounting pressures on the government to do something about stagnant growth and wide
dissatisfaction with previous economic policies.
The Statement was very much geared to a more centrist ideology, even arguably
centre-right, as was the case with New Labour policy.
?
This shows how public attitudes can influence governments to shift their ideological
positions, as growing support for Reform UK was damaging Labour in the polls.
The most significant announcements in the Spring Statement were in relation to welfare,
including major cuts to the welfare budget, particularly for those who are disabled, that will
mean the number of people in poverty will rise to 14.5 million.
?
?
?
Elligibility for Personal Independence Payment (the UK's key disability benefit) will
be tightened to mean that fewer people will be able to access it.
The amount that those who can't work (due to disability/long term sickness) will
receive in Universal Credit will also be reduced from 2026/27.
Reeves also announced £1 billion to help people back into work and £400 million for
job centres.
?
This clearly shows dealignment from Old Labour's commitment to the redistribution of
wealth and greater alignment with New Labour, who emphasised getting people back into
work and preventing people from 'exploiting' (the vast majority weren't actually!) the welfare
system when they could work.

Renters' Rights Bill:
?
The Renters' Rights Bill was introduced to Parliament in September 2024. Key provisions of
the bill include the abolition of Section 21 'no-fault' evictions to improve tenant security, and
the prohibition of landlords demanding large upfront rent payments.
?
Additionally, the bill seeks to cap rent increases to once per year, ensuring they
align with market rates, and grants tenants the right to request permission for pets,
which landlords cannot unreasonably refuse.
?
These measures reflect the Labour Party's commitment to social welfare and government
intervention in housing, reminiscent of 'Old Labour' principles advocating for greater tenant
protections and equitable housing policies.
Foreign Policy
Labour's foreign policy is focused on strongly supporting NATO and Britain's allies and significant
defence spending. It is therefore closely alligned with the Conservative Party and a broadly centrist
approach that seeks to differentiate itself from Corbyn.
?
Labour's 2024 manifesto pledged to increase defence spending to 2.5% when possible,
exceeding NATO's minimum requirement of 2%. It also strongly supported Trident, Britain's
nuclear deterrent.

After Trump indicated a reduced commitment to Ukraine's defence in February
