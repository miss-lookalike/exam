Introduction To Pressure Groups
What Are Pressure Groups?
?
Pressure groups are groups that seek to influence politics in order to achieve particular
goals. They don't seek political office, but seek to change policy through influencing public
opinion, parliament and the government
?
?
They are usually focused on one particular area of policy.
Pressure groups are crucial to a pluralist democracy in which many different groups and
sections of the population hold power and can influence politics.
Why Do People Join Pressure Groups?
?
Pressure groups are often funded by a membership, which feels as if they would benefit
from the activities of the pressure group.
?
This can be because they feel strongly about a particular political cause and want to get
involved in causing change.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
2


 
?
It can also be because they have something to gain from joining the pressure group, such
as the legal representation and job protection that comes from unions.
The Different Types Of Pressure Group
Causal/Sectional/Social Movements
Causal/Promotional Groups
?
Causal/Promotional Groups are focused on achieving a particular goal or drawing attention
to a particular issue, with membership usually open to anyone who sympathises with the
cause.
?
For example, Greenpeace promotes awareness over environmental concerns and
tries to influence the government to adopt 'green' causes.
?
?
Many causal groups are also charities, such as Oxfam.
A special category is one that stands up for a group in society without a voice and can be
membered by anyone. For example, most members of homeless pressure groups aren't
homeless but sympathise with their cause.
Sectional/Interest Groups
?
?
Sectional/Interest groups promote the interests of a particular group in society.
For example, trade unions represent their members in negotiations with employers over
wages and working conditions.
?
Membership is often limited to people with a certain qualification/expertise, e.g. the Law
Society is open to solicitors in England and Wales.
Social Movements
?
?
?
Social movements are similar to causal groups but more loosely structured. Some people
may belong to other pressure groups while others may just take part in a specific protest or
be motivated by a specific issue.
Social movements are usually politically radical and seek to achieve a single objective. The
growth of social media facilitates them, as people can connect and organise very quickly
and effectively.

Insider/Outsider
Insider Groups
?
Insider pressure groups rely on close contacts with ministers and civil servants to achieve
their aims. Some (e.g. the National Union of Farmers with DEFRA under any Conservative
Party Government) have close links with relevant government departments and most
insider groups' objectives are generally in line with the views of the government, making it
more likely for their views to be listened to
?
?
?
Some are low profile and rely on discreet behind the scenes contacts rather than seeking
publicity.
Others are high profile and supplement their lobbying with the use of the media to make
their case.
They are very dependent on their contacts within and the political leanings of the
government of the day- can quickly become outsider groups if the political situation
changes.
Outsider Groups
?
?
Outsider pressure groups are pressure groups that are not consulted by the government.
Their objectives are often so far from the political mainstream and radical (e.g. animal rights
protestors who try to intimidate animal testing laboratories into ceasing their work) that the
government is unlikely to talk to them.
Alternatively, for some groups, talking to those in power may taint their reputation and
ideological purity.
?
For example, the anti-capitalist 'Occupy London' movement, which camped outside
St. Paul's Cathedral in late 2011, saw the government as closely aligned to the
global capitalist movement against which they were protesting.
?
Recent examples include 'Just Stop Oil', which have held various disruptive public
demonstrations and protests by farmers and the National Farmers Union in 2024 and 2025
over Rachel Reeves scrapping Agricultural Property Relief, meaning inheritance tax of 20%
will now be applied to farms valued over £1 million.

How Groups Can Switch Between Insider and Outsider Status ?
?
Some groups move between insider and outsider status according to changing political
circumstances and the political leanings of the party in power.
?
For example, trade unions enjoyed privileged access and influence in the post-war era,
especially with Labour governments, but when the Thatcher government came to power in
1979, union leaders were deliberately excluded from the corridors of power and made the
enemy of the people.
The National Farmers Union As An Example Of Moving Between Insider And Outsider Status
As A Pressure Group:
?
The NFU under the last Conservative government was able to wield significant policy
influence, owing to their close ties with DEFRA and thfor example, e Tory Party.
?
For example, in 2024 Prime Minister Rishi Sunak attended their annual conference
and pledged to protect British Farming in the agricultural market - leading to the UK
suspending their trade talks with Canada after they refused to loosen food
standards. This benefited the 55,000 farmers the union represents.
?
However, since the Labour Government has been in office, their influence over policy has
declined, possibly due to Labour performing less well in rural seats and the countryside
being less important to their identity as a party. In the 2024 Budget, Chancellor Racheal
Reeves introduced a tax of 20% on inherited farming assets over £1 million - president of
the NFU Tom Bradshaw has criticised this for leading to the "death of the family farm".
?
The group has launched a campaign to "overturn the family farm tax", their
e-petition has received over 200,000 signatures, but has had little impact as their
aims lack alignment with government direction.
?
This shows how a pressure group's status (insider/outsider), and alignment with
government direction, is crucial to allow them to exert influence over policy, and they are
ultimately highly dependent on the government of the day.
Pressure Group Methods
Insider Methods
?
Insider groups tend to lobby the government/parliamentarians and negotiate quietly behind
the scenes, using their private contacts in Whitehall.
?
They may be given the opportunity to give their views and specialist advice or draft
legislation.
?
For example, The National Farmers Union was able to give Conservative
governments (specifically the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs)
specialised knowledge in return for influence over policy.
?
Insider pressure groups also lobby MPs, by briefing them on issues of concern or giving
evidence to select committees. For example, lawyers acting for the human rights group
'
Liberty' have done this in order to put their views across on counterterrorism policies that
affect people's civil liberties.
Outsider groups also do this.
?
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/

Outsider Methods
?
?
Outsider pressure groups typically use less discrete methods to gain publicity and therefore
draw attention to their concerns and gain public and political support.
They may try to exercise influence through social media campaigns and petitions or stage
demonstrations and publicity stunts.
?
For example, demonstrators from Just Stop Oil staged protests throughout 2022
and 2023, including blocking roads and glueing themselves to football posts in the
middle of games.
?
Direct action is not always peaceful, for example some of those who took part in the 2010
student demonstrations against tuition fees were prosecuted for disorderly conduct.
Well funded pressure groups initiate legal challenges against policies they disapprove of.
Trade unions go on strike as a key outsider method to cause disruption and negotiate pay
increases as a result
?
?
How The Public Order Act Was Passed To Ban Certain Outsider Pressure Group Methods:
?
The Public Order Act, which received Royal Assent on May 2, 2023, was enacted in
response to the UK government's concerns over highly disruptive protest tactics employed
by groups like Extinction Rebellion and Just Stop Oil.
?
The Act introduced new offences such as "locking-on" (attaching oneself to objects
or other people to cause serious disruption) and interfering with national
infrastructure (including the roads around Parliament), which are punishable by
imprisonment or unlimited fines.
?
This legislation was designed specifically to limit the effectiveness of pressure groups that
use disruptive tactics to draw attention to their causes, by imposing stricter penalties and
giving law enforcement new tools to manage and respond to protests.
?
On October 30th 2023, the Metropolitan Police swiftly intervened in a Just Stop Oil
slow march' around Parliament Square, arresting over 60 activists within minutes
under the new legislation.
The law, specifically section 7, prohibits activities interfering with the use of
'
¦
key national infrastructure, which in this case included the roads around
significant governmental buildings.
?
In March 2025, the Public Order Act was used to prevent farmers protesting against
inheritance tax changes from bringing tractors to their protests in Westminster and
blocking the roads.
?
This has been criticised for restricting the right to protest in disruptive ways, which is often
what makes protest successful.

How Pressure Groups May Use Both
?
?
Some groups use both insider and outsider methods, often depending on the nature of the
issue and the degree to which the government is willing to respond.
For example, on 19th of November 2024, the National Farmers Union (NFU) organised a
protest with over 13,000 farmers outside of Westminster, against inheritance tax changes in
the 2024 budget. Chancellor Rachel Reeves scrapped Agricultural Property Relief,
meaning inheritance tax of 20% will now be applied to farms valued over £1 million.
?
Additionally, the NFU organised a "mass lobby" event where thousands of farmers
met 150 MPs in London and pushed the same message to Parliamentarians.
?
Despite the large turnout and celebrity endorsement from key figures such as Jeremy
Clarkson, the Government has refused to backtrack on these changes and the treasury has
refused to engage with the NFU.
?
This shows how pressure groups can use both insider and outsider methods - in
2023 the NFU had consultancy ties with DEFRA, and had key successes such as
the suspension of the UK-Canada trade agreement to protect British agriculture.
However they now have less power under a Labour government and have resorted
to outsider methods which utilise mass participation.

Stonewall - Insider Pressure Group Success Under New Labour
?
Stonewall is a charity and pressure group in the UK that campaigns for LGBT rights. It was
formed and was an outsider group in 1989 in opposition to Section 28 of the 1988 Local
Government Act introduced by Thatcher's government, which prohibited the 'promotion of
homosexuality' by local authorities.
?
?
When New Labour came into power in 1997, it became an insider pressure group and used
its insider contacts and favour to play a key role in policy development. It also used legal
challenges in the European Court of Human Rights to challenge existing legislation.
As a result of these methods, they successfully caused a number of very important legal
changes.
?
?
?
For example, they challenged the banning of homosexuality in the armed forces,
which was lifted by the government in 2000 after a successful case in the European
Court of Human Rights (despite the High Court and Court of Appeal previously
opposing lifting the ban).
They were also involved in legislative achievements including equalising the age of
consent to 16 years old as part of the Sexual Offences Act 2000, for which the
Parliaments Acts 1911 and 1949 had to be invoked to overrule House of Lords
opposition.
Another key success was the introduction of the Civil Partnership Act 2004.
?
In April 2022, Stonewall, among other LGBT charities, boycotted the Conservative
government's first global LGBT conference over its decision to exclude trans people from a
ban on conversion therapy. It is now an outsider group as a result of government policy.

?
?
Care4Calais is a charity that delivers aid to refugees in Calais and other areas affected by
the European migrant crisis, and campaigns for refugee rights in the UK.
?
It is an outsider, cause pressure group that uses legal challenges as a key strategy,
The group has been key in fighting legal challenges on behalf of migrants in the UK
threatened with deportation to Rwanda following a deal between the UK and Rwandan
governments.

Along with other human rights campaigners, it had a notable success on 14 June 2022
when the first planned deportation flight to Rwanda was emptied one by one due to

Different courts have judged differently in relation to the legality of the deal between

Liberty - Failure Under The Conservative Party Governments That Undermined The
Right To Protest In The UK
?
?
Liberty is a pressure group in the UK that campaigns to protect civil liberties and protect
human rights in the UK, through the courts, parliament and wider campaigns to gain public
support. It aims to engender a "rights culture" within British society.
Liberty failed to prevent the Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act from being passed in
2021, which they saw as limiting the right to protest in the UK.
?
Liberty organised widespread protests under the slogan #KillTheBill, and is a very
experienced organisation, but was nonetheless unable to stop the bill being passed,
due to sections of the public opposing disruptive protests by Just Stop Oil which the
bill was in response to, and the fact that the government was intent on passing it.
?
Liberty was also unable to prevent the passage of the Public Order Act 2023 under Sunak's
government, which introduced new offences such as "locking-on" (attaching oneself to
objects or other people to cause serious disruption) and interfering with national
infrastructure (including the roads around Parliament) in order to prevent disruptive protest
in the UK.
?
Liberty lobbied Parliamentarians and sought to raise public awareness about the
act, including through arranging demonstrations, however failed to prevent the
passage of the law.
?
It has also failed to convince the new Labour Government to repeal the law and in
March 2025, the Public Order Act was used to prevent farmers protesting against
inheritance tax changes from bringing tractors to their protests in Westminster and
blocking the roads.

Other Pressure Group Examples
Marcus Rashford's Campaign For Free School Meals - Success In Causing Policy
U-Turn Due To Having A Celebrity Advocate And Generating Public Support
?
?
Footballer Marcus Rashford has been the figurehead of a number of successful outsider
pressure group campaigns in relation to child poverty.
In the summer of 2020, Rashford teamed up with Fairshare and put significant public
pressure on the UK government to reverse its decision to not extend free school meals to
children during the summer holidays, through open letters, e-petitions and his social media
accounts which generated a social movement and major public support.
His campaign was successful, with the government reversing its decision on 16 June 2020,
just a day after Rashford wrote a powerful open letter to the government urging it to do so.
This shows the importance of clear leadership, having public support and celebrity
endorsements for outsider pressure groups.

Protests For A Ceasefire In Gaza - Example Of A Social Movement Causing Some
Policy Change
?
On the 2nd of September 2024 the Labour Government announced its plan to suspend
some of the arms export licences to Israel, after receiving legal advice showing a risk of
Israel breaching international law.
?
This follows mounting political pressure on the Labour Party to address the humanitarian
crisis in Gaza. Following the 2024 General Election, multiple shadow ministers such as
Jonathan Ashworth lost their seats to 5 newly elected pro-Gaza independents, who were
elected on calls to end arms sales to Israel.
?
Additionally, throughout 2024 there were regular protests for a ceasefire in Gaza in
London, with over 100,000 attendees, organised by the Palestine Solidarity
Campaign.
?
This is a crucial example of an outsider social movement having an impact on the policies
of the UK government, by demonstrating public attitudes towards an issue. This also shows
how Pressure Groups can utilise social media to mobilise public opinion behind their cause,
allowing them to generate momentum and publicity - even if they don't have a large
membership or capital resources.

RMT Strikes
- Success Of A Trade Union Using Industrial Action
?
From the Winter of 2022/23, there were major strikes by the National Union of Rail,
Maritime and Transport Workers (RMT) who work on the railways, to demand a real terms
pay increase and protest about the increasing profits of rail companies.
?
The RMT is an outsider, sectional pressure group
?
?
They caused significant disruption but also were supported by significant sections of the
public, in part due to the successful media management and leadership of General
Secretary Mick Lynch.
After a year and a half of strikes, they accepted a pay deal of 9% over 2 years with Network
Rail, demonstrating the success of outsider methods used by trade unions.

?
This suggests that government alignment is the most significant factor in pressure
group success, rather than membership or outsider methods.

The Muslim Vote as an Example Of Pressure Groups Enhancing Democracy
?
?
The Muslim Vote is a Political Social Movement, a form of pressure group, which aims to
mobilise Muslim Voters through social media in order to shape political outcomes.
They focus on seats where there is a high Muslim population, in order to inform voters on
how to elect a pro-Gaza Independent candidate.
?
Through Social Media they have accumulated 20 million monthly visitors, who they
have encouraged to register to vote and influence politics democratically.
In the 2024 General Election, all 5 pro-Ceasefire independents elected were
endorsed by The Muslim Vote, and 1/3 of their endorsed candidates won or came
second.
?
?
This shows how pressure groups are crucial to a healthy democracy, as they are able to
ensure many voters turn out and participate in political processes. Pressure Groups play a
key role in increasing public awareness and education within the electorate, enhancing the
quality of participation.
The Supreme Court Ruling In For Women Scotland Ltd v The Scottish Ministers
(2025) As An Example Of Pressure Group Success:
?
On the 16th of April 2025, the UK Supreme Court ruled that the legal definition of a 'woman'
in the Equality Act (2010) was based on biological sex in For Women Scotland Ltd v The
Scottish Ministers (2025).
?
Pressure group ForWomenScotland challenged the Scottish Government's implementation
of the Gender Representation on Public Boards (Scotland) Act (2018).
?
They argued that the legislation was unlawful as it changed the meaning of "sex"
outlined in the Equality Act (2010) by including transgender people with Gender
Recognition Certificates in their definition of sex.
?
The role of the court was to clarify the interpretation of the words "sex" and "woman"
in the Equality Act (2010).
?
The court ruled the Scottish Parliament did not have the legal authority to redefine terms in
a UK wide law.
?
This is a good example of how pressure groups can utilise Judicial Review to
achieve their aims and influence how legislation is applied, using different access
points in the UK political system in comparison to just influencing the UK
government.
Debates Over Pressure Groups
Reasons For Pressure Groups Success
How Success Varies Considerably And Is Difficult To Judge
?
It is often hard to judge success for pressure groups. Some outsider groups may consider
publicity a success, while it is difficult to gauge the success of insider groups as they often
don't boast about their success as to not offend their government contacts.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
13


 
?
The government ultimately makes the final decision as well and is unlikely to admit
any outside influence on it.
?
?
For some pressure groups, their goals are so large that they may have to build public and
political support for years before achieving them.
Much of success depends upon the climate of public opinion and the willingness of the
government to make concessions, as well as how effective methods of communication are.
Resources
?
?
?
?
A large membership who pay subscriptions allow a large pressure group to run offices, pay
staff and organise publicity; therefore gaining significant influence.
For example, the RSPCA employs around 1600 people, supported by 1000s of volunteers
and can afford to spend a lot on advertising.
For trade unions also, a bigger membership that is more important to the economy will have
a higher likelihood of influencing policy through industrial action.
The size of membership can also be significant in persuading the government that it
represents a significant portion of the population.
Strategy and Leadership
?
Experienced, capable leadership is essential to the success of a pressure group.
?
For example, the RSPCA played a key role in securing a ban on hunting with dogs
in 2004 by collaborating with two similar groups -The League Against Cruel Sports
and the International Fund for Animal Welfare - to stop them competing.
?
For insider pressure groups, a key factor is knowing which 'access points' in the UK
government to target in order to achieve your goals, whether that be lobbying the
government directly, getting a member of parliament to instigate a Private Members' Bill,
trying to influence local government and devolved bodies first or initiating legal
challenges/using outsider methods.
?
For outsider pressure groups, capable leadership and tactics are important to ensure the
public is kept on side and effective methods are used to gain media attention and influence
the government.
?
For example, Marcus Rashford was an effective leader and public figurehead in his
successful 2020 campaign to extend free school meals to school holidays.
By contrast, Just Stop Oil lacked an effective strategy and leader.
?
Public Support
?
Pressure groups who represent a large portion of public opinion are generally more
successful, as are those who use peaceful and respectable methods to not alienate
sympathisers.
?
The government is much more likely to change policy if it is more likely to get them
elected as it is supported by significant sections of the public, as not doing so will
alienate them from voters at the next election.
?
?
The Snowdrop campaign to ban the use of handguns in the UK, for example, was
successful largely due to public horror over the 1996 Dunblane Primary School massacre.
Favourable media coverage can be important, as can the involvement of a well known
personality, as shown by Marcus Rashford's success in overturning the decision on free
school meals.

Government Attitudes
?
Governments will usually listen to the groups on which it relies for specific expertise and
those it can find common ground with, leading to insider groups often having more success
than outsider groups.
?
Insider contacts with ministers and contacts are often crucial to success. For
example, the National Farmers Union's links to DEFRA were instrumental in
bringing about the 2013 badger cull.
?
?
The willingness of a government to u-turn and change policy is also important, especially
for outsider groups.
Pressure groups are also more likely to have success when a government is weak and has
a small majority, as maintaining parliamentary and public support will be more difficult and
important for them.
Other Influences On Government And Parliament
Think Tanks
What Are Think Tanks?
?
Think Tanks are groups of experts who are brought together to investigate topics and offer
solutions to complicated, economic, social and political issues.
Unlike the civil service, they have political leanings and often have strong connections to
political parties.
?
?
Right Wing think tanks include the Adam Smith Institute and Centre for Social
Justice whilst Left Wing think tanks include the Fabian Society and Institute for
Public policy.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/

How Do Think Tanks Have Influence?
?
Think tanks are an alternative source of ideas to the civil service, with more time and
expertise than parties to carry out research and come up with policy solutions which are
sometimes adopted.
?
The Institute for Fiscal Studies was cited 150 times in government policy documents
between 2015 and 2021.
?
They ultimately have to be aligned with government thinking to have any real impact
on policy, though. Also unlikely to majorly shift policy in a direction the government
wouldn't have done so anyway.
?
Whilst they do have expertise, they have also been criticised for being filled with young
people seeking to use it as a springboard for a political career and for lacking the academic
rigour of university research and suggesting impractical, idealistic policy as a consequence.
?
David Milliband, for example, worked for the Institute for Public Policy Research
(IPPR) at the start of his career

Concerns Over Think Tank Funding
?
There are significant concerns about the role think tanks play in politics and democracy,
due to the fact that their source of funding is often not transparent and they have the
potential to allow wealthy individuals to exert unfair influence over policy.
?
Think tanks often influence policy through their recommendations, however their
funding is often not entirely transparent, whilst it is clear that individuals with certain
policy goals give money to think tanks to try and achieve their aims, raising
concerns around democracy.
?
The Tony Blair Institute has been criticised for accepting money from foreign governments,
whilst right wing think tanks including the Institute of Economic Affairs (IEA) and Policy
Exchange have been criticised for accepting large sums of money from right wing American
backers.
Lobbyists
What Are Lobbyists?
?
Lobbyists are members of professional organisations paid by clients (organisations,
companies and individuals) seeking access to and influence over government, MPs and
members of the House of Lords. Their purpose is to gain influence on behalf of their clients
and prevent/promote legislation damaging/promoting their interests.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
17


 
?
It is an extension of the long-established principle that members of the public may
lobby their MPs in person or by letter.
?
There is unease about lobbying as some don't like the idea of wealthy individuals and
corporations gaining influence as they can afford lobbyists' fees, with concerns over
corruption as well.
?
The UK Government claims lobbying has no impact on government policy, but if this
was the case there wouldn't be such a large lobbying industry.
?
?
Attention has been put on the issue by undercover journalists posing as lobbyists to entrap
MPs with financial rewards or offers. The parliamentary code of conduct strictly bars MPs
from accepting money for agreeing to represent a viewpoint.
In 2014, the government made it a legal requirement for anyone lobbying on behalf of a 3rd
party to register if their activities include discussing policy, legislation or government
contracts with a minister or senior civil servant.
New Parliamentary Standards Rules On Lobbying:
?
In March 2023, the UK Parliament introduced new standards rules regarding lobbying to
enhance transparency and regulate MPs' conduct more stringently.
?
Key updates include an outright ban on MPs providing paid parliamentary advice,
consultancy, or strategy services related to lobbying.
?
?
Additionally, MPs are now required to have a written contract for any outside work explicitly
stating that their duties do not include lobbying activities. The rules also tighten the criteria
for registering and declaring financial interests and interactions with lobbyists.
In October 2024, the Labour Government tightened rules on what MPs can do as second
jobs. The Leader of the House Lucy Powell said the changes would remove a conflict of
attention, and that MPs main priorities should be with their constituents.
?
The changes involve restricting what MPs can do as second jobs, by removing
exemptions which allowed them to provide advisory roles to employers on Public
Policy, Current Affairs, and Parliament.
?
This is part of a broader move by the Labour Government to crackdown on lobbying and
consultancy work amongst MPs, as well as fine ministers who breach rules.
How Do Lobbyists Have Influence?
?
Lobbying is big in the UK; over 100 firms employing 4000 people with a total of £2 billion
spent on it each year, however the extent to which lobbying influences the government is
unclear.
?
This is much smaller than the USA, though where it is thought the lobbying industry
controls $3.5 trillion of government spending.
?
They seek to influence government policy by meeting with parliamentarians, government
ministers and civil servants and convincing them to adopt policies that are in their
company/client's interest.
?
This may be convincing them it is the best policy for the government and economy,
showing how important their client/company is to the economy or hinting at future
job prospects for MPs/ministers after leaving politics.
?
Many lobbying firms and individual companies also hire former politicians to lobby on their
behalf, due to the importance of their contacts and friends in Westminster.
?
Due to this, there are concerns about the 'revolving door' process and that 'who you
know' is very important to exercising power in the UK, furthering the influence of
those with privilege and connections.

Scott Benton Lobbying Scandal:
?
Scott Benton, former Conservative MP for Blackpool South, was caught in an undercover
sting operation by The Times in late 2023, where he was filmed offering to lobby on behalf
of a fake gambling company.
?
During the meetings, which were secretly filmed, Benton proposed to ask

suggesting he could do so in exchange for payments of up to £4,000 a month.

In early 2024, the scandal led to Benton being stripped of the Conservative party whip and

He resigned before the end of the recall petition period, triggering a by-election.
Penny Mordaunt Joining British American Tobacco (BAT) As An Example Of The 'Revolving
Door':
?
In April 2025, former Conservative cabinet minister Penny Mordaunt took up a part-time
paid role with British American Tobacco. For years, BAT have lobbied the government in the
interests of tobacco, and with Mordaunt having been out of office for just 5 months this can
very easily be seen as an example of the 'revolving door'.
?
This is where personnel move between roles as legislators in the public sector, and
as employees of industries (affected by state legislation) in the private sector.
?
?
It is also notable that Mordaunt had previously abstained on a free vote on Sunak's gradual
smoking ban last year, and was in charge of the government's legislative programme when
the bill did not make it through Parliament before the election.
The Advisory Committee on Business Appointments said the risk her role would be seen as
"a reward for [her] decisions in office" was "limited" and that she had not had access to
specific information that would "offer an unfair advantage to British American Tobacco."
?
?
However, it also said "there is a risk your former role as a cabinet minister will be
seen to offer British American Tobacco unfair access to and influence within
government."
This guidance demonstrates the issues with the 'revolving door' in politics and the
risk of the influence of lobbyists, which allow those with money to have greater
influence over politics than the rest of the population.

Corporations
?
Corporations, who often hire lobbyists to work on their behalf, can have significant influence
on the government due to their importance to the UK economy and jobs in the UK.
The government may ask them for advice to see how new policies may affect their industry,
whilst corporations will lobby the government to ensure policy helps their business,
especially if they are threatened by foreign competition.
?
?
One of the ways they can do this is by threatening relocation to a different country
and therefore taking jobs and economic activity out of the UK. This can also force
governments to bailout corporations in financial trouble.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
21


 
¦
During COVID, for example, £7.5 billion in loans was given out by the Bank
of England to help corporations and companies struggling as a result of
lockdowns, in the Covid Corporate Financing Facility.
?
?
In the UK, corporations aren't allowed to campaign for candidates or parties during
elections, though they did campaign during the Brexit referendum. This was mostly to
remain, though some companies such as Wetherspoons and Dyson campaigned to leave.
Corporations' interests are also represented by pressure groups, notably the CBI
(Confederation of British Industry).

Private telecom companies played a crucial role in this, as the government's
strategy largely relies on private investment to build the necessary infrastructure.
Industry stakeholders, including these telecom companies, have been actively
involved in discussions about the government's broadband targets and were able to
influence the policies through their investment decisions and capabilities.

?
They have also been successful in advocating for policy reforms to facilitate infrastructure
development, such as easing access to land and ensuring new homes are built with gigabit
broadband installed.
This shows how corporations can have influence on policy if they form an important part of
the economy and the government relies on them as part of the private sector.
