The Press
How does the press play a role in politics and how is it biassed?
?
The oldest form of media is newspapers, which used to be how the majority of people
consumed politics, but has declined in importance in recent decades due to the increasing
importance of digital and online media.
?
Unlike other forms of media, newspapers have no obligation to be neutral and have
different political biases. Overall, there is a right-wing bias across the British press. The
Sun, Daily Telegraph, Times, Daily Mail and Daily Express are more right wing and support
the Conservatives, whilst only the Daily Mirror and Guardian support Labour, with the
Financial Times and Independent more in the middle.

How significant is the press in politics?
Arguments that the press is significant:
?
Around 7 million people read newspapers daily in the UK, which is a key source of political
information and views, particularly for older voters who are more likely to vote. Further,
many people read newspapers online and newspaper journalists are highly important in
political coverage on the TV, radio and social media.
?
As newspapers present a biassed view, newspapers can be seen to have a big impact
changing people's opinions and influencing how they vote. The Conservatives, who have
won the last 4 elections, have done so with the support of the majority of the press.
Arguments that the press is insignificant:
?
Newspaper circulation has declined significantly in recent years as many voters move to
other forms of media. In 2024 the Daily Mail had a circulation of 736,000, compared to
1.85M in 2012 and 2.4M in 1997.
?
It seems unlikely that the influence of the press causes people to change their voting
behaviour and it's best to be sceptical about claims that newspapers have decided the
outcome of elections. In fact, most people read papers that broadly reflect their outlook, so
papers usually confirm their readers' existing political views.

TV and Radio
How do TV and Radio play a role in politics and how is it supposed to be unbiased?
?
Many people consume political news through TV and the radio. For most channels and
particularly for the BBC, politics coverage is supposed to be balanced and neutral. More
recently, there has been the growth of other TV channels and radio stations which are
biased, such as GB News, LBC (for example James O'Brien's strongly Remain supporting
show) and Talk Radio.
?
TV is important in the media coverage of elections, with TV debates and interviews an
important fixture in campaigns and party political broadcasts.
?
Parties are allocated agreed amounts of airtime for their election broadcasts, based
on their voting share in the last contest and the number of constituencies they're
contesting.

How ITV's Reporting Of The Post Office Scandal Forced Politicians To Address It:
?
The Post Office Horizon scandal, which received significant public attention in 2024, shows
the crucial role of the media in influencing public opinion and holding politicians
accountable between elections.
?
?
The scandal, involving wrongful convictions of numerous Post Office operators
based on flawed software, had received limited coverage under the surface until it
was thrust into the limelight by a compelling ITV documentary and subsequent
detailed investigations by BBC's Newsnight.
It had received previous attention and there was an ongoing public enquiry since
2020, but it was the media and consequent public pressure that really created the
change.
?
This media spotlight escalated public awareness and concern, which in turn pressured
politicians to take action. As a result of the programme:
?
The government announced a new and improved compensation scheme for the
wrongfully convicted sub-postmasters. This included not only compensations for the
financial losses suffered but also for the personal distress and reputational damage.
A number of postmasters launched successful appeals against their convictions,
which saw many of them being overturned.
?
?
The government pursued a full public enquiry and set up the Post Office and
Horizon Committee to do so.
?
This shows how investigative journalism and media coverage are pivotal in holding
politicians to account and representing minorities and underrepresented groups between
elections; therefore being important to a pluralist democracy.

Social Media and the Internet
How do social media and the internet play a role in politics?
?
By 2015, social media began to play an important role in politics and elections especially,
with parties learning from the success of Obama in the 2012 presidential election. For
example, in 2015, the Conservatives reportedly spent £100,000 a month on Facebook
advertising.
?
Social media and the internet aren't subject to the same neutrality rules as TV and the radio
and can be highly partisan, with news sites such as The Canary (very left wing and
Corbynite) and the Spectator (more right wing, especially culturally) gaining significant
traction.
?
In the 2019 and 2024 election, the Conservative Party even rebranded their Twitter
account during the TV debates to pretend to be an independent fact checker.
?
Online advertising on social media is a problem as it is difficult to track where money is
being spent and whether it is within the legal limits, especially targeted advertising. It is also
easy for non-party supporters to spend a great deal without declaring.
?
Parties can run political adverts all the time on social media, including on election
day which they are unable to do on TV or the radio. In the 2024 election, over £1M
was spent on Google and Meta ads on the election day alone.

Opinion Polls
How do opinion polls play a role in politics?
?
Polls run by firms such as Ipsos MORI and YouGov aim to gauge the popularity of parties
by asking a sample of people how they intend to vote. They also ask the public more
detailed questions about their opinion of the party leaders and their policies.
They have become an integral part of election campaigns, with parties looking at their
findings and also conducting their own polls.
Parties also use opinion polling to shape their policies by gauging the popularity of different
policies. Focus groups are also important in this.
Exit polls are often the first indication of the election result.
?
?
?
Examples of opinion polls and their accuracy:
?
In 1992, they failed to predict Major's narrow victory, instead the majority of polls predicted
either a narrow labour victory or a hung parliament. The exit poll was even wrong that year,
predicting a hung parliament.
?
Some suggested this was because polls early in the campaign had shown Labour
winning, leading to a late swing to the Cons. It was also suggested that the results
had been swung by 'shy Tories', which is why firms made more use of telephone
polling thereafter.
?
They were wrong again in 2015. They predicted Labour being wiped out by the SNP in
015, but predicted Labour and the Conservatives would both have about 34% of the vote
in England, in reality the Conservatives had about 36.9% and Labour 30.4%.
2
?
An inquiry found the polling firms had not surveyed a representative selection of the
nation's voters, not interviewing enough retired people and interviewing too many
politically engaged young people who were atypical for their age.

Arguments that the media has little impact on elections:
?
?
?
The impact of the media during elections can be overstated. Voters don't simply follow what

?
In November 2024, Secretary of State for Transport Louise Haigh resigned after news
reports emerged of a fraud conviction in 2010. Haigh told Starmer about this conviction in
2020 when she was appointed to shadow cabinet, however she only resigned after the
Times and Sky News revealed details of it.
?
?
This has been criticised as despite Starmer knowing about the criminal conviction
and the context surrounding it, he chose to appoint her to cabinet anyway and only
fired her after they were revealed by the press.
This shows the impact of the press and their ability to hold the government to
account by exposing scandals.
?
This is also an example of IMR - Haigh has been accused of breaking the ministerial code
which states ministers must act with openness, honesty and integrity.
?
However, it highlights the weakness of IMR as it is not enforced consistently and
depends on political circumstances and if it benefits the Government.

This not only suggests that the UK continues to have a democratic deficit despite

Does The Media Have A Positive Impact On Democracy/Politics?
Arguments that the media has a positive impact on democracy/politics:
?
A free media is a vital feature of a healthy democracy and can play an essential role in
holding governments to account and highlighting key political issues, especially when
parliamentary opposition is weak.
?
?
With the growth of social media, the media can now be seen as more democratised, with
many more individuals able to have a voice and participate in politics.
TV and radio coverage is less biassed than newspapers. The BBC Charter insists upon
political neutrality and this is by and large followed by other channels.
Arguments that the media has a negative impact on democracy/politics
?
Large sections of the media present an oversimplified interpretation of political issues and
focus too much on leaders, personalities and images rather than policy. This is influenced
by the fact they are driven by getting as many clicks as possible.
?
For example, The Sun began supporting Labour but switched to Conservative in the
mid-70s, as Thatcher's approach to Trade Unions was in line with owner Rupert
Murdoch's business interests. In the run up to the 1997 election, it switched to
Labour, as Blair showed business had nothing to fear under him, but it switched
back to the Conservatives in 2010 after repudiating Brown.
?
?
Newspapers are notoriously partisan and will alter their allegiance in response to changing
circumstances as much as to any ideological loyalties.
Newspaper owners and other important media figures are primarily interested in boosting
their circulation figures and can't be held to account in the same way politicians can.

Governments have been increasingly making important policy announcements in TV

Examples That Suggest The Media Has A Positive Impact On Democracy
Telegraph Leaks Matt Hancock's Whatsapp Messages:
?
In March 2023, The Telegraph published over 100,000 leaked WhatsApp messages from
Matt Hancock, the former UK Health Secretary, which revealed details about the
government's handling of the COVID-19 pandemic.
?
These messages, given to The Telegraph by journalist Isabel Oakeshott, exposed
several controversial decisions and discussions within the government, including the
suggestion that face masks were mandated in schools not based on public health
advice but rather to avoid political disagreements with Scottish authorities.
?
The Telegraph's decision to publish these messages demonstrates that right-wing media
can act independently of the government, prioritising public interest and journalistic integrity
over political allegiance. This shows the role of the media in holding the government
accountable, regardless of its political leanings.
Examples That Suggest The Media Has A Negative Impact On Democracy

Elon Musk, the owner of social media platform X (formerly known as twitter) was criticised

Since this emerged, Phillips has reported numerous threats to her life and further

?
This shows the dangers of social media to democracy, as it can be used by foreign

?
This is particularly stark given the fact that two MPs have been killed by their

?
Braverman's team invited journalists from right-leaning publications including The
Daily Mail and The Telegraph and excluded outlets like The Guardian and The
Independent, known for their critical perspectives on government policies,
particularly those concerning human rights and immigration.

The Speaker Criticises The Chancellor For Briefing The Budget To The Media:
?
In October 2024, the Speaker of the House of Commons, Sir Lindsay Hoyle, criticised the
Chancellor Rachel Reeves on the floor of the House, accusing her of prematurely
disclosing Budget details to the media.
?
He said it was unacceptable to make major policy announcements to the Press
without first giving the information to MPs via formal ministerial statement.
Reeves was accused of departing from convention and bypassing the traditional
parliamentary protocols that key legislative announcements must be made to the
Parliament.
?
?
This followed the Chancellor confirming in broadcast interviews whilst attending the IMF
meeting in Washington, that she would rewrite the Government's "fiscal rules" around how
debt is measured.
?

democracy and Parliament in particular. Governments have been making
increasingly important policy announcements in TV studios rather than the House of
Commons.

undermines Parliamentary sovereignty, when they are elected to scrutinise and

Rupert Murdoch Meets With Rishi Sunak 5 Times In A 12-Month Period In 2022-23

GBNews' Lack Of Impartiality
?
The rise of right wing TV channel GBNews, particularly its shows hosted by sitting MP Lee
Anderson and former UKIP and Brexit Party leader Nigel Farage, challenges the impartiality
of broadcast media and shows the ineffectiveness of Ofcom.
?
Traditionally, broadcast media in the UK are bound by Ofcom's stringent impartiality rules,
which aim to ensure balanced coverage and prevent any single viewpoint from dominating.
?
?
However, Anderson and Farage are both paid hundreds of thousands of pounds to
host weekly shows that are very clearly promoting right wing policies and
viewpoints, especially in relation to immigration.
They are able to do so as GBNews claims that these shows aren't 'news' which
helps them get around the Ofcom rules.
?
This has led to criticism of Ofcom's ability to enforce its impartiality and standards rules
effectively. When prominent and politically active figures are given platforms to consistently
express partisan views, it calls into question whether the regulator can truly maintain the
fair and balanced broadcasting landscape that is vital for a healthy democracy.
