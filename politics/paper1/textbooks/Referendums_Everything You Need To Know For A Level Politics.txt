Introduction To Referendums In The UK
What Is A Referendum?
?
?
?
A referendum is a popular vote on a particular issue, usually requiring a yes/no vote. It's an
example of direct democracy within a representative system.
Unlike in other countries, there is no constitutional mechanism requiring a Prime Minister to
hold a referendum, instead they are called at the discretion of the government.
Technically, the result doesn't have legal force and it has to be approved by parliament,
which has legal sovereignty. In practice, though, it is unlikely that elected representatives
would ignore the popular sovereignty of the people.
?
Sometimes, such as in the EU referendum, it is purely advisory with Parliament
having to take action following it to implement the result.
?
In other instances, such as the Alternative Vote referendum, the law passed to
instigate the referendum includes provisions for the result's immediate
implementation following the referendum.

011 Alternative Vote Referendum
?
The Alternative Vote referendum was held in May 2011, after it was included as a provision
in the Conservative-Liberal Democrat coalition agreement.
?
The Liberal Democrats, Scottish National Party, Plaid Cymru and other smaller parties
campaigned for the 'Yes' side, the Labour Party had no official party position (Though most

Labour MPs supported the 'No' campaign), whilst the Conservative Party and Democratic
Unionist Party campaigned for the 'No' campaign.
?
The majority of the press (including most of the right-wing papers) supported the
No' campaign, whilst The Guardian, Daily Mirror, Financial Times and The
Independent supported the 'Yes' campaign.
'
?
?
The result was a 68% No vote on a 42.2% turnout.
The 'Yes' campaign argued they were representing the British public, whilst the 'No'
campaign used the unpopularity of the Lib Dems, which is why the rejection of AV can be
seen as in part a protest vote against them.
?
In the 2010 election, the Lib Dems gained a lot of support (especially from young
people) due to their policy of scrapping university tuition fees. After going into
government with the Conservatives, though, they ended up agreeing to increase
tuition fees instead, causing a public outcry and a huge loss of support.
?
The loss of AV in the referendum after this (which the Lib Dems campaigned for)
can be seen as in part a protest vote against them, rather than a vote on the specific
issue itself. This can be seen as negative, as it's a vote on a specific political context
rather than the important constitutional issue that is on the ballot paper.

014 Scottish Independence Referendum
?
The Scottish Independence Referendum was held in Scotland only in September 2014
following The Scottish Independence Referendum Act 2013, which followed an agreement
between the Scottish and UK Governments.
?
The reaching of this agreement reflected the growing popularity and electoral
success of the Scottish National Party (SNP) in recent general elections and
Scottish Parliament elections.
¦
This can be used as an example for the argument that referendums are
positive for democracy, as they are sometimes called due to public pressure.
?
All European Union (EU) or Commonwealth citizens residing in Scotland aged 16 or
over could vote.
?
'Yes Scotland', backed by the SNP and the Scottish Greens, was the campaign
organisation backing independence. 'Better Together', backed by The Conservative Party,
Labour Party and Liberal Democrats, was the campaign organisation supporting Scotland
remaining in the UK.
?
?
The result was a 55.3% No vote on a 84.6% turnout.
Key issues in the campaign were further devolution, what currency Scotland would use, EU
membership, Trident and North Sea Oil.
?
Polling reveals that retaining the pound was the key factor for those who voted 'No', whilst
disaffection with Westminster politics was the deciding factor in those who voted 'Yes'.

016 Brexit Referendum
The Referendum Itself:
?
?
The United Kingdom European Union Membership Referendum was held across the UK
and in Gibraltar in June 2016, after the Conservative Party had made holding it a manifesto
pledge in the 2015 election.
The referendum was called in response to the growing popularity of the United
Kingdom Independence Party (UKIP) and its prominent leader Nigel Farage, which was
part of growing pressure for a referendum since the late 2000s.
?
The Brexit supporting UKIP won 26.6% of the vote and was the largest party in the
2014 European Parliament election.
?
The Remain campaign, led by 'Britain Stronger in Europe', was backed by the Prime
Minister David Cameron and the majority of the Labour Party, Conservative Party, SNP and
Lib Dems. The Leave campaign, led by 'Vote Leave', was fronted by Conservative MPs
Boris Johnson and Michael Gove and backed by UKIP, with Farage playing a prominent
role. Dominic Cummings was key in running the campaign.
?
Each campaign was given the right to spend up to £7,000,000 and given £600,000
in public funds.
?
76% of MPs supported remain.
?
?
The result was surprising and not predicted by opinion polls, with a 52% Leave vote on a
72.2% turnout.
Key issues in the campaign were immigration, national sovereignty, the economy and the
cost of membership payments to the EU.
?
The campaign slogan 'Take back Control' was important to the Leave Campaign's
victory.
The Aftermath of the Referendum:
?
Following the referendum, Cameron resigned as Prime Minister and was replaced by
Theresa May. The result led to the Conservative Party shifting significantly to the right, with
those who supported Brexit gaining control, as seen in Boris Johnson becoming party
leader and Prime Minister in June 2019.

Key Issues In Relation To The Use Of Referendums In The UK
How Are Referendums Regulated?
?
Since the 2000 Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act, the conduct of
referendums has been regulated by the Electoral Commission.
This independent body is responsible for checking the wording of referendum questions, to
ensure they are as objective as possible.
?
?
For example, in 2016, the government had originally proposed to ask: "Should the
UK remain a member of the EU?". However the Electoral Commission considered it
to be insufficiently neutral and insisted the ballot paper should present two options:
"Should the UK remain a member of the EU or leave the EU?".
?
?
The Commission also manages campaign expenditure, registers groups or individuals who
spend more than £10,000 and designates one approved 'lead campaign organisation' on
each side.
?
In the EU Referendum, these two groups were 'Vote Leave' and 'Britain Stronger in
Europe'. This designation entitled them to receive a predetermined amount of public
funding.
They also produce a report following the referendum on the campaign and spending.
Why Have Referendums Been Held?
To Give Legitimacy To Key Constitutional Changes And Major Government
Initiatives
?
?
Since Blair came into power, it has become the accepted practice to secure a
demonstration of public support before embarking on important, possibly irreversible
constitutional changes.
?
Referendums give constitutional changes legitimacy and ensure they're
unlikely to be reversed.
Major developments, such as devolution for Northern Ireland, Scotland and Wales have
been given referendums, as was possibly the most far-reaching change to the constitution

in decades; leaving the EU, which almost certainly wouldn't have been approved by
parliament without a public vote.
?
Local referendums, especially on directly elected mayors, are more to give legitimacy to
government initiatives.
?
There was little public pressure/support for directly elected mayors and further
devolution in England, with the public not really caring, as seen in the low turnouts
in these referendums.
Due To Party Motivations
?
Referendums have sometimes been held when a government faces serious internal
disagreement. By handing the decision to the people and insisting colleagues have to rally
behind the popular verdict, the government can maintain its unity.
?
This can be seen in the 1975 referendum on EEC membership and the 2016 Brexit
referendum. In both of these, Collective Ministerial Responsibility (CMR) was
suspended, allowing ministers to publicly oppose government policy.
?
The 2016 Brexit referendum was also held in part due to the growing pressure on the
Conservative Party from UKIP, which was threatening it electorally by taking away its
voters.
?
Pledging to hold the referendum in the 2015 election campaign enabled the
Conservatives to maintain the support of many important voters.
As A Result Of A Deal Between Political Parties
?
Cameron agreed to hold a vote on changing the electoral system for Westminster (The AV
Referendum) because it was a demand of the Liberal Democrats for agreeing to the May
2010 coalition agreement.
Due To Pressure From The Public and Parliament
?
Cameron initially didn't want to hold an in/out EU referendum, joining forces with the Lib
Dems and Labour in October 2011 to defeat Conservative backbenchers who were
pressing for a referendum.
?
He changed his mind in January 2013 as the public demand refused to go away, and he
began to fear the possible loss of Conservative votes to UKIP if he didn't concede.
?
By announcing he would hold a referendum if re-elected in 2015, he took the issue
off of the agenda in the ensuing GE and after the election he had to make good on
his promise.
?
The 2014 Scottish Independence Referendum was held in large part due to the growing
popularity of the SNP and polling showing significant support for independence among the
Scottish population.

Debates Over Referendums In The UK
The Impact Of Referendums On UK Political Life
?
?
Referendums have had a profound impact on UK political life.
They are a form of direct democracy, which conflicts with Parliamentary Sovereignty and
representative democracy.
?
This can be seen in the EU referendum, where the vast majority of Parliament
supported Remain, whilst the majority of the electorate voted to leave, leading to
complications in the passage of Brexit through parliament following the vote.
?
?
Recent referendums have high turnouts, reflecting how they have engaged the population
in politics when there is increasing dissatisfaction with the political class in Westminster.
The Scottish Independence Referendum and Brexit Referendums in particular have been
deeply divisive and both profoundly changed and defined the normal politics of
representative democracy in the UK.
Arguments For/Against Referendums
Arguments In Favour of Referendums
Referendums Involve The People In Key Decisions, Including Between Elections, Therefore
Improving Democracy:
?
Referendums directly involve the people in decision making on important issues. In a
democracy the electorate has the right to be consulted. Trust in politicians is low and many
issues are too important to leave to them. The virtue of a referendum is that it enables a
single issue to be isolated, so that an unambiguous popular verdict can be given, especially
when an issue cuts across party lines so a real choice can't be given in a General Election.
?
Referendums are often called due to public pressure and can reorient the politics of
representative democracy to issues important to the population, as could be argued
with the fallout of the Brexit referendum.
?
The holding of referendums between General Elections gives the people an opportunity to
have their say more frequently, preventing the government from becoming remote and
unaccountable. They can be seen as a check on 'elective dictatorships'.

Referendums Can Give Legitimacy To Key Reforms and Entrench Them:
?
By demonstrating clear public support for a change, referendums settle arguments and
entrench reforms, as in the Good Friday Agreement. This isn't to say a change could never
be reversed by a future parliament, but to do so would entail another major public debate.
Referendums Increase Political Awareness:
?
Referendums raise voters' political awareness.
?
For example, the Scottish Independence referendum in 2014 has been praised for
giving an opportunity to air a wide range of issues related to independence, such as
the impact on the economy and the future of Trident.
?
The two most recent referendums received high turnouts, much higher than turnouts in
general elections.
Referendums Are Well Regulated:
?
The conduct of referendums has been subject to independent supervision by the Electoral
Commission since 2000. This reduces the chances that the result will be skewed as a result
of unfair influence, because expenditure on both sides is limited and the wording of the
question is subject to review by an independent body.
Arguments Against Referendums
Referendums Challenge Parliamentary Sovereignty And The Working Of Representative
Democracy In The UK:
?
Referendums are a challenge to parliamentary sovereignty, voters elect representatives to
take decisions on their behalf and this was the accepted way of doing things until the
1970s.
?
It can be argued that ordinary people lack the expertise to make decisions on complex
issues (which are often presented as simple by referendum questions and campaigns) and
can be easily misled by campaigns and political leaders.
?
?
In 2016, the Electoral Commission reported that the arguments used by the leaders
of both campaigns included a degree of distortion, and that there should be a
greater regulation of referendum campaigns to ensure people receive a fair
presentation of the arguments.
For example, the likely future level and negative impact of EU immigration to the UK
was overstated by the Leave campaign, whilst they also lied by saying that leaving
the EU would free up £350M per week to fund the NHS.
?
Referendums can also be extremely divisive and be seen as negatively impacting the
working of representative democracy.
?
This can be seen in Brexit dividing the Conservative Party and dominating politics in
the UK for the four years following the referendum, slowing progress and focus on
other important issues.
Popular Participation Is Often Low:
?
If the arguments aren't clearly explained to the public and the referendum is on an issue
they care little about, popular participation may be low.
?
Low turnout has been the norm, with the EU Referendum in 2016 and the Scottish
Independence Referendum in 2014 being outstanding exceptions. This limits the legitimacy
of the decision, such as after the <50% turnout in the 1997 Welsh devolution vote, which
cast a shadow over the new Assembly for some time.

Referendums Are Often Held For Party Political Purposes:
?
Governments choose when to hold referendums, often for their own political purposes,
such as defusing opposition and overcoming their own differences. There is no consistency
in when they're called and when they aren't.
?
This can be seen as damaging to democracy, with referendums being called due to
a party's interest rather than in the public interest.
?
There is an argument for greater regulation of the circumstances in which a referendum can
be triggered.
Results Can Be Influenced By Factors Other Than The Referendum Issue:
?
The outcome of referendums can be influenced by factors that have nothing to do with the
subject being put to the electorate. They can be a way of protesting against the government
or political class of the day.
?
This can be seen especially in the Brexit Referendum and the AV Referendum.
?
As a result, highly important and consequential political changes can be made without
proper consideration/support.
Potential Reforms To Referendums
Greater Use Of Referendums:
?
As is the case in Switzerland, there could be much greater use of referendums in the UK so
that the people are consulted more often on specific issues and play a more active role in
politics in the UK.
Greater Thresholds For Constitutional Changes:
?
The threshold for referendums causing significant constitutional changes could be
increased in order to ensure they are only made with significant support and are effectively
entrenched.
?
?
It could be required for there to be a consensus, through the use of a 55% threshold for
change rather than a simple majority, or through a turnout threshold.
There could also be 2 stage referendums, agreed before the first is held, to check that the
public still support the reform once the specific structure of it/deal is worked out.
Regulating When Referendums Can Be Called:
?
An Act could be introduced by Parliament to regulate when referendums can be called, so
that it limits them being used for party political reasons.
?
This could include a provision for referendums being held if, for example, enough
UK citizens sign an e-petition.
