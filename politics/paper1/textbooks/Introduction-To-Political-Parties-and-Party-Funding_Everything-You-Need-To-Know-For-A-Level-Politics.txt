Introduction To Political Parties And Party Systems
What Are Political Parties And What Roles Do They Play?
What Are Political Parties and How Are They Distinct From Pressure Groups?
?
A political party is a group of people drawn together by a broad ideology, even if they don't
have identical views. Most parties aspire to form a government and enact a range of policy
commitments.
?
Some parties, such as UKIP, are mainly focused around a single issue, but they
normally develop a broader set of policies to grow their supporter base.
?
Pressure groups are not the same as parties. They often represent a particular secular
group or interest (e.g. the environment) and try to influence parties and politics, but they do
not enter their own candidates at elections.
What Roles Do Political Parties Play?
?
?
Parties represent the views of people with a certain set of common beliefs.
Parties encourage political participation among the public through voting, joining parties
and supporting them through funding and spreading the message.
?
Party members can usually select candidates to stand in local and national elections
and play a role in choosing the party leader.
?
Parties recruit future politicians and leaders.
?
Parties develop candidates through the political skills they learn as campaigners
and organisers. Parties also have the right to deselect candidates who fail to live up
to expectations.
¦
For example, Keir Starmer decided Jeremy Corbyn would be unable to run
as a Labour Party candidate in the 2024 election

?
?
Parties also formulate policies to deliver on the ideas they stand for.
?
In an election these all come together in a manifesto setting out their programme for
government. Parties can also be said to have an educative function, by
communicating and explaining their ideas to the public.
Parties who stand in elections provide governments and run the country if they are
successful.
?
They control parliamentary business, with the aim of implementing the policies in
their manifesto by passing legislation.
?
The Political Spectrum ?
What is the political spectrum?
?
The political spectrum is a way of measuring and describing the overall policy positions of
different political parties.
?
Different parties adopt different parts of the political spectrum, therefore offering
voters a wider range of choice at elections.
?
Traditionally, the key division between the Labour and Conservative parties was on the
left-right dimension, with the Labour Party left-wing and the Conservative Party right-wing.
?
It is still the case that Labour are more left wing and the Conservatives more right
wing, but in 2019 the Conservative Party shifted significantly to the centre
economically and gained a lot of voters through 'cultural' politics that may be
described as 'authoritarian', especially Brexit.
?
Further, in 2024, Labour shifted back to the centre on economic policy in particular
from the left wing stance that had been adopted by Corbyn.
¦
Politics in the UK is no longer as simply defined by left-right politics as it
used to be.
Some Key Principles Of Left-Wing Parties
?
Desiring change, reform and alteration to the way that society operates.
?
?
In favour of big government and a large welfare state.
High Taxes, high public spending.
?
?
Includes Socialists who are critical of capitalism and free market economies.
Have tended, in history, to be on the 'right side' of social issues.
Some Key Principles Of Right-Wing Parties
?
?
?
Supporting the conservation of the status quo and little or no change.
Stressing the importance of order stability, hierarchy and private property.
In favour of small government, individualism and self determination.
?
Lower taxes, lower public spending.

What Types Of Political Parties Are There?
Mainstream/Major Political Parties
?
These are parties that run candidates in all constituencies, develop policies in all areas of
policy and realistically attempt to form a government.
?
?
The Conservative Party and Labour Party are the two most important of these.
After their success in the 2024 election, it now seems reasonable to describe The Liberal
Democrats as a major/mainstream party again, though they could also still be described as
a minor party.
Minority Parties
?
Minor parties also stand candidates in elections, but have very little chance of winning
themselves. They may be able to enter government as a minor partner in a coalition
government/confidence and supply arrangement, though.
?
Some of these parties (such as Reform UK or the Green Party) stand candidates in
the vast majority of constituencies, whilst some only stand candidates in
constituencies in a certain part of the UK (such as the Democratic Unionist Party).
?
Two key types of minor parties are nationalist parties and single issue parties.
?
Nationalist parties, such as the Scottish National Party (SNP), Plaid Cymru and Sinn
Fein, only stand candidates in a part of the UK and campaign for the
independence/self-rule of that part of the UK.
?
Single issue parties, such as the Green Party, UKIP and the Brexit Party are mainly
focused on campaigning for one particular issue/cause.
¦
These parties are very similar to pressure groups and could be described as
pressure groups that use the electoral process to seek to achieve and draw
attention to their aims.

Holding party conferences.
Carrying out research and developing policies and manifestos.
Employing special advisers and full time members of staff.

How Are Parties Currently Funded?
Membership Subscriptions
?
Members of political parties pay monthly membership subscriptions.
?
Labour Party members pay £5.71 per month and there are currently around
370,000.
?
Conservative Party members pay £3.25 per month (a recent increase) and there are
an estimated 100,000 members, though they don't publish these numbers.
¦
In 2023, the party received £1.5M from membership fees.
?
?
Scottish National Party members pay as little as £1 per month and there are
currently around 64,000 members, a drop of 18,000 from in 2022 following their
party funding scandal.
Liberal Democrat members pay as little as £1.25 per month and there are currently
around 86,000 members.
?
The overall decline in party membership in the past few decades has harmed party
finances.
Trade Unions (Labour Party)
?
The Labour Party receives significant funding from trade unions, which charge members a
political levy as well as membership fees, which can be used to support the political aims of
the trade union.
?
The Labour Party has a number of affiliated unions which give the Labour significant
funds, including UNISON, UNITE and GMB.
?
?
In 2023, The Labour Party received £5,900,000 from trade union affiliations, around 10% of
their total income.
?
Their contributions are now dwarfed by donations from individuals and companies.
The 2016 Trade Union Act, passed by the Conservatives, harmed Labour Party funding
from Trade Unions.
?
?
It obliged new trade union members to choose whether they wanted to opt into
making payments towards political influence, rather than automatically being opted
in as they were previously.
Labour intend to repeal this act through their Employment Rights Bill in 2025.

Local Constituency Fundraising
?
Political parties also receive a small amount of funding from local constituency fundraising
through events such as raffles and dinners. This funding is then often spent on local
campaigns.
?
In 2021, for example, the Labour Party received £200,000 from fundraising.
Public Funding
?
Political parties also receive some public funding in order to support their activities. The
vast majority of this is available to the opposition, as they lack the support of the civil
service.
?
'Short Money' forms the vast majority of this and is intended to support parties to
carry out parliamentary business, including policy research. It is primarily used to
cover the salaries of researchers and advisers in the central offices of parties.
¦
It is only available to opposition parties that have won either two seats in the
House of Commons at the previous general election, or one seat and more
than 150,000 votes in total from all seats contested.
?
There is also 'Cranbourne Money', which supports the opposition in the House of
Lords, as well as funding for the Office of the leader of the opposition and Policy
Development Grants.
?
?
In 2023/24, The Labour Party received £7,689,924 from Short Money, around 13% of their
total income.
In 2023/24 the main Political Parties received the following amounts of Short Money:

?
?
Labour Party - £7,689,924.
Scottish National Party - £1,301,552.
Liberal Democrats - £1,052,703.
The DUP - £229,297.
The Green Party - £211,892.
Donations
?
Political parties receive a significant proportion of their funding through donations from
businesses, organisations and rich individuals. There are no limits on how much can be
donated to political parties/politicians.

The Labour Party receives key donations from Trade Unions, but since becoming

Debates and Issues In Relation To Party Funding In The UK
The 2000 Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act
?
?
In an attempt to overcome the perception that party funding was undemocratic, the Blair
government passed the 2000 Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act.
An independent Electoral Commission was established to supervise election campaign
spending by parties.
?
Donations from individuals/businesses who weren't on the UK electoral roll (i.e.
foreign companies and individuals) were banned.
¦
It is the job of political parties themselves to ensure their donations/loans
come from permissible sources and to report to the Electoral Commission
any that don't.
?
Requirements for reporting donations over a certain amount were also introduced.
Prior to the 2010 election, loans were also made subject to the same rules as
donations.
?
These are the current requirements for recording donations and loans:
?
?
?
Donations or loans over £7,500 to the central party must be reported to the Electoral
Commission.
Party sections whose finances aren't managed by the central party must report
donations over £1,500.
MPs must report loans and donations over £500 made in connection with their
activities as an MP to the parliamentary commissioner for standards.
Increased Campaign Spending Limits:
?
On 20 November 2023, the government increased campaign spending limits for political
parties by 80% to account for inflation since the limits were last set, raising the cap to £35
million.

?
This adjustment was implemented through a statutory instrument under the Political
Parties, Elections, and Referendums Act 2000 and was called the Representation of
the People Order 2023
?
A statutory instrument is a form of secondary legislation that does not require a full vote in
Parliament, thus allowing the government to make the change without extensive
parliamentary debate.
?
The method and the magnitude of the increase have raised concerns regarding
transparency and the potential for larger, better-funded parties to dominate electoral
campaigns as they can take full advantage and reach the cap.
Concerns Over Party Funding In The UK
?
There are major concerns over party funding in the UK, particularly in relation to the
reliance of parties on donations from rich individuals and businesses.
?
This is especially in relation to the Conservative Party, as the majority of their
funding comes from donations from big business and rich individuals.
?
There are worries that rich donors are able to buy political influence, which is highly
undemocratic, and there have been a number of scandals in relation to party funding
across a range of parties.
?
Both the Labour and Conservative Parties have been accused of offering places in
the House of Lords to donors.
Scandals In Relation To Party Funding In The UK
Cash For Peerages:
?
In 2006-07, there was a scandal concerning a possible link between individuals who had
loaned significant amounts to the the Labour Party and these individuals being nominated
by Prime Minister Tony Blair for life peerages in the House of Lords
?
The nominations were rejected by the House of Lords Appointment Commission
and there was suspicion peerages were promised in return for the loans.
It ultimately couldn't be proven this had been agreed in advance, though.
?
Bernie Ecclestone:
?
Within months of Blair becoming PM in 1997, he faced criticism following the revelation that
Bernie Ecclestone, a motor-racing boss, had donated £1 million to Labour.
?
It was alleged that there was a link between this and a delay in implementing a ban
on tobacco advertising in F1 racing.
?
Blair was forced to justify himself on TV and said the events weren't connected, but the
money was subsequently returned.

?
In 2005, businessman Michael Brown donated £2.4 million to the Lib Dems. A year later, he

?
There was pressure on the Lib Dems to return the donation, but they kept it after the

Lord Cruddas:
?
Billionaire Conservative Donor Lord Cruddas was made a peer in the House of Lords in

Just days after, he donated £500,000 to the Conservative Party, the largest amount

?
?
He was made a peer by Boris Johnson despite the fact that he failed the Appointments

The Conservative Party's Joint Biggest Ever Donor Frank Hester Makes Racist Comments Yet
They Still Accept His Money:
?
Frank Hester became the joint largest donor to the Conservative Party in recent history by
contributing £10 million in 2023. He made this substantial donation through two separate
contributions; £5 million personally in May and another £5 million via his company, The
Phoenix Partnership (TPP), in November.
?
Hester, the founder and CEO of TPP, a healthcare technology firm, has built a
significant fortune by providing digital healthcare solutions widely used across the
UK's NHS.
?
The company made £135 million from NHS contracts during COVID.
?
It emerged in March 2024 that Hester had racistly attacked MP Diane Abbott in a 2019
company meeting. He reportedly stated that seeing Diane Abbott made him "want to hate
all black women" and that "she should be shot."
?
Despite these severe remarks, the Conservative Party, led by Prime Minister Rishi
Sunak at the time, chose not to return his donation.
?
This decision sparked significant criticism and debate over who should be able to
have influence in British politics.
The SNP's Party Funding Scandal:
?
Starting in February 2023, The SNP faced a significant party funding scandal that
culminated in the resignation of First Minister Nicola Sturgeon and the arrest of her
husband and SNP chief executive Peter Murrell.
?
The controversy centered around allegations that approximately £600,000 raised
specifically for campaigning for a second Scottish independence referendum was instead
diverted to cover other regular party expenses.
?
The escalating situation ultimately contributed to Sturgeon's decision to step down
as party leader and First Minister, citing the need for new leadership amid the
ongoing scandal and other pressures.
?
The event contributed to reduced trust in the SNP and their decreasing popularity in
Scotland. Labour has capitalised on these SNP setbacks. Under the leadership of Anas
Sarwar, Labour now nearly matches the SNP in popularity, with recent polls showing them
just behind or nearly even in voting intention for the next general election in Scotland.
?
A YouGov poll projected that Labour could win 24 seats to the SNP's 27 in a general
election scenario.

Russian Influence In The Conservative Party
?
When Boris Johnson was in power, over £2 million of donations to the Conservative Party
were linked to Russia, raising major security concerns.
?
This included Evgeny Lebedev who was made a peer by Boris Johnson.
?
At the same time, the Conservative Party hasn't implemented any of the 21

Labour Accused of Cronyism Following Appointment of Donors:
?
The Labour Party has appointed several affiliated individuals to high profile jobs in the civil
service, including Former Party Donor Ian Corfield (who previously donated £20,000 to
Labour MPs) to a senior civil service role in the Treasury, bypassing the usual application
processes.
?
?
Emily Middleton was appointed to director general in the Department for Science,
Innovation and Technology (DSIT). She was a previous partner at consultancy firm Public
Digital who donated over £65,000 to the Secretary of State for DSIT, Peter Kyle.
Additionally, major Labour donor Lord Waheed Alli, who has donated over £500,000 to the
party, and £16,000 to Starmer for glasses was also given an unrestricted Downing Street
pass. This appointment was dubbed "passes for glasses" by much of the print media.
?
This has been widely criticised by Conservatives as Cronyism - accusing the
Government of threatening the impartiality of the civil service by appointing political
donors to public bodies.
?
This highlights issues with Party Funding in the UK, as party's beholden to private donors
rather than state funding, who can be seen as "buying influence" over policy. Furthermore,
scandals surrounding party finance harm trust in politics.
?
Labour's appointments have the power to attend meetings, greater access to
government and the ability to potentially influence the Government's priorities.
Labour Accused of Cronyism Due To Lord Alli and "Freebiegate":
?
In the summer of 2024, the UK Labour government was involved in a significant
controversy involving prominent donor Lord Waheed Alli, a media entrepreneur and Labour
peer who had donated over £500,000 to the party since 2020.
?
The scandal, dubbed "freebiegate," and "passes for glasses" revealed that Prime Minister
Keir Starmer had accepted substantial gifts from Alli, including clothing and eyewear valued
at over £16,000, some of which were not promptly declared.
?
Further scrutiny revealed that Starmer had accepted more than £107,000 in gifts
and hospitality since 2019, surpassing all other MPs.
?
Deputy Prime Minister Angela Rayner and Chancellor Rachel Reeves were also
found to have received significant gifts from Alli, including clothing and
accommodation.
?
?
This was particularly controversial, as following Labour's electoral victory in July 2024,
unelected Alli was granted a temporary security pass to Downing Street, which he used to
organise a post-election event and reportedly advise on public appointments-a project
referred to as "Operation Integrity."
These revelations led to allegations of cronyism, undermining public trust in politicians and
increasing opposition to parties being largely funded by donations, due to the accusation
that those with money can influence politics despite being unelected.

The Debate Over State Funding
How Would State Funding Of Political Parties Work?
?
Many democracies in the West have state funded political parties.
?
In Australia state funding supports parties' election campaigning, whilst in the
Netherlands it is intended to further research.
?
?
Whilst the UK does have some state funding, the majority of funds come from donations
and membership fees, and it could move to a largely state funded approach.
This approach could include state funding based on the number of votes in the previous
election, or match donations from members of the public, which is the case in Germany.
Arguments For State Funding Of Political Parties
?
Parties are key to representing the public and upholding effective democracy. They
therefore deserve effective funding.
?
State funding would also reduce the reliance of parties on funding from trade
unions, businesses and individuals, who have vested interests.
?
Public funding would remove the disparity in resources available to different-sized parties.
As a consequence the undemocratic two-party system would be challenged and pluralist
democracy would improve, through smaller parties and other voices having the funding to
fight elections effectively.
?
?
The state could match small donations by party members, which would improve the
participation of the public in politics and ensure those who are supported the most receive
the most funding.
Parties and politicians could focus more on representing the public and effectively
governing, rather than focusing on raising money, especially before elections.
?
It would also prevent parties trying to limit the funding of other political parties when
in power, such as the Conservative Party introducing the 2016 Trade Union Act and
Labour pledging in 2024 to remove it.
?
Crucially, it would curb the possibility of corruption and those who have the money to
donate to political parties having undue influence on policy/being able to get into positions
of power, which undermines democracy.
Arguments Against State Funding Of Political Parties
?
The limited public funding currently available ensures that parties are independent from the
state, and therefore the party currently in power, which can be seen as important in a
democracy.
?
?
?
As they would no longer need to seek financial support, it could isolate parties from the
wishes of the public and their members, which could be seen as harming democracy.
It would cost the state a significant amount per year and many taxpayers likely won't want
to fund parties they don't support, especially as they don't trust those in power.
If funding was linked to past performance in elections, then it would likely uphold the power
of existing parties and not further pluralist democracy.
