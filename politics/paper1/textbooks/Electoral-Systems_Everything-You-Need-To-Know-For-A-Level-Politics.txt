Criteria With Which To Judge Electoral Systems
How To Use The Criteria
?
?
?
In an exam, you may be asked to weigh up the merits of different electoral systems, decide
which you think is best, or evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of different systems.
When doing this, it is useful to consider different criteria for success with which to judge
electoral systems.
No electoral system will deliver on all of the criteria to the same degree. The question is
deciding which of them you consider to be the most important and which system you
believe delivers a balance of them the most effectively, and explaining why.

Voter Choice
?
?
One key way in which to judge electoral systems is how much choice is given to voters.
Many different factors come into this including:
?
How many votes voters get and whether they can vote for more than one
party/candidate.
?
?
How many candidates/parties are standing.
How much influence votes have on the outcome and whether this varies between
voters.
Representation and Proportionality
?
Perhaps the most important way that electoral systems should be judged is by how
representative/proportional they are, for what is the point of voting if it doesn't have an
impact on the outcome?
?
How proportional an electoral system is can be judged by the correlation between votes
cast and seats won. The closer the correlation, the more proportional the electoral system.
Link Between Representative and Represented
?
Another way to judge electoral systems is in relation to the strength of the link between the
representative and the represented and the extent to which the former is responsive to the
latter.
?
Different ways in which this could be analysed include the number of representatives per
voter and whether each representative represents a particular community, such as a
geographical community.
Type Of Government and Politics
?
?
Another wayelectoral systems can be judged is in relation to the type of politics they create.
This includes measures such as how cooperative parliament and governance is, as well as
how strong the government is and whether it is often a single party government.

Different Electoral Systems In Detail
First Past The Post (FPTP)
Where is FPTP used and how does it work?
?
FPTP is used in United Kingdom General Elections, local council elections in England and
Wales and mayoral elections in England.
?
Voters cast a single vote by placing a cross next to the preferred candidate in their
constituency.
?
There are 650 constituencies, each of which elects one Member of Parliament. The
average number of voters in a constituency is roughly 75,000, however there is
some variation.
?
?
?
It is a simple plurality system, with the candidate with the largest number of votes in a
constituency elected as MP, even if they don't win a majority of the votes cast.
The government is then formed by the party/number of parties (if it is a coalition) with a
majority of MPs in parliament.
UK General elections take place at the discretion of the Prime Minister. They do have to
take place every 5 years, but are often called more often than this.

Advantages
Speed and Simplicity:
?
FPTP is extremely easy to use for voters, who only have to select one candidate/party. The
result is also usually known early in the morning after polling day and the government is
quickly formed, with a swift and orderly transfer of power.
?
In 1997, for example, Tony Blair arrived at Downing Street as the new Prime
Minister at 1pm on the day after the election.
¦
In 2024, Starmer arrived at 12:40pm.
?
In 2024, the first constituency result (Sunderland South) was announced at 23:15 on
the day of the election and the result was clear by the early hours of the next
morning.
?
?
The simplicity and familiarity of FPTP can be seen as key factors in the public support for it,
as shown in the 2011 Alternative Vote (AV) referendum, in which 68% of those who voted
voted against changing the electoral system, on a 42% turnout.
Under a more proportional system where coalitions are more likely, forming a government
often takes longer as it has to follow negotiations between party leaders.
?
After the 2007 Scottish Parliament Election, for example, it took two weeks for the
SNP minority government to be sworn in, after failed coalition talks with the Lib
Dems.
Strong, Single Party Governments:
?
Another often cited advantage of FPTP is that it promotes a two party system that gives
voters a clear choice and usually results in a clear majority for one party, which then has a
strong mandate to carry out its programme of government and bring about effective
change. This clear choice also leads to governments being accountable, as there's a clear
alternative.
?
In the 1980s, this enabled Thatcher to bring about widespread changes to the
economy, whilst in 1997 it gave Blair's government the mandate to carry out
extensive constitutional reforms.
?
This is the case even though governments are almost always elected on less than
50% of the vote.

¦
In 2024, Starmer's Labour Party won a 174 seat majority with just 33.7% of
the popular vote, but there was limited public outrage and questioning of his
mandate.
?
Ken Clarke also argues FPTP forces voters to face up to the fundamental choice decisively,
as only the Conservatives and Labour can realistically win.
?
This can be seen in 2019, where the election was focused around Brexit and forced
many voters to prioritise Brexit over their other political views, with many former
Labour voters voting Conservative for the first time as the two main parties took
different sides on the key issue.
Exclusion of Extremist Parties:
?
Another benefit of FPTP is that it leads to the exclusion of extremist parties, who are much
less likely to have success, as success under FPTP requires geographically concentrated
support.
?
In 2010, for example, the extreme right wing British National Party (BNP) won 2% of
the national vote, but didn't finish higher than 3rd in any constituency.
In the 2009 European parliament elections, which used a proportional Closed Party
List system, by contrast, they won 6.2% of votes and 2 seats.
?
MP-Constituency Link:
? The relatively small size of most FPTP constituencies, and the fact that a single MP is
responsible for those who live within the constituency, result in effective representation of
local interests and a strong link between the constituency and MP.
?
On December 5, 2023, 22 Conservative MPs supported a Labour amendment
aimed at speeding up compensation for victims of the infected blood scandal,
rebelling against a 3 line conservative whip to do so.
¦
The amendment, which was successfully passed by a narrow majority, called
for the establishment of a body to administer compensation within three
months of passing the Victims and Prisoners Bill.
?
This is a good example of the strong MP-constituency link and local representation
under FPTP, as the rebels were influenced by strong local movements and victims
of the scandal in their constituency.
¦
For example, Conservative MP Caroline Nokes rebelled to represent the
local movement for greater compensation and the number of victims affected
in her Romsey and Southampton North Constituency.
?
MPs handle correspondence from their constituents, problems their constituents have and
hold weekly constituency surgeries.
?
A study by the House of Commons found that MPs handle approximately 300-500
emails per week, whilst it is estimated that MPs allocate around 40% of their time to
constituency matters, including surgeries and casework.
?
Jeremy Corbyn was reelected in 2024 despite being an independent candidate,
reflecting the fact that he had strong local support in Islington North having been the

Disadvantages
Lack of Representation In Constituencies:
?
Over a half of MPs typically don't command a majority of support within their constituency,
with more votes cast against a winning candidate than for them and even lower support
when turnout is taken into account.
?
In the 2024 General Election, 58% of voters ended up with an MP they didn't vote
for.
?
In the 2024 election, Labour candidate Terry Jermy was elected in South West
Norfolk with just 26.7% of the vote, which is just 15.7% of the constituency's
population if those who didn't vote are also counted.
?
This weakens the mandate of each MP, leading to many voters feeling unrepresented and
MPs lacking legitimacy.
Lack of Proportionality at the National Level:
?
This lack of representation in constituencies translates to a lack of proportionality at the
national level, with votes not translated into seats with any real accuracy, leading to many
voters feeling the system lacks legitimacy.
?
This is because candidates who come 2nd/3rd etc in a constituency with large
numbers of votes aren't rewarded at all by FPTP.
?
The Gallagher Index, used to measure proportionality, rated the 2024 election the
least proportional election since elections in the UK began.
?
FPTP exaggerates the support of the biggest party - the winner's bonus - which almost
always enjoys a share of the seats in excess of the share of the vote it receives. This is
because this party will often win a lot of the marginal seats between the two main parties,
which usually determine the election outcome.
?
In the February 1974 and 1951 elections, the winning party in terms of seats
received fewer votes than that of the second biggest party in terms of seats.
In 2019, the Conservative Party won 56% of the seats with just 43.6% of the vote.
In the 2024 election, the Labour Party won 63% of the seats with just 33.7% of
the vote, despite winning just 202 seats with 32.1% of the vote in 2019.
?
?
?
FPTP also favours parties whose vote is concentrated rather than spread out across a
large geographical area and therefore harms most minor parties.

?
In 2019, the Liberal Democrats won 11.5% of the vote but just 11/650 seats (1.7% of
the seats). If the system was fully proportional, they would've won 75 seats.
¦
In 2024, the Lib Dems won a very similar share of the vote (12.2%) but just
72 seats.

In 2024, Reform UK received 14.3% of the vote, but just 5 seats (0.8% of the seats)
This contrasts with the SNP, who have often benefitted from FPTP as their vote is
geographically concentrated. In 2015, they won 95% of Scottish seats with just 50%

FPTP doesn't reflect the fact that the shared vote for the two major parties has been
decreasing for some time.

In 2024, Labour and the Conservatives won 81.8% of the seats with just 57.4% of
the vote.
Limited Voter Choice:
?
?
Under FPTP, each party puts forward just one candidate, so there is no choice between
different strands of the main parties, which are broad churches.
Voters also only get one vote and therefore can't rank their preferences or reflect their
political views/preferences more fully, as a system like AMS or STV allows.
?
?
Votes for the party that doesn't win in a constituency are effectively 'wasted'.
This also leads to tactical voting by many voters who live in constituencies in
which their preferred party is highly unlikely to win and therefore vote for a different
party to try and prevent their least favourite party winning in their constituency.
Voters are therefore prevented from voting for the party they actually support.
¦
Tactical voting was a significant reason for the success of both Labour and
the Liberal Democrats in the 2024 election, with many voters supporting
whichever of these parties' candidates was most likely to win in their
constituencies to remove the Tories. Combined, the two parties won 483/650
seats with just 45.9% of the vote.
¦
Pressure Group MakeVotesMatter reported that in 2024 1/5 voters intended
to vote tactically and 58% of those who voted ended up with an MP they
didn't vote for, the highest since WWII.
?
Votes are also of greatly unequal value. Not only because a vote in a smaller constituency
counts for more than it does in a larger constituency, but also due to the difference between
safe and marginal seats.
?
In safe seats, many voters have little hope of seeing their favoured candidate
winning, leading to depressed turnout among certain voters. Little campaign focus
by parties is on these constituencies/voters.
¦
Manchester Rusholme, for example, was won by Labour in 2024 with
51.9.3% of the vote on a turnout of just 40%.
?
In marginal seats, votes matter a lot more, leading to higher turnout. A great deal of
campaign focus by parties is on these constituencies/voters.

In the 2024 election, Central Devon was won by Conservative Mel Stride by

Ineffective Choosing of Governments:
?
Single party governments can be seen as negative, especially as they don't command a
majority of votes across the country and have major power in parliament, leading to huge
changes being initiated by governments with limited legitimacy.
?
The Labour Party was elected in the 2024 election with 411 seats (63% of seats)
despite receiving just 33.7% of votes.
?
The party therefore has a massive majority of 174 seats and will be able to enact
changes with little opposition.
?
?
FPTP has also recently failed to deliver single party, majority governments, with a coalition
in 2010 and a minority government in 2017.
Prior to the 2024 local elections, there were 91 local councils in England with no overall
control, where coalitions are necessary. This shows that FPTP doesn't always result in
majority governments.
?
This being said, in Northern Ireland following the 2023 local elections (which use
STV), only 1 out of the 11 local councils has majority control, showing how FPTP is
much more likely to produce majority governments.

Why is FPTP Used In UK Parliament Elections?
?
?
?
FPTP has survived largely because it suits the interests of the two main parties, who have
monopolised government since WWII.
Labour offered a referendum on FPTP in their 1997 election manifesto, but had no incentive
to deliver it after winning a large independent majority under the current system.
The 2011 AV referendum was only agreed to by the Conservatives after the Liberal
Democrats insisted on it in coalition negotiations.
?
It was conclusively rejected by the electorate, with 68% voting against changing the
electoral system, on a 42% turnout.
?
This was partly as a protest vote against the Liberal Democrats and partly as voters
favour FPTP's speed and simplicity.
Additional Member System (AMS)
Where is AMS used and how does it work?
?
AMS is used in elections to the Scottish parliament and the Greater London Assembly
(GLA). Before 2024, it was also used for the Welsh Assembly.
?
Voters have two votes, one for a constituency representative elected using FPTP and
another for a party list which uses multi-member regional constituencies, introducing an
element of proportional representation.
?
The party list element 'corrects' the constituency element by using the D'Hondt
formula to determine how many members a party should be allocated from the lists.
This formula gives parties who were underrepresented in the constituency element
greater representation and vice versa.
?
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
12


 
?
There are fewer list members than constituency representatives and they are hence known
as 'additional' or 'top-up' members.
?
In the Scottish parliament, 73 of the 129 members are elected in single-member
constituencies, with the remaining 56 being filled by list members.
Advantages
?
The 'top up' party list element introduces an element of proportionality that corrects the
disproportionality of FPTP and leads to a highly proportional system overall.
?
For example, in the 1999 Scottish Parliament election the Conservative Party won 0
constituency MPs despite getting 15.6% of the vote, but won 18 additional member
seats in order to correct this, leading to them receiving 14% of seats overall.
This is compared to the 1997 UK Parliament election, where the Conservative Party
received 17.5% of the vote but 0 seats.
?
?
?
The constituency element ensures a strong MP-constituency link remains.
Voters have a wider choice than under FPTP. They can vote for a 'split-ticket' if they wish,
voting for one party for a representative and another for their top-up vote.
?
In the 2021 Scottish Parliament election for example, the Greens won just 1.29% of
the constituency votes, but 8.12% of the top-up votes and got 8 seats. The Green
Party intentionally stands few constituency candidates in order to benefit from the
top-up element.
?
?
Votes are less likely than FPTP to be wasted.
The worst predictions made by critics of proportional representation have not been fulfilled.
Coalition governments in Scotland and Wales have proved to be largely stable and there
have not been frequent changes of government.
Disadvantages
?
It creates two different types of members, some with constituency responsibilities and some
without. There is little evidence, however, that the second category is seen as having less
legitimacy.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/

?
In smaller assemblies, there are sometimes too few seats for the top up elements to correct
the constituency element effectively and represent smaller parties, which receive less
representation than under a fully proportional system.
?
In the 2021 Senedd election, for example, the Green Party won 1.6% of the
constituency votes and 4.4% of the top up votes but no seats.
This is a key reason why the Senedd decided in 2024 to move away from the
Additional Member System (AMS).
?
A closed list system is used, meaning that the party leadership ranks candidates in order on
the list. It can use this power to limit the chances of dissident members of the party being
elected, therefore giving the party a great amount of power and making it harder for parties
to be broad churches.
?
On the other hand, this could be seen as an advantage because parties can ensure
good direct representation of disadvantaged groups, such as women and ethnic
minorities. For example, zipping is often used (where lists alternate between male
and female).
?
Following the 2021 Scottish Parliament elections, for example, 53% of the SNP's
MSPs were female.

AMS is more likely to lead to minority and coalition governments, which can be seen as
weak.

governments, whilst 2 have been minority governments.
The Breakdown Of The Scottish Government In April 2024
?
On 26 April 2024, the Scottish government, which had been a coalition between the SNP
and Greens, broke down.
?
First Minister Humza Youssaf decided to end the coalition agreements after the
Greens strongly criticised the SNP for abandoning a key climate change target and
pausing the prescription of puberty blockers to new patients at Scotland's only
gender services clinic for young people.
?
?
As a result, the Greens supported a no confidence motion against Yousaf, who soon after
resigned as party leader and First Minister.
This shows a key disadvantage of the Additional Member System (AMS); that it routinely
leads to coalition and minority governments which are weak and vulnerable to instability.
?
It also shows that there is clear partisanship and tension between the parties in
Scotland just as there is in the UK Parliament.
Why is AMS used in elections to the Scottish Parliament and Greater London
Assembly?

AMS was chosen as a compromise that would result in a broadly representative parliament

maintain local representation.

Single Transferable Vote (STV)
Where is STV used and how does it work?
?
?
STV is used in elections for the Northern Ireland Assembly and Scottish councils.
It uses multi-member constituencies, in the case of the NI Assembly there are 18 each
returning 5 members.
?
?
Voters number their choices preferentially.
In order to be elected, a candidate needs to achieve a quota, arrived at using the Droop
formula which divides the number of votes cast by the number of seats contested plus one.
The results are calculated using a complex counting process that takes into account voters'
second preferences. If a candidate reaches the quota on the first round of counting, they
are elected and their second preferences are redistributed. If no one attains the quota, the
least popular candidate is eliminated and the second preferences of those who voted for
this candidate are transferred. This process continues until all of the seats are filled.
?
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
16


 
Advantages
?
?
STV is a highly proportional system, with a very close correlation between votes and seats
Voter choice is also very high. Voters can choose between candidates standing for the
same party, as well as between candidates from different parties. They also rank their
candidates preferentially, effectively giving them multiple votes.
?
?
The fact that there are multi-member constituencies and that candidates have to compete
against other candidates from their own party results in representatives placing significant
emphasis on representing their local population and constituency in order to achieve
re-election.
?
The MP-Constituency Loink can also be seen as effective as voters can go to a
different representative if one is incompetent or doesn't listen to them. Under FPTP
voters are stuck with just one representative.
In Northern Ireland, it has created power-sharing governments which allows
representatives of the two rival communities to work together, helping to end 30 years of
the Troubles. These governments also always have majority support.
?
This is the same in Scottish Local Elections, where after the 2022 elections 94% of
councils had no overall control. In 2003, when Scottish Local Elections still used
FPTP, only 38% of councils had no overall control.
Disadvantages
?
Power sharing govts may bring rival groups together, but they're still prone to conflict. The
NI executive has been suspended several times and STV didn't help the more centrist
parties in the long term. DUP and Sinn Fein now dominate, replacing the more moderate
Ulster Unionist Party and SDLP since 2007. Voting across community lines is still rare.
?
Stormont has been suspended for 40% of its existence, including most recently
between 2022 and 2024.
?
?
Counting votes is slow and the results are difficult to understand.
It can lead to donkey voting.
?
For example in the 2019 local elections in Northern Ireland, In District Electoral
Areas where there were two candidates running from the same party, the candidate
whose surname was first alphabetically was elected 85% of the time, whilst the
second candidate on the ballot paper was elected only 54% of the time.
Stormont Suspended Between 2022 and 2024:
?
The Northern Ireland Assembly (Stormont) was suspended for nearly 2 years following the

Fein in protest against the Northern Ireland Protocol.

This meant that there was no executive and business in Northern Ireland was run by
Westminster until the government was restored again in February 2024.
?
This shows a key weakness of the Single Transferable Vote (STV) system creating coalition
governments, though it must be recognised that the context in Northern Ireland and
animosity between the parties is a unique context and that single party government is
impossible without threatening violence.

Why is STV used where it is?
?
?
STV was chosen for Northern Ireland because it is a highly proportional system, likely to
ensure the broadest possible representation of different parties.
In view of the background of conflict between the two communities, it was important to
avoid single-party domination, which could've derailed the fragile peace process. The use
of STV ensures governments are power-sharing bodies drawn from both sides of the
divide.
?
The lack of single member constituencies is also less of a problem, as representatives
represent religious communities more than they do geographical communities.
Supplementary Vote (SV)
Where is it used and how does it work?
?
Was used in elections for the London Mayor and other elected mayors, as well as Police
and Crime Commissioners in England and Wales.
?
Following the Elections Act 2022 passed by the Conservative government, this has
now been scrapped.
?
This change results in candidates with less popular support being elected, which
often benefits the Tories locally as the left wing vote is split between the Lib Dems
and Labour.
?
?
Each voter is allowed a first and second preference vote.
Any candidate who receives more than 50% of the first preference votes is selected
automatically. If this doesn't occur, all candidates except the top two are eliminated and
second preference votes for the two candidates are added to produce an overall winner.

Advantages
?
It ensures broad support for the winner.
?
Sadiq Khan, for example, had the largest personal mandate of any elected politician
in British history under SV.

?
It is simple and straightforward to use.
It gives voters greater choice than FPTP, as they can indicate a second preference and
choose their desired candidate whilst also choosing between the two candidates most likely
to win.

It has allowed some independent candidates to win. For example, 12 out of the 40 elected

Disadvantages
?
?
The winner doesn't need to get an absolute majority of the votes cast.

Why was SV used for elected mayors?
?
?
Both SV and AV were considered, but SV was chosen partly because it was simpler to use.
It was also preferred as only the top two candidates after the first preferences had been
counted would make it into the final round. This meant that candidates with little positive
support would be less likely to win merely because they were a 'lowest common
denominator' second or third choice. In this way the winner would have a clear mandate.

Representation and Proportionality
?
STV is the most proportional system, with FPTP the least proportional and AMS more
proportional than FPTP, though less so than STV.
?
On average FPTP deviates over 20% from the mean, compared to 11% for AMS in
Scotland, 15% for AMS in Wales and 7% for STV in Northern Ireland.

The Droop Formula and lack of single member constituencies ensures STV is highly
proportional. The corrective top up element of AMS ensures it is also very proportional,
though it is still often less proportional for smaller parties and in systems such as Wales and
the GLA, where there are few members elected.

STV is designed to result in power-sharing coalition governments and has done so
