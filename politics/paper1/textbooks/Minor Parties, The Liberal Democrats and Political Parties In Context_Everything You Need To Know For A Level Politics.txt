The Liberal Party
The Emergence Of The Liberal Party
?
?
?
The earliest form of liberalism in the English political system was in the 17th century, when
the Whigs emerged as opponents of the Tories.
In the mid-19th century, they joined with a variety of middle and working class supporters of
social and political change to form the Liberal Party.
From then until the start of WWI, they remained one of the two major parties in UK politics,
competing with the Conservative Party for government.
Classical Liberals
?
Classic liberals were committed above all else to the freedom of the individual and the
minimal role of government in society.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/
2


 
?
In the 19th century, they supported free trade, the widening of the franchise, the
extension of civil liberties to those outside of the Anglican Church and the widening
of educational opportunity.
?
W.E. Gladstone; their most notable leader and Prime Minister in 1868-74, 1880-85, 1886
and 1892-94; also unsuccessfully attempted to give Ireland home rule as part of the UK.
Modern Liberals
?
Modern liberals recognised that many individuals couldn't be truly free due to the
inequalities produced by free-market capitalism.
?
The definition of 'freedom' moved from being left alone to the state actively
supporting some members of society to enable them to reach their potential.
?
The 'New Liberal' govts of the Edwardian era (1901-10) marked the beginning of modern
liberalism, adopting a range of social reforms such as old age pensions and national
insurance in an attempt to discourage the working class from supporting the newly founded
Labour Party.
How The Liberal Party Declined Following WWI
?
?
?
In the decades following WWI, the Liberal Party declined rapidly, due in large part to a
rivalry between its two most significant figures: H.H Asquith (PM 1908-16) and David Lloyd
George (PM 1916-22).
The Liberals attempted to appeal to both the middle and working classes, but suffered from
an inability to define their identity in an era of increasing class polarisation between Labour
and the Conservatives.
In the post-WW2 consensus era, their representation fell to single figures.
?
The party experienced short revivals in the early 60s and early 70s, but suffered
under FPTP, failing to make a breakthrough.

The Liberal Democrats
The Emergence Of The Liberal Democrats
?
?
?
In 1981, the Liberals received a boost from the break off of the centre-left Social
Democratic Party from the Labour Party.
?
The SDP formed an alliance with the Liberals and fought the 1983 and 1987 GEs as
the SDP-Liberal Alliance. In 1988, the two parties merged and formed the Liberal
Democrat Party.
Their share of the vote grew gradually and in the 1997 election they gained a total of 46
seats, due to the careful targeting of seats and disillusionment with the Conservatives.
?
They were also helped by tactical voting, though, with Labour voters in some
constituencies voting for the Lib Dems as they knew they had a better chance of
beating the Tory candidate.
By 2005, their total had risen to 62 seats (with 22% of the vote), an impressive achievement
but not enough to make them a credible independent challenger for power. Proportional
Representation could've helped them become a major player, but it is yet to be
implemented.
Charles Kennedy and The Liberal Democrats In The Early 2000s
?
?
?
In some areas, notably constitutional reform, civil liberties and internationalism expressed
by being pro-EU, the Lib Dems have been consistent in their policy.
?
In other ways, however, they haven't always been in agreement as to how to project
themselves.
When New Labour were in power and especially when Charles Kennedy was in power, they
were a centre-left party, rather than trying to be equidistant between the two major parties.
?
They were opposed to the Iraq War, identity cards and tuition fees and wanted to
introduce a new 50% tax rate for those earning over £100,000.
Kennedy exemplified the priorities of the social liberals: modern liberals who supported the
tradition of generous welfare provision, which could be traced back to the wartime
Beveridge Report.
Nick Clegg and The 'Orange Book' Liberals
?
Also in the party was a group of 'economic' or 'Orange Book' liberals, who took their name
from the 2004 publication and supported free market solutions to problems, whilst
emphasising the party's traditional commitment to the freedom of the individual, differing
from Charles Kennedy and the social liberals' more collectivist approach.

?
?
Nick Clegg, one of the authors of the Orange Book, became party leader in 2007 and
aimed to position the party so that it could conceivably work in coalition with one of the
major parties.
In the 2010 election, their manifesto included making the first £10,000 people earn tax free,
scrapping university tuition fees for undergraduate students, changing the Trident nuclear
missiles system and backing constitutional reform.
Why did the Liberal Democrats join the coalition and how did it damage them?
?
Following the May 2010 general election, the Lib Dems had 57 seats (on 23% of the vote)
and went into coalition with the Conservatives, having favoured them over Labour.
?
Clegg saw his party moderating the policies of the Conservatives in a less
confrontational style of politics, however this proved difficult to achieve.
?
?
The party was persuaded that, due to the gravity of the Financial Crisis, it needed to go
along with the Conservatives' immediate austerity policies, in order to demonstrate its
credentials as a responsible party in government.
If the Lib Dems refused to join the coalition, they would've been accused of running away at
a time of national emergency.
?
However, by taking part in the coalition, it became associated with a number of
unpopular Conservative Party policies, which were contrary to centre-left heritage.
?
They became associated with the Conservative Party's harsh austerity policies, u-turned on
their promise to not increase undergraduate university tuition fees and saw a more
proportional electoral system rejected by the electorate in the 2011 Alternative Vote
referendum.

The Liberal Democrats' Resurgence In The 2024 Election
?
Part of the Lib Dems' past success was always grounded in (1) tactical voting against the
Conservative Party and (2) the fact they were the natural protest vote for those who
normally supported the Conservative Party but weren't comfortable voting for Labour.
?
These factors came together in the 2024 election to give the Lib Dems their biggest
electoral success in their history with 72 seats, despite increasing their vote share
by less than 1% to 12.2%.
?
?
Ed Davey took control of the party in 2019. Prior to the 2024 election, his party gained little
media attention or traction with the electorate, with Davey a largely unknown figure to the
electorate.
The party ran an incredibly successful campaign in the 2024 election, however. They
almost exclusively targetted the most affluent constituencies in the UK and won almost all
of the seats they targetted.
?
The vast majority of the seats won were previously held by the Conservative Party,
including four that were held by ministers and the seats previously held by Prime
Ministers Theresa May, Boris Johnson and David Cameron.
?
Though some were attracted to some of the Lib Dems' policies, they were largely not that
different from the policy proposals of the Conservative Party or Labour.
?
?
Instead of due to policy, their success therefore came from the fact that much of the
electorate voted tactically in order to vote against the Conservative Party. In many
affluent constitutencies, the Liberal Democrats were the natural tactical vote to do
so rather than the Labour Party and they were inoffensive enough that many former
Tory voters were happy to vote for them.
During the campaign, Ed Davey was also successful in getting media attention for
himself and his party through the interesting strategy of consistently doing fun
activities such as paddleboarding and bungee jumping, even if in some he made fun
of himself.
?
They are now once more a significantly parliamentary party and could conceivably be
considered a major party once more.

The Historical Development Of The SNP
?
The Scottish Nationalist Party was founded in 1934 and is a centre-left party whose main
purpose is to secure Scottish independence from the UK. The SNP's growing strength
helped to convince Labour to take up the cause of devolution ahead of the 1997 General
Election.
?
Blair's Labour government believed that granting devolution would ensure Labour
remained the dominant political force in Scotland, by giving the Scottish people just
enough self-governing power so that they didn't vote for the SNP in the UK and
devolved elections.
?
?
This strategy was relatively successful until 2007, when the talented nationalist leader Alex
Salmond formed a minority SNP government in Scotland, turning this into a small majority
in 2011.
?
Since 2016, they have been in power in Scotland either as a minority government or
as the major party in a coalition with the Scottish Greens, who also support
independence.
The SNP's majority victory in 2011 led to the Westminster government agreeing to a
Scottish Independence referendum that was held in 2014.
?
The independence campaign narrowly lost this and SNP leader Alex Salmond
resigned as a result and was replaced by Nicola Sturgeon.
?
Sturgeon was largely popular and had significant success both in Scottish and
Westminster elections, but unexpectedly resigned in February 2023, saying she no
longer had the stamina to continue and that it was the right time to quit.
The SNP In Westminster
?
?
Nicola Sturgeon became a key figure in UK politics, particularly during COVID when
Scotland and England differed in approaches.
The SNP had major success in UK General Elections under Sturgeon's leadership.
?
?
?
In 2015, they won 56/59 Scottish MPs.
In 2017, they won 35/59 Scottish MPs.
In 2019, they won 48/59 Scottish MPs.
?
When English Votes For English Laws (EVEL) was in place, the power of Scottish MPs in
Westminster was reduced, but it has since been scrapped.
?
Even when EVEL wasn't in place, however, SNP MPs often refrained from voting on
matters that didn't affect their Scottish constituents out of principle and to emphasise
their argument that there should be Scottish Independence.

The Growth Of UKIP
?
The United Kingdom Independence Party (UKIP) began as a fringe nationalist party in
991, but by the 21st century it was associated with one man and one issue: Nigel Farage
and opposition to Britain's membership of the EU.
1
?
Farage was a charismatic individual, whose outspoken persona was one ordinary
people could relate to. By not conforming to the image of mainstream 'liberal
establishment' figures such as Cameron, Clegg and Miliband, he appealed to voters
disillusioned with the 3 main parties.
?
?
Its profile slowly grew, in large part due to the growing sense of dissatisfaction with the way
in which the 3 main parties accepted the quickening pace of European integration.
In the 2014 European elections, UKIP gained a total of 24 MEPs, making it the largest UK
party in the European Parliament. It won 3.9 million votes in the 2015 General Election,
though this returned just 1 MP.
?
Its growing support put pressure on the Conservative Party to commit to holding a
referendum on leaving the EU in their 2015 election manifesto, though, which led to
the 2016 Brexit Referendum.
?
After securing victory in the 2016 EU referendum, Farage saw his job as done and the party
has declined in importance since. UKIP is now a largely irrelevant fringe nationalist party.

?
By 2019, Britain had still not left the EU and Nigel Farage launched the Brexit Party ahead
of the May 2019 European Parliament elections to pressure for a hard Brexit deal and
oppose Theresa May's more moderate withdrawal agreement.

The Brexit Party won the most votes out of any party (30.5%) and the most seats
(29).

Reform UK's Success In The 2024 Election:
?
?
Reform UK received major support in the 2024 election. They were the 3rd largest party in
terms of vote share with 14% of the vote and finished second in 98 seats, many of which
were in the North of England.
?
Though they were greatly underrepresented by FPTP, winning just 5 seats, they
therefore demonstrated significant support and the potential to dramatically increase
their seat share and alter the UK party system in future elections.
During the election campaign, Reform dramatically increased their support. They rose from
just over 10% in the polls at the start of the campaign to 14% of the vote by 4th July.
Particularly important to this jump was the announcement in the beginning of June that
Nigel Farage would return to stand in Clacton and once again become leader of the party.
?
Though Reform took votes from both Labour and the Tories, with both parties'
support dropping through the campaign, they did much more damage to the
Conservative Party in terms of seats.
?
By splitting the right wing vote in many Leave voting constituencies, Reform stopped
the Conservatives from winning a large number of seats, a lot of which Labour won
even if they also lost some voters to Reform.
?
This demonstrates how minor parties can have a huge influence in elections, not by
winning seats, but through the 'spoiler effect'. This is when a minor party (Reform) draws
votes away from a major party (The Conservative Party), causing it to lose an election.
?
This effect can inadvertently benefit another major party (The Labour Party) by
splitting the vote, particularly when the minor party's platform appeals to voters who
might otherwise support the more dominant party.

Different Party Systems
What Different Party Systems Are There?
?
Single/One-Party Systems where there is only one party. These are dictatorships and
other parties are banned. For example, the Chinese Communist Party in China.
Dominant-Party Systems where only there are many parties, but only one holds power
over a long period of time. For example, the Botswana Democratic Party, which has held
power since independence in 1966.
?
?
?
Two-Party Systems where two main parties compete for power and are the only parties
that can conceivably win. For example, the Democrats and Republicans in the United
States.
Multi-party Systems where more than two different parties can compete for and
realistically win power. For example many European countries with proportional voting
systems, such as Germany.

The Conservative Party was punished by the electorate in the 2024 election for their

How United A Party Is
?
It can be argued that the level of unity within a party is important to party success. Parties
that are unified appeal to the electorate as it gives them an air of competence and suggests
the party will be successful once in power.
?
Divided parties, by contrast, seem weak, incompetent and lacking in effective leadership,
whilst failing to present an agreed upon, inspiring vision of hope to the electorate.
?
In 1997, the Conservative Party's damaging divisions over Europe were important in
their poor electoral performance. Their disunity contrasted with the newfound unity
and clear vision of the Labour Party under Blair.
?
Similar can be said of the failure of the Labour Party in the 2019 election, in which
the party was divided between the more left wing leadership and membership and
significant sections of the Parliamentary Labour Party, which was much more
moderate, as well as over their Second Referendum policy.
