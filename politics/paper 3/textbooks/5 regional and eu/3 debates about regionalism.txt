The Relationship Between Regionalism and Globalisation
Regionalism and globalisation are two major forces shaping international relations and the global
economy. While globalisation promotes worldwide interconnectedness, the integration of
economies, and the dissolution of national barriers, regionalism focuses on strengthening ties
between geographically or culturally proximate nations. The relationship between the two can be
complex: regionalism often complements globalisation by fostering trade networks and facilitating
international cooperation, but it can also serve as a counterbalance, allowing regions to protect
their interests against the pressures and volatilities of global markets.
How Regionalism Can Be Seen As Complementing Globalisation
Reinforcing Global Trade Networks
● Regionalism often complements globalisation by reinforcing and expanding global trade
networks. Regional blocs reduce trade barriers among member countries, facilitating the
free flow of goods, services, and investments across borders.
○ This regional economic integration can stimulate trade not only within the bloc but
also with external countries, contributing to global trade growth.
● For instance, the EU’s single market has enabled its members to operate more
competitively on the global stage, with companies benefiting from a large, borderless
market and collectively negotiating trade deals that improve access to non-EU markets.
● Regionalism also helps align policies across member states, making trade more efficient.
Shared regulatory standards and customs practices, as seen in the EU’s customs union,
lower the cost of doing business across borders.
Promoting Global Trade
● Regional blocs often pursue trade agreements that extend beyond their borders, actively
promoting global trade. By acting as unified entities, these blocs can negotiate large-scale
trade deals that smaller nations wouldn’t have been able to secure independently. It can
therefore be argued that regionalism promotes global trade.
● For example, the EU-South Korea Free Trade Agreement has significantly boosted trade
between Europe and South Korea, increasing EU exports by 76% since its implementation.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 3
● Similarly, ASEAN has established free trade agreements with key global economies,
including China, Japan, and India, demonstrating how regionalism can foster connections
beyond the region itself.
○ These agreements can create vast networks of interdependence, drawing regions
into the global economy and supporting the flow of goods, services, and technology
worldwide.
How Regionalism Counters and Can Protect Against Globalisation
Under free trade and globalisation, smaller and developing countries often find themselves at a
disadvantage, as they lack the economic scale or political influence to negotiate favourable terms
independently. This can lead to marginalisation in global trade systems, leaving these countries
vulnerable to exploitation by larger economies that dictate the rules of trade and investment.
Regionalism addresses this imbalance by allowing smaller countries to unite as a bloc, significantly
amplifying their collective bargaining power and influence on the global stage.
● Small African nations, for example, often struggle to secure favourable trade terms with
major economies like the US or EU who have exploited them for their raw materials for
decades, as their individual markets are relatively small and lack bargaining power.
○ By joining the African Union and African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA),
these countries gain collective influence, allowing them to negotiate better trade
terms as a unified bloc.
This protective function of regionalism is especially vital in the context of intensifying US-China
competition. As these two superpowers vie for global economic dominance, smaller countries risk
being drawn into one sphere of influence or the other, often compromising their own economic and
political interests. Regional organisations like the EU and ASEAN provide a platform for member
states to engage with both the US and China while avoiding over-reliance on either.
● For example, ASEAN has strategically maintained trade partnerships with the US and
China but also strengthened ties with other Asia-Pacific economies like Japan and
Australia, creating a diversified trade network that bolsters its economic independence.
● Similarly, the EU has taken steps to safeguard its technology and manufacturing sectors
from Chinese and American dominance by imposing tariffs and regulatory standards that
protect regional industries.
In this way, regionalism serves as a critical tool for smaller countries, allowing them to assert
economic sovereignty and pursue growth on their own terms within a globalised world increasingly
shaped by great power rivalry.
Criticisms of Regionalism As A Threat To Globalisation
On the other hand, critics argue that regionalism undermines globalisation and global
interdependence by creating insular blocs that focus on regional rather than global cooperation.
● They argue that regionalism encourages inward-looking policies, where nations prioritise
regional ties at the expense of wider global engagement. Such policies can be seen as
protectionist, limiting the benefits of globalisation by prioritising the needs of the regional
bloc over global economic integration.
○ For example, critics of the EU have argued that its extensive internal market
regulations and customs union create a “fortress Europe” that makes it harder for
non-member countries to access EU markets.

■ The EU’s Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) has been criticised for providing
subsidies to European farmers, which lowers prices and disadvantages
farmers from developing countries who cannot compete with these artificially
supported prices.
○ The United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), has also been criticised
for having protectionist elements that favour North American industries at the
expense of global competition.
■ The agreement imposes strict rules of origin requirements on the automotive
sector, making it difficult for cars with significant parts from non-member
countries to qualify for tariff-free trade within the bloc, disadvantaging
manufacturers from outside North America.
● Similarly, as regional blocs establish internal standards and protections, they may
inadvertently create barriers for global businesses that cannot meet the specific
requirements of each regional market. For instance, the EU’s strict environmental and
consumer safety standards, while beneficial for its own market, can make it challenging for
companies from non-EU countries to compete in Europe.
○ This situation leads to what is sometimes referred to as the “Brussels Effect,” where
global companies and even entire countries adapt their regulations to comply with
EU standards to access its market.
○ While this enhances the EU’s influence, critics argue that it may limit the diversity
and competition that are hallmarks of globalisation.
Prospects for Political Regionalism and Regional Governance
Economic regionalism has always been the primary driver of regional organisations, however
political regionalism is becoming increasingly prominent and countries seek to pool their
sovereignty to have a greater voice on the global stage and respond to shared regional challenges.
Intergovernmentalism vs. Supranationalism
A central debate within political regionalism concerns the balance between intergovernmentalism,
where member states retain sovereignty, and supranationalism, where some decision-making
authority is transferred to a central body. Most regional organisations, including ASEAN, the Arab
League and the AU, adhere to an intergovernmental approach, where decisions require consensus
and member states retain full sovereignty. This model facilitates cooperation without eroding
national autonomy, but it can also slow decision-making and limit the effectiveness of regional
governance, especially in responding to crises.
In contrast, the EU has taken a supranational approach in several areas, particularly in economic
and regulatory policy. This model allows for binding decisions that apply to all member states, even
if some disagree, thereby streamlining decision-making and enabling more cohesive policy
implementation. However, supranationalism has also sparked debates over sovereignty and the
democratic legitimacy of centralised decision-making, with critics arguing that it diminishes the
autonomy of individual member states.
While other regions may not be inclined to adopt a fully supranational model, there is potential for a
hybrid approach, where certain issues—such as security or environmental policies—could be
managed through partially centralised structures that allow for faster, more coordinated action.

The Prospects For Further Political Integration
Overall, while regional organisations like the AU and EU have made notable progress in political
integration, each faces challenges in moving further toward supranational governance. The EU
remains the most integrated example globally, but its path toward deeper unity is hindered by
internal divisions and diverse member interests, whilst AU member states are unlikely to give up
sovereignty in order to move towards supranationalism. ASEAN and the Arab League are even
less likely to pursue political integration, with strong preferences for intergovernmentalism that
have been in place since their founding.
The African Union (AU)
The African Union (AU) is arguably the most developed and integrated regional organisation apart
from the EU, with significant cooperation on peace, security, and socio-economic integration. One
of its most significant steps towards political cooperation is the Peace and Security Council, which
addresses conflicts and supports peacekeeping missions in regions affected by instability, such as
Somalia and the Sahel. The AU has also launched the African Continental Free Trade Area
(AfCFTA), which could pave the way for closer economic and political ties as member states
coordinate policies to facilitate trade.
However, further political integration and moves toward supranationalism face challenges due to
the diversity of political systems and levels of development among member states, as well as
strong national sovereignty sentiments. While the AU shows potential for increased coordination
and integration on security and economic issues, a shift to supranationalism remains unlikely in the
near future, with most member states favouring an intergovernmental approach that respects their
individual sovereignty.
European Union (EU)
The European Union (EU) is the world’s most advanced example of political regionalism, with
substantial supranational structures already in place. Through institutions like the European
Parliament, European Commission, and Court of Justice of the EU, member states have
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 6
transferred significant legislative, regulatory, and judicial powers to a centralised authority, allowing
the EU to act as a unified political entity in areas such as trade and environmental policy.
The EU’s commitment to “ever closer union” reflects an ongoing push for deeper integration,
although recent events like Brexit have sparked debate on the limits of this ambition. While there is
still potential for further political integration in areas like foreign policy and defence (e.g., through
the Common Foreign and Security Policy) and some key political figures such as Olaf Scholz and
Emmanuel Macron support this, other member states have shown reluctance to surrender more
sovereignty. There are increasing tensions within the EU over issues including migration,
democracy and economic policy, with the organisation struggling with having such economically
and politically diverse member states. Overall, while the EU remains committed to its supranational
framework, further integration currently seems unlikely due to these divisions.
Arab League
The Arab League has long struggled with limited political integration, operating primarily as an
intergovernmental body focused on dialogue and cooperation rather than binding policy decisions.
Efforts to develop a unified stance on issues such as Palestinian statehood and regional conflicts
have faced challenges due to differing national interests, particularly regarding foreign alliances
and domestic policies. Although there is periodic momentum for enhanced political cooperation, as
seen in joint statements and diplomatic initiatives, the Arab League is very unlikely to move toward
a supranational model. Most member states prioritise sovereignty and prefer flexible cooperation
rather than centralised authority.
Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN)
ASEAN operates on a strongly intergovernmental basis, with a core principle of non-interference in
the internal affairs of member states, which has limited the extent of political integration within the
bloc. The organisation’s approach to regional security issues, such as territorial disputes in the
South China Sea, reflects its cautious stance on political integration, focusing on
consensus-building and diplomatic engagement rather than supranational action.
Although some voices within ASEAN advocate for stronger regional coordination, particularly in
response to economic and security challenges, most members are wary of formal integration that
could infringe upon their autonomy. The region is deeply important in US-China competition and
there are divergent attitudes and economic interests within ASEAN on how to respond, which
would make further political integration and supranationalism very difficult, as states wouldn’t want
to give up their veto and there will be pressure from China and the US on them not to do so.

Arguments That Regionalism Does Undermine State Sovereignty
Supranational Institutions Mean That Decisions Can Be Imposed On A Country Even
If They Oppose Them
In regional organisations with supranational elements, member states can find themselves bound
by decisions even when they oppose them, challenging their national sovereignty.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 7
● The European Union (EU) exemplifies this through institutions like the European
Commission and the European Court of Justice, which hold significant power to enforce
regional policies and legal rulings across all member states.
● Similarly, in the African Union (AU), the Peace and Security Council has occasionally
deployed peacekeeping forces in conflict zones, even in cases where the affected state has
shown reluctance.
○ For instance, in Sudan, AU peacekeeping forces were deployed in the Darfur region
to contain the violence, despite resistance from the Sudanese government.
○ In Somalia, the AU’s mission (AMISOM) has been critical in stabilising the country,
yet it required significant AU intervention that overrode Somali sovereignty to some
extent. Although the AU operates primarily as an intergovernmental body, its ability
to impose security measures highlights how regional organisations can limit
member states’ control over domestic affairs in the interest of regional stability.
Economic Integration Creates Dependency and Economic Agreements Serve the
Whole Bloc Rather Than the Particular Interests of a State
● Economic integration within regional blocs often creates dependencies, as states become
reliant on the regional market and economic policies that are designed to benefit the bloc
as a whole rather than individual countries.
○ In the EU, member states, especially those within the eurozone, have ceded control
over key economic policies, including monetary policy, to the European Central
Bank (ECB).
■ During the eurozone crisis, Greece was required to implement strict austerity
measures and economic reforms in exchange for financial support from EU
institutions, even though these measures were highly unpopular
domestically and led to severe economic hardship.
○ The priority was stabilising the euro and protecting the interests of the bloc, not
necessarily the welfare of Greek citizens, demonstrating how regional economic
policies can supersede national interests.
● In the African Union, smaller economies within the bloc may struggle to compete with
dominant players such as Nigeria, South Africa, and Kenya. These larger economies have
significant influence over trade policies, which may align more closely with their interests
than those of smaller or less-developed member states.
● In ASEAN, economic agreements such as the ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA) facilitate
the reduction of tariffs and barriers within the bloc, but they also increase dependency

among member states on regional trade rather than fostering truly diversified,
self-sustaining economies.
○ Countries like Myanmar and Laos, with smaller, less competitive economies, may
find themselves at a disadvantage within AFTA, as the interests of stronger
economies like Thailand and Singapore shape regional economic policies.
Regionalism Can Erode Countries’ Cultural and Political Identity
● Regionalism can also lead to the erosion of cultural and political identity, as countries adapt
to shared norms and standards that may not align with their unique values and traditions.
● In the EU, for instance, member states must comply with common policies on issues like
immigration, human rights, and social welfare, which may conflict with local customs or
political preferences.
○ Countries such as Hungary and Poland have resisted EU mandates on migration
and judicial reforms, arguing that these policies clash with their national values, yet
they remain bound by EU rules and face sanctions for non-compliance.
Arguments That Regionalism Doesn’t Undermine State Sovereignty
Other Than The EU, Regional Organisations Are Intergovernmental
● Most regional organisations, unlike the European Union, operate on an intergovernmental
model where member states maintain a high degree of autonomy and control over
decision-making.
● In ASEAN, decision-making is based on consensus and the core principle of
non-interference in each country’s internal affairs, meaning that no policy or initiative can be
implemented unless all member states agree.
○ This consensus model allows ASEAN countries, such as Indonesia, the Philippines,
and Thailand, to engage in regional cooperation while retaining full control over
policies affecting their sovereignty.
● Similarly, the Arab League is structured as a loose cooperative alliance that prioritises
sovereignty and national autonomy, focusing on coordination rather than imposing binding
policies. Member states like Egypt and Saudi Arabia can pursue their foreign policies
independently, even when these differ from regional priorities, such as during the Gulf
states’ diplomatic shift toward Israel.
● The African Union also adheres to an intergovernmental framework where member states
retain decision-making power, as seen in its approach to peacekeeping operations, where
participation is voluntary and reflects the individual country’s willingness to engage. These
examples demonstrate how regionalism can function as a platform for cooperation without
undermining national autonomy, especially in blocs that prioritise intergovernmentalism.
States Retain Complete Sovereignty Over Key Policy Areas
● Even within highly integrated regional organisations like the EU, member states retain
complete control over core areas of national interest, such as taxation, defence, and foreign
relations.
● EU member states maintain independent defence forces and make sovereign decisions on
military matters, with NATO serving as the primary defence alliance in Europe rather than
the EU itself.
○ France, for example, has conducted military interventions in Africa independently, as
seen in its operations in Mali, showing that EU countries do not have to coordinate
military actions through the EU.

● In the African Union, member states similarly retain authority over their defence policies,
with countries making autonomous decisions about their military engagements, even
though the AU provides a platform for cooperation.
● The Arab League also reflects this principle, with members maintaining separate foreign
policies and alliances; for instance, countries like Qatar and Saudi Arabia pursue distinct
diplomatic agendas, as demonstrated during the recent Gulf blockade, where Qatar
maintained relations with Iran despite pressure from other Arab states.
● ASEAN also operates under a non-interference policy that allows countries to
independently manage foreign relations and defence, which is crucial given the diversity of
political and security alliances among members, such as Thailand’s military cooperation
with the US and Cambodia’s ties with China.
Regionalism Enhances States’ Overall Influence
Regionalism enhances the overall influence of individual states by enabling them to act collectively
on the global stage, amplifying their voice in international forums and negotiations.
● For smaller or developing nations, joining a regional bloc can strengthen their bargaining
power with larger economies and give them a seat at the table in international discussions.
● In the African Union, member states collectively address issues such as climate change
and trade, presenting a unified front that boosts their negotiating power with global powers
like China, the United States, and the EU.
○ During negotiations on climate policy, for instance, the AU has advocated for
increased climate funding for Africa, a stance that benefits all members, including
smaller economies that might otherwise struggle to be heard independently.
● Similarly, ASEAN has used its collective influence to establish itself as a central player in
Asia-Pacific diplomacy, particularly in areas of trade and regional security.
○ The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), which includes
ASEAN countries along with China, Japan, and Australia, demonstrates how
ASEAN’s regional cooperation can open access to wider trade networks,
strengthening the economic position of all member states.
● The Arab League, though less active in economic integration, provides a unified platform
for Arab states to advocate on issues like Palestinian statehood at the UN, allowing smaller
countries like Jordan and Palestine to participate in discussions where they might otherwise
lack influence.
● These examples show that regionalism can amplify the global influence of individual states,
allowing them to leverage collective power. Rather than losing power and control, they are
therefore increasing their overall power and control.
States Can Leave Regional Organisations If They Choose To
● One of the fundamental safeguards of sovereignty within regional organisations is the
freedom to withdraw if a state decides its interests are no longer aligned with the bloc.
○ In 2020, the United Kingdom exercised its right to leave the EU, a process known as
Brexit, after a referendum determined that a majority of British citizens wanted to
regain full control over issues such as immigration, trade, and regulatory autonomy.
○ This precedent highlights that even in highly integrated organisations like the EU,
membership is ultimately voluntary, and states can choose to leave if they believe
the organisation no longer aligns with their national interests.
● Similarly, ASEAN and the African Union do not impose binding membership; while no
ASEAN member has ever exited the blocs, the flexibility to withdraw reinforces the

sovereignty of each state, ensuring that participation remains a strategic choice rather than
a permanent obligation.
● In the Arab League, membership is fluid, with instances of countries temporarily
suspending or adjusting their participation based on political disagreements, as was the
case with Egypt’s temporary suspension following its peace treaty with Israel in 1979.
● The principle that states can freely exit regional organisations reinforces the concept that
regionalism is a voluntary alliance, and members retain ultimate control over their
engagement, ensuring that sovereignty remains protected within the regional framework.
Realist and Liberal Perspectives On Regionalism
Realist Perspective On Regionalism
Realists view regionalism as a strategic tool for states to safeguard their security within a
competitive international system. According to realist thought, states join regional organisations
primarily to bolster their own power against external threats, rather than to integrate politically or
economically in ways that would compromise their autonomy. For realists, regionalism is valuable
only insofar as it strengthens state sovereignty, allowing members to collaborate on shared
interests like security and economic stability without ceding control over critical areas of national
policy.
Realists argue that regionalism is fundamentally intergovernmental, as states are unlikely to cede
significant sovereignty; they may cooperate on shared interests but will always prioritise national
interests. In this view, regionalism is primarily a pragmatic tool for power consolidation and is
unlikely to lead to deep political integration, as states remain wary of any arrangements that could
constrain their ability to act independently in pursuit of their national interests.
Liberal Perspective On Regionalism
Liberals, by contrast, see regionalism as a positive force that can enhance rather than undermine
sovereignty through cooperation and mutual dependence. From a liberal perspective, regional
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 11
organisations create stable frameworks that encourage states to share sovereignty in specific
areas, believing that such cooperation ultimately strengthens national security and prosperity.
The European Union is often viewed by liberals as an example of how pooling sovereignty in
economic, political, and legal realms can produce collective benefits that outweigh individual
compromises. By transferring certain policy areas, such as trade or environmental standards, to a
regional level, member states gain collective influence and stability that they might struggle to
achieve alone. Liberals argue that regionalism fosters interdependence, making states less likely to
prioritise absolute sovereignty and more willing to embrace shared governance in pursuit of
common goals, ultimately creating a more peaceful and integrated global order.
The Extent To Which Regionalism Addresses Contemporary
Global Issues
Conflict
Arguments That Regionalism Is Effective In Reducing Conflict
● It can be argued that regionalism is highly effective in reducing conflict by building trust
among member states, creating economic interdependence that discourages conflict and
through mechanisms for mediation and collective security that address regional tensions
before they escalate.
● The African Union (AU) has developed a peacekeeping and conflict resolution framework
through its Peace and Security Council, which addresses instability across the continent.
The AU has authorised peacekeeping missions in conflict zones like Somalia and the
Sahel, where African troops work to stabilise areas plagued by insurgency and violence.
● Similarly, the European Union (EU) has been highly effective in maintaining peace among
its members, which was one of its founding aims after decades of bloody war on the
continent.
○ The EU’s strategy of economic integration, coupled with shared political institutions
such as the European Parliament and the European Court of Justice, has created
strong incentives for peaceful cooperation, helping prevent armed conflict among
member states for over seventy years.
● In Southeast Asia, ASEAN provides a platform for dialogue and confidence-building,
particularly around contentious issues like the South China Sea disputes, involving member
states like the Philippines and Vietnam.
○ Although ASEAN relies on consensus-based diplomacy rather than binding
decisions, its role as a forum for discussion has helped de-escalate tensions and
promote regional stability.
Arguments That Regionalism Is Ineffective In Reducing Conflict
● However, not all regional organisations have succeeded in reducing conflict, and some
struggle to enforce collective decisions due to internal divisions or lack of political will.
● The Arab League, for instance, has faced significant challenges in resolving conflicts
among its members, due to competing national interests and divergent foreign policy goals.
○ In the case of the Syrian Civil War, the Arab League initially responded by
suspending Syria’s membership and imposing economic sanctions, signalling
disapproval of the Assad government’s actions.

○ Yet, despite these measures, the League was unable to facilitate a resolution or
provide a unified response to the crisis. Different stances among member
states—such as Qatar’s support for Syrian opposition groups versus Egypt and
Algeria’s more neutral approaches—highlighted deep divisions that limited the
League’s capacity to mediate effectively.
● This example illustrates that while regionalism has the potential to mitigate conflicts, its
effectiveness heavily depends on member states’ willingness to prioritise collective stability
over their individual interests and alliances.
Human Rights
Arguments That Regionalism Is Effective In Promoting Human Rights
● On the one hand, it can be argued that regionalism is effective in promoting human rights
by establishing monitoring mechanisms and encouraging member states to uphold certain
minimum human rights protections.
○ As member states don’t want to lose the economic benefits of being a member of a
regional organisation, this makes them less likely to undermine human rights, as
this would risk them being kicked out.
● The African Union (AU), for example, has adopted the African Charter on Human and
Peoples’ Rights, a legally binding document that sets out a framework for human rights
across the continent.
○ The AU has also established the African Court on Human and Peoples’ Rights,
which allows individuals and organisations to bring cases against member states,
increasing accountability for human rights abuses.
● Similarly, the European Union (EU) has embedded human rights within its legal framework
and made them a fundamental component of EU membership criteria.
○ EU member states are required to comply with the EU Charter of Fundamental
Rights, which covers rights ranging from freedom of expression to
non-discrimination, and the European Court of Justice has the authority to rule on
cases where member states violate these rights.
● In Southeast Asia, ASEAN has made strides towards promoting human rights through the
establishment of the ASEAN Intergovernmental Commission on Human Rights (AICHR),
which, although limited in enforcement powers, provides a platform for discussing and
encouraging adherence to human rights principles among member states.
Arguments That Regionalism Is Ineffective In Promoting Human Rights
● On the other hand, it must be recognised that regionalism is not always effective in
promoting human rights. Regional organisations often face internal divisions and lack the
enforcement mechanisms necessary to hold member states accountable.
● The Arab League, for example, has a mixed record on human rights, and its member states
have diverse political systems, political interests and human rights practices that make
consensus difficult.
○ While the League has made statements condemning human rights abuses in
conflict zones, such as Syria and Yemen, it lacks a binding human rights charter or
enforcement body, limiting its ability to take concrete action.
● Similarly, the AU, while promoting human rights through its Charter, has been criticised for
its limited effectiveness in cases involving powerful member states.
○ Despite concerns over human rights abuses in Ethiopia and Sudan, the AU has
often struggled to intervene decisively due to political sensitivities and the need to
maintain regional unity.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 13
● ASEAN also faces challenges in promoting human rights effectively; its Intergovernmental
Commission on Human Rights operates on a non-binding, advisory basis, which restricts its
ability to hold member states accountable for abuses.
○ The organisation’s principle of non-interference limits its capacity to address human
rights violations within member states like Myanmar, where recent actions against
the Rohingya have drawn international condemnation.
● These examples demonstrate that while regionalism can create frameworks for human
rights, its impact often depends on member states’ willingness to prioritise human rights
over national sovereignty and political alliances.
Reducing Poverty
Arguments That Regionalism Is Effective In Reducing Poverty
● Regionalism is highly effective in reducing poverty by fostering economic cooperation,
facilitating trade, and providing funding and support for development initiatives that target
less-developed member states.
● The African Union (AU) has made poverty reduction a core priority, notably through
initiatives like the New Partnership for Africa’s Development (NEPAD), which promotes
sustainable economic growth, improves infrastructure, and boosts human development
across the continent.
○ According to the World Bank, the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) has
the potential to lift 30 million Africans out of extreme poverty by 2035, as the single
African market stimulates economic activity, creates jobs, and provides better
access to goods and services.
● Similarly, the European Union (EU) has significantly reduced poverty within its member
states through its Structural and Cohesion Funds, which provide targeted financial support
to less-developed regions in countries like Romania, Bulgaria, and Greece.
○ Between 2014 and 2020, the EU allocated €367 billion to reduce regional
disparities, resulting in infrastructure improvements, better educational
opportunities, and economic growth, which have contributed to lower poverty rates
in many areas.
● In Southeast Asia, ASEAN’s ASEAN Economic Community (AEC) has fostered economic
integration and investment, with poverty rates in ASEAN countries such as Cambodia
dropping from 47.8% in 2007 to 16.6% in 2021, thanks in part to increased regional
investment and job opportunities.
Arguments That Regionalism Is Ineffective In Reducing Poverty
● On the other hand it can be argued that regionalism is ineffective in reducing poverty, as
economic integration can lead to uneven development and benefits wealthier states more
than poorer ones.
● The EU has faced criticism for creating economic disparities within the bloc. For example,
following the 2008 financial crisis, Greece’s poverty rate surged to 35% by 2015, driven by
austerity measures imposed by the EU and the IMF as conditions for bailout packages.
○ These austerity policies led to high unemployment, with youth unemployment
reaching nearly 50% in Greece, highlighting how EU-driven economic policies
sometimes exacerbate poverty rather than reduce it.
● In the African Union (AU), while initiatives like NEPAD and AfCFTA show promise, limited
infrastructure and implementation challenges have hindered their effectiveness.
○ For instance, only 17% of Africa’s roads are paved, which restricts the AfCFTA’s
ability to reach the poorest populations by limiting market access in rural areas.

The Environment
Arguments That Regionalism Is Effective In Tackling Climate Change
● It can be argued that regionalism is effective in tackling climate change by enabling
countries to collaborate on environmental standards, coordinate policies, and pool
resources for climate action.
● The European Union (EU) is a leading example, having made ambitious commitments
through the European Green Deal, which aims to make Europe the first climate-neutral
continent by 2050.
○ As part of this initiative, the EU has set a target to reduce greenhouse gas
emissions by at least 55% by 2030 compared to 1990 levels, and it has allocated
over €1 trillion in sustainable investments to support this goal.
○ The EU Emissions Trading System (ETS), the world’s largest carbon market, has
successfully reduced emissions from power and heavy industry by 35% between
2005 and 2019, demonstrating the effectiveness of regional mechanisms in driving
large-scale emissions reductions.
● The African Union (AU) has promoted initiatives such as the African Renewable Energy
Initiative, which aims to provide 300 gigawatts of renewable energy by 2030, addressing
both energy access and climate resilience.
● ASEAN, through its ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution, has taken
regional action to address air pollution caused by slash-and-burn agriculture, with countries
like Indonesia committing to reduce deforestation and illegal burning practices.
● These examples show that regional cooperation can play a pivotal role in addressing
climate change, as shared commitments and pooled resources allow for impactful,
region-wide environmental strategies.
Arguments That Regionalism Is Ineffective In Tackling Climate Change
● However, regionalism is not always effective in tackling climate change, as internal divisions
and the prioritisation of economic interests can limit the ability of regional organisations to
enforce climate commitments.
● Within the EU, countries with high reliance on fossil fuels have resisted ambitious
emissions targets, complicating the bloc’s efforts to establish a unified climate policy.
○ Poland, for example, has frequently opposed stricter EU emissions targets due to its
dependence on coal, which accounts for approximately 70% of its electricity
production.
● In the African Union, while the African Renewable Energy Initiative and similar programs
are promising, limited infrastructure and funding often prevent effective implementation; for
example, only 48% of Africa’s population has access to electricity, and climate financing for
the continent remains far below what is needed to meet adaptation and mitigation goals.
● ASEAN also faces challenges in addressing climate change effectively, as its member
states have diverse economic priorities and development levels.
○ While Singapore has committed to halving emissions by 2050, other countries, like
Indonesia and Malaysia, continue to rely on deforestation and palm oil production to
support their much smaller economies, contributing to carbon emissions.
