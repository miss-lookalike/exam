The History and Development of the European Union
The Foundations of European Integration (1945–1957)
The idea of European unity gained momentum in the aftermath of World War II, as the continent
searched for ways to secure peace and rebuild devastated economies. Some European leaders
began to advocate for economic integration as a means to prevent war between European
countries and create a lasting peace on the continent. Winston Churchill, for example, famously
called for a “United States of Europe” to safeguard against the return of war.
The Formation of the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) - 1951
● On 9 May 1950, French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman proposed that France and
Germany, historical adversaries, should pool their coal and steel resources under a single,
supranational authority. This aimed to make war between the two countries “not merely
unthinkable but materially impossible” by creating economic interdependence.
● The declaration laid the foundation for the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC),
established by the Treaty of Paris in 1951. Signed by Belgium, France, Germany, Italy,
Luxembourg, and the Netherlands, the ECSC became the first European institution to
govern economic policy at a supranational level.
○ Its purpose was to manage coal and steel production across member states,
eliminating tariffs and trade barriers for these resources to foster collaboration and
growth. The ECSC was empowered to act independently of national governments.
The Founding of the European Economic Community (EEC) With The Treaty Of
Rome - 1957
● Following the ECSC’s achievements, the six founding countries pursued broader economic
integration through the Treaty of Rome, signed in 1957. This treaty established the
European Economic Community (EEC) with the shared goal of creating a “common market”
where goods, services, labour, and capital could move freely.
○ The EEC aimed to foster economic growth and ensure stability by removing trade
barriers and aligning regulations in order to integrate economies.
● The Treaty of Rome established new governing institutions, including the European
Commission, Council of Ministers, and European Parliament (initially known as the
Assembly). This structure created a framework for decision-making that balanced the
national interests of member states with the collective goals of the Community.
● The founding of the EEC marked a significant step toward economic unity, setting the stage
for further collaboration and solidifying a vision of Europe bound together by shared
economic and political interests.
Early Developments and Challenges in European Integration (1958–1985)
The formation of the EEC marked the beginning of a new era in Europe, with member states
striving for deeper economic and political cohesion. As the Community expanded and economies
became more interconnected, tensions arose between national sovereignty and shared European

goals. Nevertheless, this period laid much of the groundwork for Europe’s eventual shift towards
even closer integration and the European Union.
The Customs Union and Common Agricultural Policy
During this period, two key parts of the European Union today were founded; the Customs Union
and the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP).
● The EEC’s customs union, completed in 1968, removed internal tariffs on goods traded
within the Community. This milestone transformed the EEC into a significant economic
force, and trade within the union surged.
● The Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) was also introduced in 1962. It created a system of
subsidies for farmers across member states, ensuring stable prices for agricultural products
and protecting the livelihoods of those in the agricultural sector, so that they weren’t
undercut by the rest of the world.
First Enlargement (1973): UK, Denmark, and Ireland Join
● Initially, the United Kingdom was hesitant to join the EEC, preferring to retain economic ties
with its Commonwealth allies. However, Britain’s economic struggles in the post-war period
led it to reconsider its position.
● After negotiations, the UK, Denmark, and Ireland joined the EEC in 1972, marking its first
major expansion. The UK in particular strengthened the EEC’s economic and political
weight on the global stage.
● This enlargement also highlighted the difficulties of decision-making within the EEC,
though, as member states with different economies and policy priorities had to work
together and agree on decisions.
The Single European Act (1986) - The Single Market and Removing Vetoes
● Recognising the need to streamline decision-making and deepen integration, the EEC
introduced the Single European Act (SEA) in 1986. This marked the first substantial
revision of the Treaty of Rome and aimed to create a truly unified internal market by 1992.
● The SEA expanded the scope of “qualified majority voting” (QMV) within the Council of
Ministers, allowing a super-majority of countries to make a decision even if it was opposed
by some countries.
○ This change allowed for faster decision-making on issues related to the single
market and signalled an important shift towards greater supranational governance.

● The act also set a deadline for completing the single market, which sought to eliminate
remaining barriers to the free movement of goods, services, people, and capital—the “Four
Freedoms” central to the EEC’s mission.
The Maastricht Treaty and the Birth of the European Union (1991–1993)
By the early 1990s, European integration had already achieved significant economic successes,
including a customs union and a largely completed single market. However, political integration
remained limited. The end of the Cold War and the collapse of the Soviet Union presented both an
opportunity and a challenge for the European Community (EC). In this climate, member states
moved towards deeper integration, aiming to create a stronger political and economic union.
The Founding Of The European Union With The Maastricht Treaty (1991)
The Maastricht Treaty was signed in 1991 and came into force in 1993. This treaty marked a
significant turning point, transforming the European Economic Community into the European Union
(EU), a broader political and economic entity with ambitions that went beyond economic
cooperation.
The Maastricht Treaty established a three-pillar structure for the new EU:
1. The European Communities: This pillar covered the traditional areas of European
integration, such as trade, agriculture, and regional development, with policies governed
supranationally by EU institutions.
2. Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP): This new pillar aimed to coordinate
foreign policy and security matters, promoting a united European presence on the world
stage. However, CFSP decisions remained intergovernmental, requiring consensus
among member states.
3. Justice and Home Affairs (JHA): This pillar introduced cooperation in areas such as
immigration, asylum, and law enforcement, laying the groundwork for a unified approach to
justice and security. Like CFSP, JHA was also managed intergovernmentally, maintaining
member state sovereignty in sensitive areas.
In addition to these three pillars, the Maastricht Treaty introduced European citizenship, allowing
EU citizens the right to live, work, and vote in local and European elections in any member state.
This concept of European citizenship reinforced a shared European identity.
Economic and Monetary Union (EMU) and the Euro
● One of the most ambitious goals of the Maastricht Treaty was to establish an Economic and
Monetary Union (EMU), ultimately leading to a single currency for the EU.
● The treaty established strict criteria (e.g., low inflation rates, controlled government deficits,
and stable exchange rates) for countries wishing to join the Eurozone. These requirements
aimed to ensure that economies were aligned to minimise risks associated with adopting a
single currency.
● While the euro itself was not introduced until 1999 (as an accounting currency) and 2002
(as physical currency), the Maastricht Treaty set the framework for its creation. The euro
symbolised a significant step towards European unity, creating a shared economic identity.

The Political Significance and Legacy of the Maastricht Treaty
● The Maastricht Treaty formalised a shift from a purely economic community to a
union with both political and economic dimensions.
○ Although some provisions remained intergovernmental, the treaty reinforced the
EU’s commitment to closer integration and influence on the world stage.
● However, the treaty also sparked debates about national sovereignty, with critics arguing
that it represented an overreach of EU powers. Countries such as Denmark and the United
Kingdom secured “opt-outs” from certain provisions including the Euro, reflecting ongoing
tensions between integration and sovereignty.
Expansion, Consolidation, and Constitutional Challenges (1994–2007)
The period from 1994 to 2007 marked a phase of rapid expansion, consolidation, and attempts at
constitutional reform. Following the Maastricht Treaty, the EU faced the dual challenges of
integrating newly democratic countries from Eastern Europe and reforming its institutional
framework to accommodate a growing membership. These years brought unprecedented
enlargement, but also revealed the structural limitations of the EU, prompting efforts to address
these through treaty reforms.
The Enlargement Waves of the 1990s and 2000s
● The Second Enlargement (1995): Austria, Finland, and Sweden joined the EU in 1995,
bringing the total number of member states to 15. This enlargement underscored the EU’s
appeal as a prosperous and stable bloc.
● The “Big Bang” Enlargement (2004–2007): Following the end of the Cold War, many
former Eastern Bloc countries aspired to join the EU, seeking the economic benefits and
democratic values that membership offered. In 2004, the EU welcomed ten new member
states, including Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic, in its largest single expansion.
In 2007, Romania and Bulgaria joined, bringing the total to 27.
● The Challenges of Enlargement: The enlargement of the EU posed several challenges,
including economic disparities between Western and Eastern Europe and increased

pressure on the EU’s decision-making processes, as more voices and interests needed
representation within existing institutions.
The Treaty of Nice (2001)
The Treaty of Nice was introduced in 2001 as a response to the anticipated enlargement of the
European Union, with many Eastern European countries preparing to join. The treaty sought to
reform the EU’s institutional framework to accommodate a significantly larger membership and
prevent gridlock in decision-making processes.
● Adjusting Voting Weights in the Council of Ministers: The treaty modified the voting
system in the Council of Ministers to reflect the relative population sizes of each member
state more accurately. Larger countries like Germany, France, and the United Kingdom
received more voting weight, while smaller countries received a proportional amount.
● Qualified Majority Voting (QMV): The Treaty of Nice extended the use of QMV to more
policy areas. QMV is a voting procedure in which decisions are made not by unanimous
consent but by a majority, with each country’s vote weighted. For a decision to pass under
QMV, a specified threshold of votes (based on both the number of member states and their
populations) must be met.
The Constitutional Treaty’s Rejection and the Lisbon Treaty (2007)
The Constitutional Treaty (2004):
● To address the EU’s growing complexity, leaders proposed the Constitutional Treaty, a
document that aimed to replace existing treaties with a single constitution for the EU.
○ It sought to streamline decision-making, clarify the division of powers, and enhance
the EU’s global influence by creating positions like the President of the European
Council and a European Foreign Minister.
● Despite its intentions, the Constitutional Treaty faced public opposition, with voters in
France and the Netherlands rejecting it in 2005 referendums.
○ Concerns over the perceived loss of national sovereignty, bureaucracy, and
democratic accountability contributed to the treaty’s failure. This setback forced EU
leaders to reconsider their approach to institutional reform.
The Lisbon Treaty (2007):
● After the failure of the Constitutional Treaty, the Lisbon Treaty was proposed as a more
pragmatic solution. Signed in 2007 and coming into force in 2009, it preserved many of the
reforms of the Constitutional Treaty while retaining the existing legal structure of the EU.
○ The treaty established the position of President of the European Council,
responsible for coordinating the Council’s work and representing the EU globally.
○ It also created the role of the High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs
and Security Policy, aiming to give the EU a stronger voice in international
diplomacy.
● The Lisbon Treaty is often regarded as a turning point for the EU, as it addressed many of
the institutional limitations revealed by enlargement. By balancing national sovereignty with
supranational governance, the treaty sought to make the EU more effective, transparent,
and democratic.

The Eurozone Crisis and Further Integration Challenges (2008–2015)
The early 2000s had been a period of optimism for the European Union, with the establishment of
the euro and significant enlargement to include post-communist countries. However, by 2008, the
EU faced a major challenge that would expose some of the inherent weaknesses of its monetary
union. The global financial crisis triggered a sovereign debt crisis across several member states,
which would ultimately lead to questions about the sustainability of the Economic and Monetary
Union (EMU) and the euro itself.
The 2008 Financial Crisis and Its Impact on the Eurozone
● The 2008 global financial crisis quickly spread to Europe. The eurozone, bound by a single
currency but with diverse economies, found itself vulnerable to systemic shocks.
○ While some member states, like Germany, had strong economies and sound fiscal
policies, others including Greece and Portugal, faced mounting debt and recession.
● Without the ability to control their own currencies or devalue to regain competitiveness,
weaker economies struggled to recover. This was a key flaw in the eurozone structure:
while the euro promoted financial integration, it limited individual governments’ ability to
respond to crises.
● Greece in particular has a growing debt crisis and was unable to repay its creditors.In
response, the EU and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) coordinated a series of bailout
packages, beginning in 2010.
○ These bailouts came with strict conditions, requiring austerity measures such as tax
increases, public spending cuts, and pension reforms. While these measures aimed
to reduce deficits, they also led to economic hardship and social unrest.
○ To manage these crises and prevent future ones, the EU introduced several
mechanisms, including the European Stability Mechanism (ESM), a permanent
bailout fund established in 2012 to provide financial assistance to eurozone
countries in distress.
The Role of the European Central Bank (ECB)
● The European Central Bank (ECB), led by then-president Mario Draghi, played a critical
role in stabilising the eurozone. In 2012, Draghi famously pledged to do “whatever it takes”
to preserve the euro, a statement that calmed markets and helped restore confidence.
● In addition, the EU introduced the Fiscal Compact in 2013, a treaty aimed at enforcing fiscal
discipline within the eurozone. This required member states to maintain balanced budgets
and limit their deficits, reinforcing the eurozone’s fiscal rules to prevent future crises.
○ However, critics argued that these austerity policies hindered economic recovery in
affected countries and contributed to rising unemployment and inequality.

Political and Social Consequences of the Crisis
● The economic hardships caused by austerity policies led to political and social
consequences, particularly in southern European countries.
○ In Greece, Spain, and Italy, high unemployment rates, particularly among young
people, fuelled disillusionment with the EU and resentment toward perceived
German dominance.
● The crisis also fostered the rise of populist and eurosceptic parties, which criticised the
EU’s handling of the crisis and questioned the merits of the single currency.
● While the EU managed to prevent the collapse of the euro, the crisis exposed structural
weaknesses and deepened divisions between member states.
○ It became clear that while economic integration was advanced, the EU’s political
unity remained fragile.
The Rise of Populism and Brexit (2016–Present)
The eurozone crisis had left the European Union divided and vulnerable to criticism. By the
mid-2010s, growing disillusionment with the EU’s policies, combined with rising nationalism and
scepticism towards globalisation, contributed to the rise of populist movements across Europe.
These movements would challenge the EU’s vision of European integration, particularly in the case
of Brexit—the departure of one of its largest and most influential member states.
The Rise of Populism and Euroscepticism
● Populist and eurosceptic parties gained traction in various EU countries, driven by concerns
over economic inequality, immigration, and a perceived loss of national sovereignty.
○ Parties such as France’s National Front (now National Rally), Germany’s Alternative
For Germany (AfD), and Hungary’s Fidesz capitalised on public discontent and
criticised the EU’s principles of open borders and liberal democracy.
● These parties often criticised the EU as an undemocratic institution, accusing it of
overreach and failure to address the needs of ordinary citizens. Populist leaders
also promoted the idea of “taking back control” from Brussels.
● The migration crisis of 2015–2016, during which the EU struggled to manage an influx of
refugees from conflict zones like Syria, further fuelled anti-EU sentiments.
○ Some member states resisted EU policies on refugee relocation, challenging the
EU’s authority and sparking debates about national sovereignty.
The Brexit Referendum and the UK’s Withdrawal from the EU
● The most dramatic manifestation of euroscepticism came with the United Kingdom’s
decision to leave the EU. The UK had always had an ambivalent relationship with the EU,
securing opt-outs from the euro and Schengen Area.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 9
○ In June 2016, a referendum was held in which 52% of voters supported leaving the
EU, citing concerns about sovereignty, immigration, and perceived economic
burdens imposed by EU membership.
● Brexit prompted questions about the future of the European integration project and also had
a profound impact on the EU’s global influence.
○ The departure of one of its largest economies weakened the EU’s collective weight
in global negotiations and raised concerns about potential exits by other member
states.
○ However, the economic and logistical challenges faced by the UK after Brexit
served as a cautionary example, potentially deterring other countries from pursuing
a similar path.

The Powers and Structures of the European Union
The European Union is a unique political entity with a distinct balance of powers shared between
its institutions and the governments of its member states. At its core, the EU combines
supranational elements, where it acts independently to create and enforce policies for the Union as
a whole, and intergovernmental aspects, where decisions are made collectively by member states,
retaining their sovereignty in critical areas.
Understanding the powers and nature of the EU involves examining what it controls versus what
remains within the sovereignty of individual member states, as well as exploring the EU’s core
structures, such as the single market, customs union, and Eurozone.
The Division of Powers: EU vs. Member States
The division of powers between the EU and its member states is outlined in EU treaties, which
categorise competences (areas of policy authority) into three main types:
● Exclusive Competence Of The EU: In certain areas, only the EU has the authority to
legislate and adopt legally binding acts. Member states can only intervene in these areas if
empowered by the EU.
○ Exclusive competences include the customs union, competition rules for the internal
market, common trade policy, conservation of marine resources, and the monetary
policy of the Eurozone.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 12
○ In these domains, EU institutions create laws that apply uniformly across member
states to maintain consistency and avoid regulatory conflicts.
● Shared Competence: In areas of shared competence, both the EU and its member states
can legislate and adopt binding measures, though member states may only exercise their
powers where the EU has chosen not to act and where EU law doesn’t cover.
○ Shared competences cover major policy areas such as the internal market, social
policy, agriculture, environment, consumer protection, and transport.
○ This setup ensures coordinated action across the EU while allowing member states
the flexibility to address local needs within the broader framework of EU law.
● Exclusive Competences of Member States: Certain areas remain under the exclusive
control of member states, meaning the EU has no authority to legislate or take direct action.
○ These include national defence and security, education systems, taxation, criminal
justice, and healthcare systems.
○ Member states retain full sovereignty over these areas, reflecting the sensitivity of
national identity, cultural values, and security interests.
○ Although the EU can provide frameworks for cooperation, particularly in areas like
health or criminal justice, any binding laws or policies are decided and implemented
by each individual country.
The Four Freedoms of the Single Market
Central to the EU’s identity is the single market, which aims to create a “borderless” Europe,
enabling goods, services, people, and capital to move freely across member states. The single
market is supported by four fundamental freedoms, which promote economic integration, enhance
competition, and foster economic growth across the EU.
● Freedom of Movement for Goods: Goods produced in any member state can be traded
freely across borders within the EU, facilitated by the removal of tariffs and quotas. The EU
sets common standards and regulations to prevent trade barriers/customs checks, making
it easier for businesses to operate across Europe.
● Freedom of Movement for Services: EU citizens and businesses can provide services
across borders without restrictions, making it easier for professionals to work in different
countries and for businesses to expand their services across Europe.
● Freedom of Movement for People: EU citizens have the right to live, work, study, and
retire in any member state. This freedom has been strengthened through the Schengen
Area, which allows for the removal of passport controls at most internal borders within the
EU, creating a seamless travel experience across member countries.
● Freedom of Movement for Capital: Capital, including investments, loans, and other
financial assets, can move freely across the EU, supporting businesses, investment, and
economic growth. The free movement of capital encourages cross-border investment and
helps create a more integrated financial market.
The Customs Union
The EU operates a customs union, which is a cornerstone of the single market. Within the customs
union, goods can move freely between member states without customs checks or tariffs, allowing
for more efficient trade within the EU. To ensure fair competition, the EU has a common external
tariff for goods entering the Union from non-member countries, which means that all member
states apply the same tariff rates on imports from outside the EU.

The customs union enables the EU to act as a single entity in trade negotiations with other
countries, giving it greater leverage on the global stage. By coordinating trade policy and setting
unified standards for imports, the EU can promote fair trade practices and protect the economic
interests of its members.
The Eurozone and Economic and Monetary Union (EMU)
The Economic and Monetary Union (EMU) represents a deeper level of economic integration,
aiming to coordinate economic and fiscal policies across the EU and introducing a single currency,
the euro, for member states that choose to adopt it. The EMU is based on three key pillars:
● Coordination of Economic Policy: Member states coordinate economic and fiscal policies
to ensure stability and convergence across the Union. This includes setting budgetary
targets and monitoring economic performance to prevent imbalances.
● Monetary Policy: The eurozone countries share a single monetary policy governed by the
European Central Bank (ECB), which is responsible for maintaining price stability,
managing interest rates, and controlling inflation within the eurozone.
● The Euro: The euro was introduced in 1999 as a shared currency among eurozone
members, facilitating easier trade, transparent pricing, and deeper financial integration. The
single currency has strengthened the EU’s economic influence globally, though challenges
such as the eurozone debt crisis have highlighted the complexities of managing a shared
currency across diverse economies.
The EMU reflects the EU’s supranational ambitions, as it requires countries to cede control over
monetary policy to the ECB. However, fiscal policy remains primarily within the hands of member
states, which sometimes creates tensions within the eurozone, particularly during economic crises.
Cooperation on Foreign Affairs and Security
While the EU has significant powers in economic matters, cooperation on foreign affairs and
security remains largely intergovernmental, reflecting the sensitive nature of these areas. Through
the Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP), member states work together to coordinate
diplomatic efforts, address global challenges, and promote stability in neighbouring regions.
● Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP): The CFSP allows the EU to develop joint
positions and conduct diplomatic initiatives on issues ranging from human rights to crisis
response. However, decisions in foreign policy require unanimity, meaning all member
states must agree for the EU to take action, which can sometimes delay or limit its
effectiveness.
● European Defence Policy: The EU has taken steps towards closer defence cooperation
through initiatives like the Permanent Structured Cooperation (PESCO) and the European
Defence Fund.
○ These programs encourage member states to collaborate on defence projects,
research, and capabilities, enhancing the EU’s ability to act independently in matters
of security while respecting the sovereignty of each member state.
The Aims and Objectives of the European Union
The European Union was founded with a set of core aims that reflect its commitment to peace,
prosperity and promoting European countries’ interests on the global stage. Rooted in the
post-World War II vision of a stable and cooperative Europe, these aims guide the EU’s decisions
and shape its identity as a unique political and economic union.

● Promoting Peace and Stability: At its foundation, the EU aimed to prevent conflicts and
foster lasting peace across Europe. Through economic integration and political cooperation,
the EU seeks to create strong interdependencies among member states, making conflict
within Europe highly unlikely and maintaining stability within and beyond its borders.
● Economic Integration and Prosperity: By creating a single market and removing barriers
to trade, the EU aims to boost economic growth, create jobs, and enhance the standard of
living for its citizens.
● Defending Democratic Values and Human Rights: The EU is built on principles of
democracy, rule of law, and respect for human rights. Both within its borders and in its
external relations, the EU actively promotes these values, ensuring that member states
adhere to democratic standards and protecting individual freedoms for all EU citizens.
● Promoting Europe’s Interests Globally: The EU seeks to be a strong and influential actor
on the world stage, representing European interests in trade negotiations, international
agreements, and geopolitical matters. As a single bloc in trade, the EU has the economic
leverage to negotiate robust trade deals that benefit its members, setting standards that
impact global markets.
Political Regionalism in the European Union: Supranationalism vs.
Intergovernmentalism
The European Union’s governance model is a unique blend of supranationalism and
intergovernmentalism, making it a distinctive example of political regionalism. This dual approach
allows the EU to function both as a single, unified entity on certain issues and as a cooperative
alliance of independent nations on others.
Whilst this is certainly beneficial in maintaining members states’ sovereignty in many areas but
allowing efficiency in some economic policy in particular, it also creates significant tensions within
the European Union and undermines its ability to act as a decisive and unified bloc on foreign
policy issues in particulr.
Supranationalism in the EU
● Supranationalism is a governance model in which authority is transferred from individual
states to a higher, independent authority. In the EU, this supranational authority lies with
institutions such as the European Commission, the European Parliament, and the Court of
Justice of the European Union (CJEU), which act in the interests of the EU as a whole
rather than individual member states.
○ These institutions are empowered to make binding decisions that override national
laws, providing a unified framework that all members must follow.
● Key policy areas where the EU exercises supranational power include trade policy,
competition rules for the single market, and monetary policy in the Eurozone.
○ For instance, the EU negotiates international trade agreements as a single entity,
representing all member states and applying uniform tariffs through its customs
union.
○ The European Central Bank (ECB), another supranational body, manages monetary
policy for eurozone countries, prioritising stability for the Euro and the economic
cohesion of the eurozone.

● Supranationalism allows the EU to implement policies efficiently across all member states,
ensuring consistency in areas like economic policy, environmental standards, and
consumer protection.
○ This model strengthens the EU’s global influence, as it can act as a single entity on
the international stage.
○ However, supranationalism is sometimes criticised for limiting national sovereignty,
as it requires member states to follow decisions made at the EU level even if these
may conflict with national interests.
Intergovernmentalism in the EU
● Intergovernmentalism is a governance model in which national governments retain their
sovereignty and collaborate on decisions collectively, often requiring consensus.
○ In the EU, this approach is applied in sensitive areas where member states wish to
maintain control, including foreign policy, security, and defence.
○ Intergovernmental cooperation allows countries to negotiate and pursue policies
collectively while safeguarding their individual authority in key areas.
● The European Council and the Council of the European Union are primary
intergovernmental bodies.
○ In the European Council, heads of state or government from each member state
meet to set the EU’s strategic direction, often reaching decisions by consensus to
reflect the interests of all nations.
○ Similarly, the Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP) is an intergovernmental
policy area where decisions require unanimous support from all member states,
allowing them to retain control over national defence and foreign relations.
● Intergovernmentalism allows member states to protect their sovereignty, especially in
matters of national security, foreign relations, and taxation. It promotes cooperation and
mutual respect, as decisions in these areas are often reached by consensus.
○ However, the need for unanimity can slow down decision-making, particularly when
member states have differing priorities.
○ This can make it challenging for the EU to respond swiftly to crises, such as
conflicts or security threats, where immediate action may be needed.
