The Different Forms Of Regionalism
What Is Regionalism?
Regionalism is when states within a specific geographic area come together to form alliances or
organisations to achieve common goals and address shared challenges. By working collectively,
regional organisations aim to enhance stability, economic development, and political influence on
the global stage. Together, they can have much greater influence than as independent countries.
Regionalism In The 21st Century
Regionalism is arguably more important than ever in the current world order. With the increasing
dominance of global powers like the United States and China, smaller and medium-sized countries
often find it challenging to assert their interests independently. Regionalism offers a way for these
countries to build stronger coalitions, enhancing their collective voice on the global stage and
allowing them to better protect their interests in a globalised economy.

Introduction
The European
Union (EU)
Established in 1993, the EU is one of the world’s most advanced examples of
regionalism, evolving from its earlier forms (the European Economic
Community and European Coal and Steel Community) established in the
1950s. Initially focused on economic cooperation, the EU expanded into a
political and social union to prevent conflict in Europe and increase member
states’ influence on global matters. The EU’s shared political and economic
policies give it significant power in global trade and diplomatic discussions,
providing member countries with a strong platform to assert themselves.
The African
Union (AU)
Founded in 2002 as a successor to the Organisation of African Unity (OAU),
the AU aims to strengthen unity among African countries, promote peace and
security, and drive economic growth across the continent. The AU reflects
Africa’s aspirations to prevent exploitation and marginalisation in the global
order by providing a united front against external pressures. The AU also
helps African countries develop strategies to manage regional conflicts,
promoting stability and economic development across the continent.
The
Association of
Southeast
Asian Nations
(ASEAN)
Established in 1967, ASEAN seeks to enhance economic growth, promote
cultural exchange, and foster political stability within Southeast Asia.
ASEAN’s growth reflects the importance of regional solidarity in countering
the influence of major powers like China and the United States. With
members committed to principles of non-interference and cooperation,
ASEAN promotes a shared approach to economic development and regional
security, helping members negotiate more effectively in the global economy.
The United
States-Mexico-
Canada
Agreement
(USMCA)
Originally founded as the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) in
1994, this agreement was restructured as the USMCA in 2020 to reflect
modern trade concerns. The arrangement provides a framework for economic
cooperation and free trade among North American countries, allowing them to
compete globally by leveraging their collective economic strengths. In a world
where global supply chains are vulnerable to external pressures, regional
agreements like USMCA enable these countries to maintain a degree of
economic independence.

The Arab
League
Founded in 1945, the Arab League is one of the oldest regional
organisations, originally created to promote political and economic
cooperation among Arab states. The League aims to strengthen unity within
the Arab world, support regional development, and address issues impacting
Arab countries, such as Palestinian statehood and regional conflicts.
Although the Arab League’s influence varies across members, it continues to
provide a platform for dialogue on key political issues and regional security
concerns, offering Arab countries a collective voice on the global stage.
Economic Regionalism
Economic regionalism refers to the collaboration between countries within a geographic region to
achieve economic goals.
● By forming alliances and creating larger, integrated markets, states can attract foreign
investment, increase trade flows, and boost economic growth.
○ For smaller economies, economic regionalism offers protection from the risks of
globalisation, allowing them to negotiate as part of a larger bloc and avoid
exploitation by larger economies.
● Economic regionalism can include free trade agreements, customs unions, and common
markets, each representing different degrees of economic integration.
○ Some organisations, like the EU, have adopted a high degree of economic
integration, including a single currency and shared regulations, while others, like
ASEAN, maintain looser economic ties, primarily focusing on reducing trade barriers
and standardising regulations.

Security Regionalism
Security regionalism involves collaboration among countries within a geographic area to address
shared security threats and maintain stability.
● The motivation for security regionalism often stems from geographic proximity, as countries
with shared borders and regional challenges seek to prevent conflict and protect against
shared enemies.
○ Security regionalism has grown in importance in the 21st century due to the
interconnected nature of modern security challenges, such as terrorism,
transnational crime, and cyber threats, which require coordinated regional
responses. For many countries, regional security partnerships allow them to protect
their sovereignty while balancing the influence of global powers.
● Security-focused alliances can range from loose agreements on mutual defence/forums for
dialogue (such as ARF) to highly integrated military partnerships with shared strategies,
infrastructure, and policies (such as NATO).
○ North Atlantic Treaty Organisation (NATO): Although NATO includes members
from outside a strictly defined geographic region, it exemplifies a highly integrated
security alliance. Founded in 1949, NATO was designed to counter the Soviet
Union’s influence, establishing a system of mutual defence. It continues to operate
with a high level of coordination among members, and its military and strategic
planning functions as a unified response to threats.
○ African Union (AU) Peace and Security Council: The AU’s Peace and Security
Council, founded in 2004, is a prime example of security regionalism in Africa. It
aims to prevent conflicts and manage peacekeeping operations across the
continent, responding to regional crises such as civil wars and insurgencies. The
AU’s ability to organise peace missions helps reduce dependence on external
actors, allowing African nations to address security issues autonomously.
○ ASEAN Regional Forum (ARF): The ARF, established in 1994, brings together
ASEAN members and other regional partners to discuss security concerns in
Asia-Pacific. Though ASEAN members avoid a formal military alliance, the ARF
facilitates diplomatic engagement on issues like territorial disputes in the South
China Sea, counterterrorism, and disaster management, fostering security
cooperation without formal integration.
Political Regionalism
Political regionalism refers to the cooperation between states in a region to achieve shared political
goals and establish a stronger collective presence in global politics. While economic and security
considerations often drive regionalism, political motivations are also important, particularly when
countries are aligned in their foreign policy goals and values.
Political regionalism allows countries to enhance their influence in the global arena by acting as a
bloc, helping them navigate the pressures of globalisation and assert shared values and interests.
Regional organisations can counterbalance the power of dominant states and enhance the voice of
their member states on the global stage.
Political regionalism operates across a spectrum, with intergovernmentalism being the weakest
level of political cooperation and federalism being the strongest:

● Intergovernmentalism: Most regional organisations, including ASEAN and the Arab
League, adhere to an intergovernmental model. In these organisations, decisions are made
by consensus, and member states retain full sovereignty. Intergovernmental organisations
facilitate political dialogue and collaboration without diminishing national sovereignty.
● Supranationalism: The EU exemplifies supranationalism, where member states agree to
transfer some sovereignty to a central body that makes binding decisions on behalf of the
region. Supranational institutions allow for deeper political integration, making it possible to
implement policies across member states and establish a unified stance on global issues.
● Federalism: Though no regional organisation currently operates as a true federal system,
federalism represents the deepest level of regionalism, where member states would
function almost as provinces within a single, centralised political entity. In a federal system,
the central authority would hold the power to make laws and govern, while member states
would retain limited autonomy. The United States is an example of federalism.
