African Union (AU)
The Origins and Aims of the African Union
The African Union (AU) was established in 2002, succeeding the Organisation of African Unity
(OAU), which had been founded in 1963. The OAU’s primary goal was to promote unity among
African states, support decolonisation, and resist external influence and intervention in African
affairs. However, as African countries gained independence, the need for a more robust institution
that could address modern challenges, such as economic development, security, and political
stability, became evident. The AU was thus formed with a broader scope and stronger institutional
framework to meet these evolving demands.
Key aims of the African Union:
● Promoting African Unity: The AU works to strengthen solidarity among African nations
and encourage cooperation on social, political, and economic issues.
● Economic Integration: It seeks to facilitate regional economic integration to foster
sustainable development and improve standards of living across Africa.
● Peace and Security: The AU is committed to promoting peace, security, and stability
through mechanisms for conflict prevention, resolution, and management.
● Advocating Human Rights and Democracy: It aims to protect human rights and
encourage democratic governance across the continent.
● Improving Africa’s Global Standing: The AU strives to provide a collective voice on the
international stage, enabling African countries to address global issues from a position of
strength and enhance their role.
What Does the African Union Do?
The African Union has a wide range of functions, including promoting peace and security, fostering
economic growth, and supporting democratic governance and human rights. The AU is structured
as an intergovernmental organisation with some supranational elements, especially in areas like
peace and security.
Although member states retain sovereignty, the AU’s Peace and Security Council (PSC) has been
granted powers that allow for intervention in situations involving war crimes, genocide, or gross
human rights violations. This balance enables the AU to address regional issues while respecting
the sovereignty of its member nations.

The Structure of the African Union
Though its member states have given up less sovereignty, in many ways the African Union (AU) is
structured like the European Union, though with a greater security role.
● The Assembly of the African Union: Composed of heads of state, this is the AU’s highest
decision-making body. It meets twice a year to set policies and make decisions.
● The African Union Commission (AUC): The AUC acts as the executive branch of the AU,
implementing decisions and coordinating the organisation’s activities. It is based in Addis
Ababa, Ethiopia, and headed by a Chairperson.
● The Pan-African Parliament (PAP): This body aims to represent African citizens and
provides a forum for debate on issues affecting the continent. While it currently has
advisory powers, the PAP aims to gain legislative authority in the future.
● The Peace and Security Council (PSC): One of the AU’s most critical bodies, the PSC is
responsible for conflict prevention, peacekeeping, and crisis management. It has the
authority to sanction member states, mediate conflicts, and deploy military forces. It is
composed of 15 member states, elected by the AU Assembly, with five members serving
three-year terms and ten serving two-year terms, representing a balance of regional
representation across Africa.
● The African Court on Human and Peoples’ Rights: This judicial institution seeks to
protect human rights across Africa, complementing national judiciaries by allowing
individuals and NGOs to bring cases related to human rights abuses.
Case Studies of the African Union’s Work
Case Study – Peacekeeping in Somalia (AMISOM)
The African Union Mission in Somalia (AMISOM), launched in 2007, is one of the AU’s most
notable peacekeeping missions. AMISOM deployed AU peacekeepers with United Nations
authorisation to stabilise Somalia in response to the growing threat posed by the terrorist group
Al-Shabaab, which had taken significant territory from the government.
With support from the UN, AMISOM helped Somali forces reclaim territory and maintain relative
stability in parts of the country. This was especially impressive given the fact that the UN and
US-led interventions in Somalia designed to do the same in the 1990s were largely a failure.
Case Study – The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)
Established in 2018 and operational since January 2021, the AfCFTA is the AU’s most ambitious
economic initiative, aiming to create a continent-wide free trade area by reducing tariffs and
harmonising trade policies. With 54 of the AU’s 55 member states participating, AfCFTA is the
largest free trade area globally by number of countries, seeking to boost intra-African trade,
which has traditionally been limited by high tariffs, regulatory differences, and inadequate
infrastructure. The AU aims for AfCFTA to foster economic integration, attract foreign investment,
create jobs, and support sustainable development across Africa.
Several member states have started reducing tariffs and aligning trade policies, but significant
work remains in areas like customs infrastructure and dispute resolution. COVID-19 slowed initial
progress, affecting supply chains and economic activity. Despite this, AfCFTA has already
increased interest in African markets, with some countries reporting higher cross-border trade.
Full implementation could increase intra-African trade by up to 52%, according to the United
Nations Economic Commission for Africa.
Case Study – Preventing the Ebola Crisis

During the 2014-2016 Ebola outbreak in West Africa, the AU launched the African Union Support
to the Ebola Outbreak in West Africa (ASEOWA) mission. Over 800 healthcare workers, including
doctors and specialists, were deployed from various African nations to assist affected countries
like Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone. ASEOWA’s personnel played a critical role in treatment,
contact tracing, and public awareness to contain the virus, particularly in areas with limited health
infrastructure, showcasing the AU’s capacity for a coordinated transnational health response.
ASEOWA’s efforts significantly helped control the outbreak, coordinating with local health
systems and international organisations to reduce transmission rates. The mission’s quick,
effective mobilisation drew international praise and highlighted the AU’s capability to address
non-military threats.
However, the crisis also underscored the need for improved health infrastructure and a
permanent disease response system. Building on ASEOWA’s experience, the AU established the
Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) to strengthen future health
emergency responses, marking ASEOWA’s lasting impact on the AU’s health security framework.
The Debate About The African Union (AU)
Arguments for the Strengths and Success of the African Union
Economic Regionalism:
● The AU has significantly advanced economic integration across Africa, particularly with the
African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), launched in 2021.
○ AfCFTA’s goal is to create a single market by reducing tariffs, aligning trade policies,
and facilitating the free movement of goods and services across member states.
○ With participation from 54 of the AU’s 55 member nations, AfCFTA has the potential
to boost intra-African trade by over 50%, providing a pathway for Africa to develop
greater economic independence.
● In addition, the Programme for Infrastructure Development in Africa (PIDA) supports
AfCFTA by building the infrastructure, such as roads, energy networks, and digital systems,
needed for seamless cross-border trade.
○ For example, the Central Corridor Transit and Transport Facilitation Project, an
AU-coordinated PIDA initiative, connects Tanzania, Rwanda, Burundi, and Uganda,
enhancing road and rail connectivity across East Africa. Improved transport along
this corridor enables faster movement of goods, reducing trade costs and aligning
with AfCFTA’s economic integration objectives.
● These initiatives reflect the AU’s dedication to fostering economic growth, creating jobs, and
reducing reliance on external markets.
Key Recent Example - The African Financial Stability Mechanism:
● In February 2025, during the 38th African Union Summit in Addis Ababa, African heads of
state approved the creation of the African Financial Stability Mechanism (AFSM).
○ The AFSM is a $20 billion regional fund designed to help African countries facing
debt distress by refinancing their debt.
○ It will be led by the African Development Bank and will enable countries to borrow
from international capital markets, allowing for more affordable debt.
● This is a key example of how regional organisations can address key economic issues
facing their member states by pooling resources and establishing regional protection for
states.

○ High debt is a key issue facing AU member states, with public debt on the continent
over $1.8 trillion.
Security Regionalism:
● In terms of regional security, the AU has made considerable progress in stabilising
conflict-prone areas and addressing transnational threats.
● The Peace and Security Council (PSC) coordinates peacekeeping operations, such as the
African Union Mission in Somalia (AMISOM), which has been instrumental in countering the
extremist group Al-Shabaab and helping restore relative stability to Somalia.
○ Through AMISOM, the AU has successfully reclaimed territories from insurgents,
allowing for governance and reconstruction efforts to begin.
● Beyond traditional peacekeeping, the AU’s frameworks for conflict prevention and rapid
response enable member states to collaborate on addressing issues like terrorism,
organised crime, and cross-border conflict, reducing reliance on foreign powers for security
assistance and empowering African countries to handle their own security challenges.
○ For example, the AU-led Nouakchott Process promotes cooperation among 11 West
African and Sahel countries to tackle terrorism and organised crime. Through
regular intelligence sharing and coordinated border security efforts, the AU supports
these nations in handling cross-border security issues independently.
Key Recent Example - Efforts To Resolve The Civil War In Sudan:
● In both 2024 and 2025, the African Union has played an important role in trying to resolve
the civil war in Sudan, holding multiple mediation talks and working alongside the United
Nations to encourage a ceasefire.
○ Though it hasn’t yet been successful in brokering a ceasefire, the AU played a key
role in establishing humanitarian corridors in late 2024, which made sure that
civilians in Darfur and Khartoum had access to aid.
● This is an important example of how the African Union is often a very effective actor in
terms of security regionalism.
Political Regionalism:
● Politically, the AU has strengthened Africa’s influence in global affairs and promoted
democratic governance within the continent. The AU’s African Charter on Democracy,
Elections, and Governance sets standards for good governance, human rights, and
democratic accountability, encouraging countries to uphold these values.
○ The AU has taken tangible actions in response to unconstitutional changes in
government, suspending member states such as Mali and Sudan after military
coups, which reinforces its commitment to democratic principles.
● Additionally, the AU enables African countries to present a unified stance on global issues
like climate change and trade reform, ensuring that Africa’s interests are represented on the
international stage. This collective voice helps the continent engage in global diplomacy on
more equal footing, bolstering Africa’s sovereignty and political standing.
○ For example, through the African Group of Negotiators on Climate Change (AGN),
coordinated by the AU, African nations present a united position in global climate
talks, advocating for climate finance and adaptation support.
○ This collective stance strengthens Africa’s influence in negotiations, ensuring its
specific climate challenges are addressed.

○ This included raising the importance of debt restructuring and calling for access to

Arguments for the Weaknesses and Failures of the African Union
Economic Regionalism:
● Despite the promise of AfCFTA, economic integration within the AU remains slow and faces
numerous challenges. Many African countries lack the infrastructure, customs capacity, and
regulatory systems needed to support seamless cross-border trade, limiting the short-term
impact of AfCFTA’s initiatives.
○ The COVID-19 pandemic further delayed implementation, as member states
focused on handling the immediate health crisis and its economic effects.
● Additionally, AfCFTA’s dispute resolution mechanisms are still underdeveloped,
complicating enforcement of trade policies and compliance among states.
● These structural and logistical challenges raise concerns about how quickly AfCFTA can
deliver meaningful economic benefits, limiting its ability to drive regional growth in the
immediate future.
Security Regionalism:
● In terms of security, the AU’s reliance on external funding has been a significant barrier to
sustainable peacekeeping operations and regional stability.
● While the AU has undertaken critical missions, such as AMISOM in Somalia, these efforts
rely heavily on support from donors like the European Union.
○ This financial dependency limits the AU’s autonomy and places its missions at risk if
donor priorities change.
● Furthermore, in regions like the Sahel and Libya, complex conflicts involving global powers
and regional actors have often constrained the AU’s influence, as external interests
overshadow its peace efforts. Without a stable, self-funded model for security operations,
the AU’s ability to manage long-term security across the continent remains uncertain.
Political Regionalism:
● The AU’s limited enforcement mechanisms have hindered its influence over political and
human rights issues within member states, as it operates as an intergovernmental rather
than a supranational institution.
○ Unlike a supranational body, the AU cannot impose binding decisions on its
members, which limits its power to enforce compliance with its standards.
○ While the AU has suspended members following coups, its capacity to enact real
change or ensure adherence to democratic principles remains limited, as member
states retain their sovereignty and decision-making authority.
● In cases like Eritrea, where human rights abuses have been documented, the AU has faced
criticism for inconsistent responses, reflecting challenges in addressing politically sensitive
issues among its members.

and human rights, diminishing its capacity to influence governance and uphold its principles

Association of South East Asian Nations (ASEAN)
The Origins and Aims of ASEAN
The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) was established in 1967 by five founding
members—Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand—primarily to promote
regional stability and cooperation in the face of Cold War tensions and ideological conflicts.
ASEAN’s focus was initially on political unity and collective security to counter external influences
and prevent regional conflicts.
Over time, however, the organisation expanded its mandate to include economic integration,
cultural cooperation, and diplomatic unity, with the goal of fostering a stable and prosperous
Southeast Asia. Today, ASEAN comprises ten member states and represents a significant political
and economic bloc on the global stage.
In the current context, ASEAN’s role is increasingly important as Southeast Asia finds itself at the
centre of strategic competition between China and the United States. With China’s growing
economic and military influence in the region, and the United States’ efforts to maintain its own
strategic interests, ASEAN’s ability to balance relations with both powers is critical. ASEAN’s
approach to regional unity and non-alignment allows it to navigate this competition, seeking to
protect the sovereignty and economic interests of its members while avoiding direct alignment with
either global power.
Key Aims of ASEAN:
● Promoting Regional Peace and Stability: ASEAN aims to foster cooperation among
Southeast Asian nations to create a region free from conflict and external interference.
● Economic Integration: Through initiatives like the ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA),
ASEAN seeks to establish a single market and production base, boosting trade and
investment across the region.
● Political and Security Cooperation: ASEAN works to establish frameworks for collective
security, fostering diplomatic unity and conflict resolution mechanisms among member
states.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 8
● Strengthening ASEAN’s Global Standing: ASEAN strives to establish a unified voice in
international forums, empowering its member states to address global issues with a
stronger collective influence.
What Does ASEAN Do?
ASEAN engages in a range of activities to promote economic, political, and social integration
among its members. Unlike supranational organisations, ASEAN operates on a consensus-based,
intergovernmental model, meaning that all decisions must be agreed upon by all member states,
which retain full sovereignty. This approach limits ASEAN’s enforcement power but allows
members to collaborate without yielding sovereignty to a central body.
The Structure of ASEAN:
● ASEAN Summit: The highest decision-making body, composed of leaders from each
member state, convenes annually to set policy directions and make strategic decisions.
● ASEAN Secretariat: Headquartered in Jakarta, Indonesia, the Secretariat coordinates
ASEAN’s activities, supports member states in implementing policies, and manages
administrative tasks.
● Additionally, specialised bodies like the ASEAN Economic Community (AEC) and the
ASEAN Regional Forum (ARF) focus on specific goals such as economic integration and
regional security. Countries’ finance/trade ministers sit on the AEC and their foreign
ministers/senior diplomats sit on the ARF.
Case Studies of ASEAN’s Work
Case Study – ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA)
The ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA), established in 1992, is a landmark economic initiative that
aims to create a unified regional market by reducing tariffs and trade barriers among ASEAN
member states. Under AFTA, tariffs on intra-ASEAN trade have been successfully lowered to
nearly zero for most goods, leading to a significant increase in trade flows, with intra-ASEAN
trade reaching over $800 billion in 2022, accounting for approximately 22% of ASEAN’s total
trade. By reducing trade barriers, AFTA has facilitated a more interconnected ASEAN economy,
making it easier for businesses to operate across borders and enhancing ASEAN’s
attractiveness as a destination for foreign investment.
Case Study – Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP)
The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), signed in 2020 and entering into
force in January 2022, is a historic free trade agreement that unites the ten ASEAN member
states with five key trading partners: China, Japan, South Korea, Australia, and New Zealand. As
the world’s largest free trade area, RCEP covers nearly one-third of the global population and
around 30% of global GDP.Through RCEP, member countries have committed to reducing tariffs
on a range of goods and streamlining customs procedures, which simplifies trade across borders
and reduces costs for businesses operating in multiple member countries.
RCEP has already begun to drive economic growth within ASEAN, with the bloc’s intra-RCEP
trade projected to grow by 10.4% annually, reaching approximately $1 trillion by 2025. By
expanding ASEAN’s economic links with major regional economies, RCEP enhances the bloc’s
position as a central player in Asia-Pacific trade networks, attracting foreign investment and
facilitating cross-border supply chains.

Case Study – ASEAN’s Attempts to Manage the South China Sea Dispute
The South China Sea dispute remains one of ASEAN’s most significant security challenges, with
overlapping territorial claims to the South China Sea and its islands from member states like
Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, and Brunei, alongside China’s claim over nearly the entire
sea. The region is critically important, as it serves as one of the world’s busiest maritime routes
and is rich in resources like fish stocks, oil, and natural gas reserves.
Given the potential for conflict, ASEAN has been working to manage tensions in the South China
Sea, primarily through diplomatic channels and by pursuing a Code of Conduct (CoC) with China
to establish rules for peaceful navigation and conflict prevention in the disputed waters. However,
progress on the CoC has been slow, with internal divisions within ASEAN weakening its
negotiating stance. While countries like Vietnam and the Philippines advocate for a binding CoC,
others, such as Cambodia, have close ties with China and avoid actions that could damage
relations.
In 2016, a ruling by the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) in favour of the Philippines
invalidated China’s broad claims in the South China Sea under international law. However,
ASEAN was unable to formally endorse the ruling due to internal disagreements, weakening its
stance and limiting its ability to hold China accountable. Although ASEAN has succeeded in
preventing open conflict and maintaining a dialogue with China, the lack of a cohesive approach
among its members has undermined its effectiveness in managing the South China Sea dispute.
The Debate About ASEAN
Arguments for the Strengths and Success of ASEAN
Economic Regionalism:
● ASEAN has achieved significant success in economic integration through landmark
initiatives like the ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA) and the Regional Comprehensive
Economic Partnership (RCEP).
○ AFTA has played a crucial role in increasing trade and investment flows among
member states by reducing tariffs on intra-ASEAN trade to nearly zero, which has
boosted economic growth, strengthened supply chains, and enhanced regional
competitiveness.
● By acting as a collective bloc, ASEAN has strengthened its collective economic standing,
helping its members compete effectively in the global economy, including through signing
RCEP which will significantly boost their economies.
● Additionally, the AEC’s goal of establishing a single market and production base has
positioned ASEAN as an increasingly attractive destination for foreign investment,
particularly for companies seeking integrated markets and efficient supply networks.
○ For example, Japanese car companies Toyota and Honda have expanded their
manufacturing operations across multiple ASEAN countries to take advantage of
reduced trade barriers within the region. This integration allows them to efficiently
produce and distribute vehicles across ASEAN markets.
Security Regionalism:
● ASEAN has also contributed to regional stability through cooperative security initiatives and
conflict-prevention mechanisms.
● The ASEAN Regional Forum (ARF) and the ASEAN Defence Ministers’ Meeting (ADMM)
promote dialogue and cooperation on security issues, helping member states address
threats like terrorism, maritime security, and transnational crime.

○ For instance, ASEAN’s Code of Conduct negotiations with China over the South
China Sea demonstrate its commitment to peaceful resolution of territorial disputes,
showcasing its role as a stabilising force in Southeast Asia.
Political Regionalism:
● Politically, ASEAN has succeeded in fostering a collective regional identity and influence,
whilst operating on an intergovernmental model that respects member sovereignty.
○ ASEAN’s principle of non-interference has allowed it to build trust among members
and engage constructively with external powers.
● By presenting a unified front on global issues, such as climate change and trade reform,
ASEAN has amplified the voices of its members in international forums, helping Southeast
Asia engage in global diplomacy from a stronger, more cohesive position.
○ For example, at the COP26 climate summit, ASEAN member states collectively
called for enhanced climate finance from developed nations to support adaptation
and mitigation projects across Southeast Asia, a region highly vulnerable to rising
sea levels and extreme weather.
○ This coordinated approach helped secure funding commitments, including
contributions to the ASEAN Catalytic Green Finance Facility, which finances
sustainable infrastructure projects in member states.
Key Recent Example - ASEAN’s Unified Response To U.S. Tariffs:
● In 2025, after the U.S. imposed tariffs on all of its member states, the Association of
Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) issued a joint statement, after ASEAN’s economic
ministers held an emergency meeting.
○ They expressed concern of the U.S. tariffs, arguing that they disrupt global trade
and investment, but decided not to retaliate with counter-tariffs, but instead to
negotiate with the U.S.
● This coordinated, unified response is a clear example of how regional organisations like
ASEAN can protect their member states in the global economy, by pooling their economic
weight which allows them to have a much stronger collective voice and position from which
to negotiate with major economies like the U.S. and China.
Arguments for the Weaknesses and Failures of ASEAN
Economic Regionalism:
● Although AFTA has led to significant progress, ASEAN’s economic integration faces
obstacles, particularly in addressing non-tariff barriers and regulatory differences among
member states.
○ For example, Indonesia and Thailand impose unique labeling and certification
standards on certain food products, creating extra compliance costs for exporters
within ASEAN.
○ These challenges have slowed the realisation of a fully integrated ASEAN Economic
Community, limiting its ability to function as a unified economic bloc.
● Furthermore, ASEAN’s consensus-based model makes it challenging to enforce
compliance, hindering its efforts to harmonise economic policies effectively.
○ For example, the ASEAN Economic Community’s initiative to standardise electrical
product safety requirements has been inconsistently adopted, with Malaysia
implementing more stringent checks than some neighbours.

Security Regionalism:
● ASEAN’s ability to address regional security threats is limited by its principle of
non-interference, which restricts member states from intervening in one another’s internal
affairs.
○ This approach has often hampered ASEAN’s capacity to take decisive action in
security crises, such as the Rohingya crisis in Myanmar, where ASEAN’s response
has been criticised as inadequate.
○ Member states like Thailand and Indonesia called for stronger action, while others,
including Cambodia, suggested a non-interventionist approach. This divergence
delayed a unified response, limiting ASEAN’s ability to address the humanitarian
and security aspects of the crisis effectively.
● Additionally, while ASEAN promotes dialogue on security issues, its reliance on consensus
can limit effective responses to issues, especially when there is disagreement between
member states.
○ For example, ASEAN’s efforts to address the South China Sea dispute have been
hampered by differing stances, with Vietnam and the Philippines advocating for a
firmer position against China’s claims, while countries like Laos and
Cambodia—both close allies of China—oppose actions that could strain relations.
Political Regionalism:
● ASEAN’s intergovernmental structure, while valuable for respecting member sovereignty,
restricts its enforcement power in promoting human rights and democratic governance.
○ The ASEAN Intergovernmental Commission on Human Rights (AICHR) has limited
authority and can only act on issues with member consent, diminishing its
effectiveness in addressing human rights concerns.
● ASEAN’s inability to respond consistently to internal political crises, such as those in
Myanmar and Thailand, highlights its challenges in upholding democratic principles and
human rights across the region.
Arab League
The Origins and Aims of the Arab League
The Arab League was established in 1945 by six founding members—Egypt, Iraq, Jordan,
Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, and Syria—primarily to promote political unity and cooperation among
Arab states with shared cultural ties, language, and the need to strengthen regional autonomy
following colonial rule. Originally focused on promoting Arab solidarity and supporting the
Palestinian cause, the League aimed to create a bloc that could counter foreign influence in the
Arab world and resolve inter-Arab disputes peacefully. Over time, the League expanded to include
22 members and broadened its agenda to address economic integration, social development, and
coordinated foreign policy.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 12
Today, the Arab League remains an essential platform for Arab countries to address shared
concerns, although its influence has varied due to the diversity of political systems, economic
conditions, and foreign policy orientations among its members. In the current context, with
increasing instability due to conflicts in Syria, Yemen, and Libya, as well as the regional influence
of non-Arab powers like Iran and Turkey, the League faces both heightened challenges and the
potential to play a stabilising role.
The Importance Of The Palestinian Cause:
● Since its founding, the Arab League has placed the Palestinian cause at the forefront of its
agenda, viewing Palestinian rights and sovereignty as central to Arab unity and identity.
○ For Arab countries, support for Palestine is both a political and symbolic stance,
representing resistance to perceived foreign occupation by Israel and solidarity with
Arab populations facing displacement and statelessness.
● While the Arab League maintains a collective commitment to the Palestinian cause,
member states have differed in their approaches to Israel.
○ Egypt was the first Arab League member to recognise Israel with the signing of the
Camp David Accords in 1978. Jordan followed suit in 1994, establishing diplomatic
relations with Israel after the Oslo Accords signalled progress in the
Israeli-Palestinian peace process.
○ Although these decisions marked a departure from the Arab League’s broader
stance, Egypt and Jordan remained committed to advocating for Palestinian rights
and statehood within the League.
● In 2002, the League collectively proposed the Arab Peace Initiative, offering Israel full
normalisation with all Arab states in return for a withdrawal from occupied territories and a
fair solution for Palestinian refugees.
● However, recent normalisation agreements between Israel and certain Arab states, such as
the UAE and Bahrain under the Abraham Accords in 2020, have introduced new divisions
within the League.
○ While some members pursue peace with Israel for economic and security gains,
others, including Palestine, strongly oppose recognition without substantial progress
on Palestinian statehood.
What Does the Arab League Do?
The Arab League engages in a variety of initiatives aimed at promoting political, economic, and
security cooperation among its members. However, unlike supranational organisations, it operates

on an intergovernmental, consensus-based model, where decisions are non-binding and require
member states’ approval. This structure respects the sovereignty of its members but often limits
the League’s ability to enforce decisions or take decisive action on contentious issues. As with
ASEAN and NAFTA, it doesn’t have a peacekeeping force.
The Structure of the Arab League:
● Arab League Council: The primary decision-making body, comprising foreign ministers or
leaders from each member state, meets biannually to discuss policy and coordinate actions
on key issues.
● Arab League Secretariat: Headquartered in Cairo, Egypt, the Secretariat handles
administrative functions and supports member states in implementing League policies and
coordinating joint initiatives.
● Specialised Councils: The League includes bodies such as the Arab Economic and Social
Council and the Arab Peace and Security Council, which focus on economic development
and regional security, respectively. Each council is composed of relevant ministers from
member states, who work to harmonise policies and facilitate cooperation on shared goals.
Case Studies of the Arab League’s Work
Case Study – Greater Arab Free Trade Area (GAFTA)
The Greater Arab Free Trade Area (GAFTA), launched in 1997, is a significant economic initiative
aimed at boosting intra-Arab trade by reducing tariffs and trade barriers. Under GAFTA, most
tariffs on goods traded between member states have been eliminated, fostering increased
economic cooperation within the Arab world. As a result, intra-Arab trade grew from
approximately $15 billion in 1997 to over $120 billion in recent years, reflecting GAFTA’s role in
strengthening regional economic ties and enhancing trade flows within the League.
Case Study – Arab League Response to the Syrian Civil War
The Syrian Civil War, which began in 2011, has posed one of the Arab League’s greatest
challenges in terms of regional stability and humanitarian impact. In a rare collective action, the
League suspended Syria’s membership in 2011 due to the government’s violent response to
protests, demonstrating a unified stance on human rights abuses. Despite this move, the League
has struggled to play a direct role in resolving the conflict, as divisions between member states,
especially regarding the role of external actors like Iran and Russia, have limited its
effectiveness. Nonetheless, the suspension marked a significant moment for the League’s
approach to internal conflict and underscored its potential, albeit limited, to address serious
humanitarian crises.
Case Study – Arab League Mediation in the Yemen Conflict
The Arab League has attempted to mediate in Yemen’s civil war, which has severely destabilised
the region and created a humanitarian crisis. The League initially supported diplomatic efforts to
restore Yemen’s internationally recognised government and called for a negotiated settlement.
However, internal disagreements among members—some of whom support different factions
within Yemen—have hindered the League’s ability to take a unified approach. Although it has
raised awareness of the humanitarian crisis and supported international aid, the Arab League’s
influence in the Yemen conflict remains limited due to its intergovernmental nature and lack of
enforcement power.

The Debate About the Arab League
Arguments for the Strengths and Success of the Arab League
Economic Regionalism:
● The Arab League has made strides in economic integration, primarily through GAFTA,
which has encouraged trade by eliminating tariffs on goods traded between member states.
○ This initiative has increased trade within the Arab world, supporting economic
cooperation and building a foundation for future regional economic projects.
● For instance, Jordan’s exports to other Arab League countries have grown significantly
since GAFTA’s implementation, increasing by over 300%, from approximately $500 million
in the late 1990s to over $2 billion today, showcasing the potential of a united economic
front within the region.
Security Regionalism:
● The Arab League has played a key role in coordinating responses to regional security
threats and has occasionally taken unified actions, such as suspending Syria’s membership
during the civil war.
○ Through mechanisms like the Arab Peace and Security Council, the League
provides a forum for addressing shared security issues and coordinating diplomatic
responses.
● The League’s condemnation of foreign intervention in Libya in 2011 showcased its
commitment to regional autonomy and ability to present a united front and represent Arab
states, though its capacity to enforce decisions is limited.
Political Regionalism:
● Politically, the Arab League has championed the Palestinian cause, presenting a unified
stance on this issue in international forums, including at the United Nations, calling for
resolutions that recognise Palestinian rights and oppose Israeli settlement expansion.
○ This collective approach has helped sustain global attention on Palestinian rights
and promoted diplomatic support for a two-state solution.
● By coordinating the foreign policies of its members on this and other issues, the League
has given the Arab world a more cohesive voice in global diplomacy, bolstering its collective
influence.
○ For example, during the Iraq invasion in 2003, the Arab League collectively voiced
opposition to the U.S.-led intervention, emphasising respect for Iraqi sovereignty
and regional stability.
○ This coordinated stance highlighted the League’s commitment to non-interference
and how its collective voice can have more weight than individual countries.
The Arab League’s Unified Response To The Escalating War In Gaza In 2025 - Example Of
How Regionalism Can Enhance The Voice Of Smaller States:
● In March 2025, amid escalating tensions in Gaza, the Arab League convened an
emergency summit in Cairo. At the summit, the Arab League endorsed Egypt’s $53 billion
Gaza Reconstruction Plan.
○ This plan was in response to Trump’s proposal to displace Gaza’s residents and
establish U.S. leadership of the territory, which the Arab League saw as a threat to
Palestinian rights.
○ Egypt’s plan, by contrast, sought to rebuild Gaza whilst not moving its residents or
infringing upon their rights.

● This is a key example of how regional organisations can enhance the influence and voice of
member states on issues highly important to their region. By presenting a unified Arab
voice, Trump’s plan was publicly challenged and the Arab League was able to draw

Arguments for the Weaknesses and Failures of the Arab League
Economic Regionalism:
● Although GAFTA has reduced tariffs, economic integration within the Arab League remains
limited due to non-tariff barriers, varied regulatory standards, and political instability.
○ For instance, customs regulations differ significantly among member states, leading
to delays and inefficiencies in trade. These differences can result in lengthy customs
clearance times, with goods often delayed by days or even weeks at border
crossings.
○ For example, Egyptian exporters face challenges with Algeria’s stringent
documentation requirements and differing standards for imported goods.
● These obstacles restrict GAFTA’s potential and prevent the League from realising a fully
integrated economic bloc like the European Union.
Security Regionalism:
● The Arab League’s security initiatives are hindered by its consensus-based structure, which
makes decisive action challenging in complex conflicts.
○ In the Yemen crisis, the League’s response has been limited, as differing political
alliances among members prevent a unified approach, with some countries
supporting various factions in the conflict.
● This lack of cohesion weakens the League’s effectiveness in addressing internal security
issues and conflicts that impact the entire region.
● Further, the League lacks any real means to enforce its will, unlike the African Union which
has its own peacekeeping force.
Political Regionalism:
● The League’s intergovernmental model, while preserving sovereignty, limits its influence
over member states’ domestic policies and human rights practices.
○ The Arab League has been criticised for inconsistently addressing human rights
abuses within member states, such as the limited response to the situation in
Sudan.
○ While Sudan has experienced severe crises in the past five years, including
violence against protesters and a Civil War, the League’s responses have often
been muted, with statements largely focused on calls for stability rather than direct
condemnation of certain actors or intervention.
● The lack of enforcement mechanisms weakens the League’s credibility in promoting
democratic values and human rights, reducing its influence over internal governance issues
across the region.

North American Free Trade Association (NAFTA)
The Origins, Aims, and Structure of NAFTA
The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) was established in 1994, bringing together
the United States, Canada, and Mexico to form one of the world’s largest free trade zones. Unlike
many regional alliances, NAFTA was solely focused on economic regionalism, aiming exclusively
to reduce trade barriers, increase cross-border investment, and promote economic growth among
its members. It was not a political or security alliance, nor did it create an overarching
organisational structure with decision-making powers. Instead, NAFTA operated purely as an
agreement between sovereign states, with each country maintaining full political autonomy and
responsibility for its own security policies.
NAFTA’s primary objective was to eliminate tariffs on most goods traded among the three
countries. The agreement included mechanisms for regulating trade-related matters, including a
dispute resolution system to address disagreements and protect against unfair trade practices.
Additionally, NAFTA provided guidelines on intellectual property, labour standards, and
environmental regulations to ensure fair practices across borders. However, NAFTA operated
without a centralised authority, with implementation and enforcement primarily managed by each
country’s national institutions. This allowed for a high degree of economic coordination while
maintaining the sovereignty of each member state.
The Impact Of NAFTA
● NAFTA successfully increased trade flows and economic integration between the United
States, Canada, and Mexico.
○ Between 1994 and 2017, trade among the three countries more than tripled,
reaching nearly $1.3 trillion in total trade.
● NAFTA facilitated the growth of supply chains across North America, particularly in
industries such as automotive, agriculture, and manufacturing, as companies could source
parts and materials from any member country without facing tariffs.
○ The agreement also aimed to provide job growth, stimulate consumer spending, and
improve economic conditions across the region.
● However, NAFTA faced criticism for its uneven impact: while it benefited certain industries
and consumer markets, it also led to job losses in some sectors, particularly in the U.S.
manufacturing industry.

NAFTA’s Replacement: The United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement
(USMCA)
In 2018, NAFTA was renegotiated and replaced by the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement
(USMCA).
● Although NAFTA had led to significant increases in trade and economic integration,
criticism grew over its long-term impacts, especially in the United States, where it was
blamed for the loss of manufacturing jobs.
○ These criticisms became prominent during the 2016 U.S. presidential election.
President Trump took an especially strong stance, describing NAFTA as “the worst
trade deal” and arguing that it disadvantaged U.S. workers and incentivised
companies to outsource jobs to Mexico, where labour costs were lower.
● As a result, once in power his administration renegotiated it to address these concerns. In
particular, USMCA included enhanced labour standards requiring Mexico to implement
reforms that would support workers’ rights and reduce the labour cost gap between Mexico
and its northern neighbours.
○ The agreement also introduced new rules in the automotive sector, mandating that
75% of auto content be sourced within North America (up from 62.5% under
NAFTA) and requiring a portion of labour to be compensated at a minimum of $16
per hour.
○ These changes were intended to support U.S. and Canadian manufacturing jobs
and discourage companies from relocating production to Mexico solely for cost
advantages.
● The USMCA took effect in July 2020, replacing NAFTA while retaining many of its core
elements. Although USMCA preserved tariff-free trade for most goods, its updated
standards and reforms sought to promote production in the U.S. and Canada, rather than
just in Mexico.
