How We Measure And Understand Poverty
How Poverty Is Measured
Orthodox Measurement Of Poverty
The orthodox measurement of poverty primarily relies on economic indicators, most commonly
using Gross Domestic Product (GDP). This approach focuses on material deprivation, viewing
poverty as a lack of financial resources.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 3
● The World Bank defines extreme poverty as living on less than $2.15 per day. This
threshold sets a minimum standard of living, below which individuals are unable to meet
their basic needs, such as food, shelter, and clothing.
● By using quantitative economic benchmarks like per capita income, poverty lines, and
gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, the orthodox measurement provides a clear but
narrow understanding of poverty.
○ While useful for international comparisons and trend analysis, this method has been
critiqued for oversimplifying poverty by ignoring non-economic factors like inequality,
access to public services, and social exclusion.
Alternative Measurement Of Poverty
Alternative measurements of poverty take a more holistic view by incorporating social, cultural, and
political dimensions. This recognises that poverty is not just about income but also about lacking
access to essential services and about inequality between different groups in a population.
● The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) was developed by the United Nations
Development Programme (UNDP) and measures poverty by evaluating three key
dimensions, education, health, and living standards, each of which is further broken down
into specific indicators.
○ The education dimension assesses years of schooling and school attendance, the
health dimension considers child mortality and nutrition, and the living standards
dimension examines access to clean water, sanitation, electricity, cooking fuel,
housing, and assets.
○ By identifying whether individuals or households experience deprivation across
multiple dimensions, the MPI provides a more nuanced picture of poverty than
income alone, recognising that people can be poor in various aspects of life, even if
they meet income thresholds.
● These alternative measures aim to highlight the complex nature of poverty, emphasising the
importance of assessing human dignity, capabilities, and opportunities rather than just
financial wealth.
How The UN’s Sustainable Development Goals Measure Poverty
The United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) include a direct focus on poverty,
specifically under Goal 1: “End poverty in all its forms everywhere.” This goal aims to eradicate
extreme poverty for all people by 2030, defined as living on less than $2.15 a day (adjusted for
purchasing power parity).
However, the SDGs also go beyond orthodox income-based measures, recognising that poverty is
multidimensional. Goal 1 is broken down into several specific targets that address various aspects
of poverty. These include:
● Target 1.1: Eradicate extreme poverty for all people everywhere, currently measured as
people living on less than $2.15 a day.
● Target 1.4: Ensure that all men and women, particularly the poor and vulnerable, have
equal rights to economic resources, as well as access to basic services, ownership and
control over land and other forms of property, inheritance, natural resources, appropriate
new technology, and financial services, including microfinance.
● Target 1.5: Build the resilience of the poor and those in vulnerable situations and reduce
their exposure and vulnerability to climate-related extreme events and other economic,
social, and environmental shocks and disasters.

levels, based on pro-poor and gender-sensitive development strategies, to support

The North-South Divide
What Is The North-South Divide?
The North-South Divide was introduced in 1980 by the Brandt Report, commissioned by former
West German Chancellor Willy Brandt, which highlighted the stark inequalities between the “Global
North” (including regions like North America, Europe, Japan, and Australia) and the “Global South”
(encompassing Latin America, Africa, and most of Asia).
● The divide was intended to show how wealth and poverty are unevenly distributed across
the globe, with the Global North being characterised by economic prosperity, advanced
infrastructure, and political stability, while the Global South suffered from widespread
poverty, underdevelopment, and political instability.
● The report sought to promote global cooperation to address this disparity, emphasising the
need for more equitable distribution of resources and development aid to lift the South out
of poverty.
Developments In The Geographical State Of Poverty
Since the North-South Divide concept was introduced in the 1980s, global poverty has evolved
significantly. Some regions of the Global South have made substantial progress, while others have
remained stuck in the cycle of poverty.
The most significant change has been the rise of emerging economies in East Asia and South
Asia, where rapid industrialisation and economic reform have lifted millions out of extreme poverty:
● In 1981, more than 88% of China’s population lived on less than $2.15 a day (the current
global extreme poverty line set by the World Bank), but by 2019, that figure had dropped to
less than 1%.
○ China alone accounted for over 70% of global poverty reduction between 1990 and
2015.
● Vietnam has also made remarkable progress. In 1992, over 52% of Vietnam’s population
lived in extreme poverty, but by 2019, that figure had fallen to less than 2%. The country’s

commitment to economic reforms led to industrialisation, agricultural reforms, and an
expansion of international trade, transforming it into a lower-middle-income economy.
● Similarly, India has made significant strides, with extreme poverty falling from 45% in 1990
to around 11% in 2019, although pockets of deep poverty still exist, particularly in rural
areas and among marginalised communities.
In contrast, Sub-Saharan Africa has seen much slower progress. The region remains the most
impoverished in the world, with over 40% of its population (approximately 550 million people) still
living in extreme poverty as of 2022.
● Countries such as Nigeria, Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), and Madagascar are
among the poorest in the world, despite receiving substantial international aid.
● In fact, Nigeria is projected to have the largest population of people living in extreme
poverty by 2030 (over 15 million), with poverty expected to become increasingly
concentrated in a small number of conflict-affected or fragile states.
Overall, while poverty has decreased globally (extreme poverty rates fell from around 35% in 1990
to just under 9% in 2019) this progress has been highly uneven. Regions like East Asia and
Southeast Asia have seen extraordinary success, while Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South
Asia still face entrenched poverty and underdevelopment. The changing geographic distribution of
poverty complicates the original North-South Divide understanding, as some southern nations have
risen to economic prominence while others remain impoverished.
Is The North-South Divide Still A Useful Way To Understand Global Poverty?
Arguments That It Is Still Useful:
1. The Economic Divide Remains: Despite changes in the global economy, significant
economic inequalities between the Global North and South remain. Wealthier nations
continue to dominate global trade, finance, and technology, while many countries in the
Global South struggle with debt, underdevelopment, and limited industrialisation.
a. In 2022 the Global North accounted for over 60% of global GDP despite
representing less than 20% of the world’s population.
2. The North Dominates Global Governance: The structure of international organisations,
including the IMF and World Bank reflect and reinforce the North-South Divide, as they are
dominated by wealthy Northern countries, especially the US. Further, many countries in the
Global South remain dependent on foreign aid and international development assistance.
This reinforces a dependency relationship that was central to the original concept of the
North-South Divide.
a. In 2022, official development assistance/aid ($44B in total) accounted for 10% of the
region’s government revenues.
3. Climate Change Hits The Global South The Hardest: Poorer Global South nations are
disproportionately affected by environmental degradation and climate change, even though
the Global North is responsible for the majority of historical greenhouse gas emissions.
4. The North Dominates Culture and Education: The Global North continues to dominate in
terms of multinational corporations (MNCs), cultural production and education. Major global
media and entertainment companies, as well as tech giants, are headquartered
predominantly in the North, giving these countries disproportionate control over global
cultural narratives and information flows. The vast majority of the world’s top universities
are also based in North America and Europe.

1. Many Global South Countries Are Now ‘Developed’ : The rise of a number of

the North-South Divide. These countries are deeply integrated into global markets and
often successfully compete with Northern economies. This complictes the idea that the

South’.
a. China is a close second to the US in terms of GDP, whilst India, Brazil, South Korea

b. Further, certain ‘Global North’ countries including Russia seem much more similar to

Theories Of Development
Theories That Argue Global Capitalism Is The Key To The Global South’s
Development
Global capitalism, characterised by free markets, international trade, and competition, is seen by
some as central to the economic growth and development of the Global South. These perspectives
argue that integrating into the global economy through capitalism can help countries in the Global
South achieve prosperity, reduce poverty, and improve living standards. The two main theories that
advocate for this approach are Classical Economic Development Theory and Neo-Classical
Development Theory, both of which emphasise the benefits of capitalism and market forces for the
development of less developed nations.
Classical Economic Development Theory:
● Classical Economic Development Theory, rooted in the ideas of early economists like Adam
Smith, argues that free markets and global trade are the primary engines of development.
○ The theory suggests that when countries in the Global South open their economies
to trade and investment, they can specialise in producing goods where they have a
comparative advantage.
○ This specialisation leads to greater efficiency and productivity, helping countries
grow wealthier over time.
● By engaging in global capitalism, nations can benefit from increased capital flows, access
to technology, and new markets for their products.
● Classical economists believe that minimal government intervention allows market forces to
work most effectively, leading to economic progress and improved living standards. For
countries in the Global South, integrating into the global capitalist system is seen as a key
way to develop their economies and lift populations out of poverty.
Neo-Classical Development Theory:
● Neo-Classical Development Theory builds on the classical ideas of free markets but
focuses more on modern policy approaches that emphasise reducing government
intervention and promoting market-driven growth.
● Neo-classical economists argue that countries in the Global South can develop by
implementing policies that foster free trade, privatisation, and deregulation.
○ These policies are designed to create an environment where businesses can thrive,
encouraging entrepreneurship and innovation.
● By removing trade barriers, countries can access global markets, attract foreign investment,
and benefit from the transfer of technology.
Case Study - The Rise Of East Asian Economies
East Asian economies, particularly China, South Korea, and Vietnam, are often cited as
examples of how embracing global capitalism can drive development, including by the IMF and
World Bank when promoting Structural Adjustment Programmes in Africa and Latin America in
particular in the 1980s and 1990s.
In the late 20th century, these countries transitioned from largely agrarian or centrally planned
economies to rapidly growing, export-driven powerhouses. China’s “Reform and Opening Up”
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 8
policies in the late 1970s, South Korea’s export-led industrialisation following the Korean War,
and Vietnam’s Đổi Mới reforms in 1986 all involved integrating into the global market by
embracing trade, foreign investment, and industrial growth.
This shift allowed these economies to become manufacturing hubs, attract foreign direct
investment, and benefit from the transfer of technology, resulting in remarkable poverty reduction
and economic growth. China lifted over 800 million people out of poverty, while Vietnam’s poverty
rate dropped from 50% in 1992 to less than 6% by 2020.
However, this narrative of success through global capitalism has been questioned due to the
significant role of state action in these countries’ growth. Governments in East Asia did not
simply open their economies to market forces; they actively guided development through
strategic industrial policies, investment in education and infrastructure, and state-led export
strategies. The state’s role in managing and directing economic growth was critical, particularly in
protecting developing industries and steering investment into key sectors.
Theories That Argue Global Capitalism Maintains The Underdevelopment Of The
Global South
Others strongly disagree with Classical Economic Development Theory and Neo-Classical
Development Theory. They instead argue that the global capitalist system creates structural
imbalances, where wealthier nations benefit at the expense of poorer ones. Theories such as
Structural Theory, Dependency Theory, and World-Systems Theory emphasise the exploitative
relationships that exist within the global economy, asserting that capitalism reinforces a cycle of
dependency, poverty and exploitation for the Global South.
Structural Theory:
● Structural Theory, developed in the mid-20th century by economists including Raúl
Prebisch, argues that global capitalism is inherently unequal for developing nations.
○ The theory emphasises the core-periphery relationship, where wealthier,
industrialised countries (the core) dominate global markets, while poorer, less
developed nations (the periphery) are dependent on exporting raw materials.
● As a result, countries in the Global South remain stuck in a cycle of exporting low-value
commodities while importing expensive manufactured goods, keeping them in a state of
underdevelopment.
● Structuralists advocate for import substitution industrialisation (ISI), where countries should
focus on using state intervention to build domestic industries and reduce reliance on global
markets dominated by the Global North.
Dependency Theory:
● Dependency Theory emerged in the 1960s as a response to the failures of classical and
neo-classical economic development models in addressing global inequality.
○ Thinkers such as Andre Gunder Frank and Samir Amin argued that global
capitalism has created a dependency relationship between developed (core) and
developing (periphery) nations.
● This theory suggests that wealthy nations benefit from exploiting the labour and resources
of poorer countries, keeping them in a state of dependency and underdevelopment.
○ The Global South remains reliant on the North for technology, investment, and
markets, while multinational corporations (MNCs) extract wealth from the South,
transferring profits to the North.
● This exploitation hinders industrialisation and development in the Global South,
perpetuating poverty. Dependency theorists argue that true development in the Global
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 9
South can only occur by breaking free from this unequal relationship, through delinking
from the global economy or implementing autonomous development strategies focused on
self-reliance.
World-Systems Theory:
● World-Systems Theory, developed by Immanuel Wallerstein in the 1970s, expands on
Dependency Theory by analysing the entire global capitalist system as a hierarchical
network divided into core, semi-periphery, and periphery regions. According to Wallerstein,
the world economy operates as a single system, where wealth is continually extracted from
the periphery to benefit the core.
○ In this system, the semi-periphery serves as a buffer zone, exhibiting both core and
periphery characteristics, but the periphery is trapped in a cycle of low-wage labour,
commodity exports, and economic dependence.
● World-Systems Theory asserts that capitalism, by its very nature, perpetuates global
inequality and prevents meaningful development in the Global South.
○ The core nations maintain their dominance through the control of global trade,
finance, and technology, leaving peripheral countries with limited opportunities for
upward mobility.
○ This theory suggests that until the global capitalist system itself is transformed, the
underdevelopment of the Global South will persist.
Case Study – The Continued Underdevelopment of Sub-Saharan Africa
Sub-Saharan Africa provides a powerful example of how global capitalism has maintained the
underdevelopment of the Global South. Despite being rich in natural resources like oil, minerals,
and agricultural products, many countries in the region have remained economically stagnant or
dependent on foreign aid, whilst multinational corporations benefit from the export of their raw
materials.
Nigeria is one of the world’s largest oil producers, yet over 40% of its population still lives in
extreme poverty, with much of the oil wealth benefiting foreign corporations and local elites.
Similarly, the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), which has some of the world’s richest
deposits of cobalt and copper, saw over 70% of its population living on less than $2.15 a day in
2022, while multinational mining companies continue to dominate the industry.
The resource curse, where countries rich in natural resources experience poor development
outcomes, has left many Sub-Saharan nations dependent on the export of raw materials and
vulnerable to global price fluctuations. During the 2014-2016 global oil price crash, Nigeria’s
government revenues dropped by 70%, plunging the country into recession, which highlights how
dependence on a single resource can destabilise economies.
Moreover, Structural Adjustment Programs (SAPs) implemented by the IMF and World Bank
during the 1980s and 1990s, which promoted free-market reforms and decreased state
intervention, led to severe economic dislocation and social unrest in many African countries.
These programs sought to integrate African economies into the global capitalist system and
required African governments to cut public spending, privatise state-owned enterprises, and open
markets to foreign investment. The result was the dismantling of social services, increased debt,
and the concentration of wealth in the hands of elites, exacerbating inequality.
In Zambia, SAPs led to the privatisation of its copper mines, resulting in widespread job losses
and increasing poverty rates; by 1996, 68% of Zambians were living in poverty, up from 49% a
decade earlier. The reduction of state spending also led to the collapse of health and education
services across the continent, with infant mortality rates rising in countries like Ghana during this
period.

The Role Of Civil Society and Non-State Actors In Addressing
Conflict and Poverty
Non-state actors, including NGOs, multinational corporations, charitable foundations, and
celebrities, have become increasingly important in efforts to address global conflict and poverty.
These actors work independently or alongside governments and international organisations to
provide humanitarian aid, advocate for policy change, and promote sustainable development.
Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs)
NGOs such as Oxfam and Save the Children operate independently of governments and focus
on humanitarian and development goals, but they often work in close collaboration with IGOs
such as the UN, WHO and World Bank. These partnerships allow NGOs to expand their reach
and access resources for larger-scale operations. For example, Doctors Without Borders
collaborates with the WHO in the coordination of global health responses during epidemics, as
seen during the 2014 Ebola outbreak in West Africa. The WHO provided technical guidance and
logistics support, while Doctors Without Borders delivered direct medical care on the ground,
operating treatment centers in Liberia, Sierra Leone, and Guinea.
One of their key benefits is their ability to respond more quickly and flexibly to conflict and
disaster situations than governments or IGOs. Their ability to operate independently allows them
to bypass bureaucratic delays and provide immediate medical care, food, and shelter to those
affected by crises. For example, during the Syrian Civil War, Doctors Without Borders set up field
hospitals and mobile clinics in hard-to-reach areas, delivering life-saving care to civilians caught
in the conflict.
On the other hand, though, NGOs have faced criticism for a lack of accountability and
transparency in their operations. Oxfam faced a scandal in 2018 when it was revealed that some
of its aid workers had engaged in sexual exploitation while working in Haiti.
NGOs have also been criticised for sometimes reinforcing dependency or exacerbating conflict.
For example, in South Sudan, the influx of international NGOs providing aid has, in some cases,
undermined the capacity of local institutions to develop sustainable solutions. The presence of so
many external actors has led to a parallel system of governance, where local governments and
communities become reliant on international assistance rather than building their own resilience.
Venezuela’s Anti-NGO Law - Example Of How Non-State Actors’ Effectiveness In Tackling
Contemporary Global Issues Can Be Limited By States:
● In November 2024, the Venezuelan government passed a law widely referred to as the
Anti-NGO Law. The law requires all NGOs to obtain explicit state approval to operate and
imposes tight restrictions on their activities, foreign funding, and public advocacy work.
○ As a result, prominent international NGOs including Acción Solidaria, which
provided HIV treatment and healthcare services to vulnerable communities, and
Transparency International, which worked on anti-corruption, were forced to
drastically scale back their operations in Venezuela.

● This shows how non-state actors can be limited in their ability to tackle major global
challenges like poverty, climate change and human rights as governments have state
sovereignty and can use it to restrict civil society and the effectiveness of NGOs.
Oxfam CEO’s Support For Decolonising Aid - How This Supports The View That
International NGOs Are Ineffective In Tackling Contemporary Global Issues:
● In 2025 Oxfam GB’s new CEO, Halima Begum, publicly acknowledged that the traditional
model of international NGOs often do more damage than they do help, as they reinforce
dependency in the Global South by undermining the ability of countries to develop their
own support structures and strong local organisations.
○ As a result, she called for a shift towards giving more funding and decision making
power to local organisations.
● This is an important argument for the ineffectiveness of international non-state actors in
tackling issues such as climate change and poverty, as they prevent the growth of
effective local capacity even if they are well intentioned.
Charitable Foundations
Philanthropic foundations, such as the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, Ford Foundation, and
Open Society Foundations, play a crucial role in funding large-scale, long-term projects aimed at
reducing poverty, promoting peace, and addressing global inequality. These foundations are
often equipped with vast financial resources, enabling them to tackle complex and systemic
issues at a scale that many other organisations cannot. For instance, the Bill & Melinda Gates
Foundation has committed over $50 billion since its inception to initiatives focusing on global
health, agricultural development, and education, particularly in low-income regions. It has been
instrumental in financing programs such as the Global Polio Eradication Initiative, which has
brought the world to the brink of eradicating polio, as well as GAVI, the Vaccine Alliance, which
provides life-saving vaccines to children in developing countries, dramatically reducing mortality
rates.
These philanthropic foundations often partner with governments, international organisations, and
NGOs to scale up their impact and ensure that their programs address the root causes of poverty
and conflict. The Ford Foundation, for example, focuses on addressing systemic inequality by
funding initiatives that promote human rights, democratic governance, and sustainable economic
development. One of its key programs has been supporting microfinance initiatives in countries
like Bangladesh and India, which empower women and marginalised communities by providing
access to credit for small businesses.
Whilst they can be beneficial in providing funding for long-term development, Charitable
Foundations and the NGOs they fund have been criticised for creating dependence on charitable
funding and support, rather than strengthening the capacity of local governments.
Multinational Corporations (MNCs)
MNCs can influence poverty reduction and conflict resolution through corporate social
responsibility (CSR) initiatives, investments in local economies, and job creation. They often have
a profound impact on communities where they operate, both negative and positive. MNCs are
increasingly expected to address social and environmental challenges as part of their CSR
commitments.
Nestlé has developed its Nestlé Cocoa Plan and Nespresso AAA Sustainable Quality™ Program,
which focus on improving the livelihoods of cocoa and coffee farmers in countries like Ivory
Coast, Ghana, and Colombia. These initiatives aim to increase the productivity of small-scale
farmers by providing technical assistance, quality inputs, and training in sustainable agricultural

practices. Additionally, Nestlé has invested in education programs and community infrastructure,
helping to tackle child labour in the cocoa industry and improve access to healthcare and

While MNCs’ CSR initiatives can have positive impacts, they are often criticised for not
addressing deeper, systemic issues such as labor exploitation and environmental degradation
within their global supply chains. For example, despite Nestlé’s initiatives to improve conditions
for cocoa farmers, the company has faced ongoing criticism for failing to fully eliminate child
labour in its supply chains in West Africa. Additionally, some CSR programs are seen as
“greenwashing,” where companies promote socially responsible images without making
substantial changes to their core business practices.
In some cases, MNCs’ operations have contributed to resource depletion, pollution, and social
displacement, exacerbating poverty and conflict in vulnerable regions, especially when profits are
prioritised over long-term sustainability and fair labor practices.
Celebrities and Social Movements
Celebrities and social movements play a significant role in raising awareness about global
conflicts, poverty, and social justice. Prominent figures like Angelina Jolie, Emma Watson, and
Bono have used their platforms to advocate for issues such as refugee rights, gender equality,
and debt relief for developing countries. Angelina Jolie, as a Special Envoy for the UNHCR, has
traveled to war-torn regions like Syria, Iraq, and Afghanistan to highlight the plight of refugees
and internally displaced persons, helping to drive international attention and resources to these
crises. Emma Watson, as a UN Women Goodwill Ambassador, has spearheaded the HeForShe
campaign, which promotes gender equality and engages men in the fight for women’s rights.
In addition to these individual efforts, social movements can amplify the work of NGOs and
create momentum for policy change. For instance, the #MeToo movement, which gained
international attention after high-profile figures like Alyssa Milano and Ashley Judd shared their
experiences of sexual harassment, has sparked global conversations on women’s rights,
gender-based violence, and workplace inequality. These movements not only raise awareness
but also encourage grassroots activism, pushing governments and institutions to address
deep-rooted social issues.
However, there are criticisms that celebrity advocacy can sometimes oversimplify complex issues
or result in “slacktivism,” where public engagement is limited to symbolic gestures like sharing
posts on social media rather than driving substantial change. Moreover, celebrities can
occasionally overshadow the voices of those most affected by poverty and conflict, diverting
attention from local activists and experts who may be more deeply connected to the issues.
