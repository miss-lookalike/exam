The World Trade Organisation (WTO)
The Origins and Aims Of The WTO
The World Trade Organisation (WTO) was established on January 1, 1995, as a successor to the
General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), which was created in 1947 to promote
international trade by reducing trade barriers such as tariffs and quotas. Today, the WTO has 164
member countries and represents 98% of global trade.
The WTO’s primary aim is to promote free trade, ensuring that global trade flows as
smoothly, predictably, and freely as possible. It achieves this by providing a forum for
negotiating trade agreements, settling disputes between its members, and promoting compliance
with trade rules.
The WTO operates on the principle of non-discrimination, meaning that member countries should
not discriminate between their trading partners (the “most-favored-nation” principle) and should
not favour domestic goods over foreign goods (the “national treatment” principle). This system
aims to prevent protectionism, promote fair competition, and facilitate global trade by reducing the
risk of trade disputes or imbalances between countries.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 3
● The “most-favored-nation” principle means that no country can offer special trade
advantages, such as lower tariffs or preferential treatment, to one trading partner without
extending the same conditions to all other WTO members.
● The “national treatment” principle means that, once a foreign good has entered a country’s
market, it must be treated the same as domestically produced goods, ensuring a level
playing field for international trade.
What Does The WTO Do?
1. It Facilitates Trade Negotiations
The WTO provides a multilateral platform where member countries can come together to
negotiate trade agreements. These negotiations focus on reducing tariffs, quotas, and other trade
barriers to make international trade more open and accessible. By lowering these barriers, the
WTO aims to increase trade flows, which can drive economic growth and development.
Case Study – Bali Package (2013)
The Bali Trade Facilitation Agreement was a landmark success and the first comprehensive trade
agreement approved by the WTO in two decades. It focused on reducing red tape and simplifying
customs procedures globally. The agreement was designed to cut the time and cost associated
with trade, particularly benefitting developing countries by reducing bureaucratic barriers. The
Bali Package is estimated to reduce global trade costs by 14.3% on average, helping small
businesses and landlocked countries participate more effectively in international trade.
Member states can also negotiate free-trade agreements with each other rather than with all WTO
member states:
● Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP):
This agreement, involving 11 countries including Japan, Australia, and Canada, was formed
in 2018, reducing trade barriers and covering nearly 13.4% of the global economy. The UK
joined in 2023.
● UK-Australia Free Trade Agreement (2021): After Brexit, the United Kingdom negotiated
a bilateral free-trade agreement with Australia, reducing tariffs and increasing market
access between the two nations.
● African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA): Launched in 2019, AfCFTA aims to
create the largest free trade area in the world, covering 54 African countries, promoting
intra-African trade by lowering tariffs across the continent.
If no trade deal is in place between countries, member states must trade under WTO rules and
therefore aim to reduce tariffs and promote free trade as much as possible.
2. It Settles Disputes
The WTO’s Dispute Settlement Body (DSB) provides a structured mechanism for resolving trade
disputes between member countries. When one country believes another is violating WTO
agreements, it can bring the issue before the DSB for resolution.
● This process helps avoid unilateral retaliatory actions such as raising tariffs for a particular
country, which could escalate into broader trade conflicts or even trade wars. Instead, the
DSB provides a neutral platform where countries can resolve their disagreements based on
agreed international trade rules.
● The Appellate Body, a critical part of the dispute settlement process, reviews appeals
against the decisions made by dispute settlement panels. If a country is found in violation, it

must either comply with the ruling or face possible sanctions such as retaliatory tariffs
imposed by the affected party.
Successful Case Study - Brazil vs. United States (Cotton Subsidies, 2002-2009)
Brazil argued that the United States provided illegal subsidies to its cotton farmers, violating
WTO agreements and distorting global cotton prices, harming Brazilian farmers. The DSB ruled
in favour of Brazil in 2004, and after appeals, the WTO confirmed that the U.S. had violated
international trade rules. The U.S. agreed to pay $300 million in compensation to Brazil and
reform its agricultural policies to bring them in line with WTO rules.
Unsuccessful Case Study - US-China Trade War (2018-Present)
In 2018, the United States imposed tariffs on $360 billion worth of Chinese goods, accusing
China of unfair trade practices, including intellectual property theft and subsidies to state-owned
enterprises. China retaliated by imposing tariffs on US exports, including agricultural goods like
soybeans, leading to a significant escalation in the trade conflict.
The US-China trade war largely bypassed formal WTO dispute mechanisms. Both countries
engaged in tariffs that led to a sharp decline in bilateral trade. The two nations sought resolution
and signed the Phase One Trade Agreement in January 2020, which required China to increase
purchases of U.S. goods by $200 billion over two years. However, China failed to meet its
purchasing targets, and many of the underlying issues remained unresolved.
In 2020, China took its grievances to the WTO, and in 2021, the WTO ruled that the US tariffs
violated international trade rules. However, the US rejected the ruling, arguing that the WTO was
ill-equipped to handle modern trade issues related to China’s state-controlled economic
practices. No meaningful resolution was reached through the WTO, and tariffs remained in place.
India’s Digital Trade Tariffs - Example Of The Failure Of The WTO To Uphold State
Sovereignty:
● In late 2023, the World Trade Organisation’s Dispute Settlement Body (DSB) ruled that
India’s tariffs on imported digital services, including cloud storage and software, violated the
WTO’s free trade rules. These tariffs had been challenged by the EU, which argued they
unfairly restricted access to India’s digital market.
○ However, India failed to comply with the ruling and hasn’t removed the tariffs. India
claimed that the tariffs were necessary to protect national sovereignty and support
their domestic tech industry.
○ In order to justify their lack of implementation of the ruling, India appealed the
decision to the WTO’s Appellate Body in 2024, which they knew wasn’t currently
functioning. The EU called this ‘appealing into the void’.
● This is a clear example of the limitations of the WTO. Powerful member states frequently
ignore its rulings and the organisation can’t enforce the rulings due to national sovereignty.
This is compounded by the fact that the Appellate Body isn’t currently working due to
gridlock and the U.S. blocking appointments.
The United States Continues To Block Appointments To The WTO’s Appellate Body - Example
Of The Limitations Of The WTO Due To State Sovereignty:
● Since 2019, the United States has blocked the appointment of new judges to the WTO’s
Appellate Body, arguing that it has engaged in judicial overreach, especially in cases that
relate to U.S. tariffs and alleged breaking of WTO rules.

○ As a result, the body continues to be completely non-functional and this has left a
number of trade disputes unresolved and WTO rules not followed, as appeals
against decisions made by the WTO’s Dispute Resolution Body (DSB) can’t be

○ For example, In January 2025, Panama notified its decision to appeal a WTO panel

United States Pauses Financial Contributions To The WTO - Example Of Trump’s Nationalism
Undermining Economic Global Governance:
● In March 2025, the U.S. announced it would pause its financial contributions to the World
Trade Organisation’s budget, citing concerns that the WTO no longer served the economic
interests of the United States.
○ This poses a significant risk to the WTO, which has begun to let staff go as a result,
because the U.S. is its biggest funder and normally contributes over 10% of its
budget.
● This is a clear example of how the Trump Administration’s nationalism and move away from
seeing free trade as aligned with U.S. interests poses a major challenge to the existing
institutions of economic global governance.
○ The WTO was established by the United States and is very dependent on its
support and willingness to accept its authority, showing how sovereignty can
undermine international institutions.
The Debate About The WTO
Arguments for the Strengths and Success of the WTO
The WTO is credited with playing a pivotal role in promoting global trade, resolving disputes, and
supporting both developed and developing countries in navigating the complexities of international
commerce. Advocates argue that the WTO’s framework has been crucial in fostering economic
growth, preventing trade conflicts, and ensuring that the voices of developing nations are
represented on the global stage.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 6
The WTO Has Successfully Promoted Free Trade and Economic Growth:
● Since the WTO’s creation in 1995, it has played a significant role in facilitating multilateral
trade agreements that have helped open markets and boost global trade. The WTO has
successfully promoted trade liberalisation across various sectors, ranging from agriculture
to technology.
○ In the decades following the establishment of the WTO, the volume of world
merchandise trade has more than tripled, growing from $5 trillion in 1995 to over
$22 trillion in 2021.
● The Information Technology Agreement (ITA), signed in 1996, led to the elimination of tariffs
on a wide range of high-tech products, helping to spur rapid growth in global technology
markets.
● The Bali Trade Facilitation Agreement (2013) successfully reduced red tape and simplified
customs procedures globally. It is estimated to reduce global trade costs by 14.3% on
average, helping small businesses and landlocked countries participate more effectively in
international trade.
The WTO Successfully Resolves Disputes and Prevents Trade Wars:
● The WTO’s Dispute Settlement Body (DSB) plays a critical role in managing trade conflicts
between member states, offering a neutral and legally-binding process for resolving
disputes.
○ This prevents countries from engaging in unilateral retaliatory actions, which could
escalate into broader trade conflicts or even trade wars.
○ Successful cases such as Brazil vs. United States cotton subsidies dispute
(2002-2009) showcase how the WTO has been effective in mediating complex
issues and ensuring that trade rules are upheld.
● By enforcing agreed-upon rules and offering a platform for legal resolution, the WTO has
been instrumental in maintaining global trade stability and protecting the interests of smaller
economies that may not have the power to negotiate on their own.
The WTO Isn’t Dominated by Developed Countries and Supports Developing Countries:
● The WTO operates on a “one country, one vote” system, ensuring that it isn’t dominated by
developed, Western countries like the IMF and World Bank.
○ This provides a critical platform for developing countries to influence global trade
policies, allowing them to negotiate terms that benefit their own economic interests.
● One of the most significant examples of the WTO supporting developing countries is the
case of China. Since joining the WTO in 2001, China experienced a period of
unprecedented economic growth. By gaining greater market access through reduced tariffs
and being able to challenge discriminatory trade practices within the WTO framework,
China has become one of the world’s leading exporters, particularly in sectors such as
electronics, machinery, and textiles.
○ Its integration into the global trading system helped China boost its exports, attract
foreign investment, and grow its GDP at an average rate of 9% per year in the first
decade after accession.
○ Between 2001 and 2020, China’s GDP grew from approximately $1.3 trillion to
$14.7 trillion, making it the second-largest economy in the world.
● Further, through initiatives like the Aid for Trade program, the WTO offers technical
assistance and capacity-building programs to help developing nations engage more
effectively in international trade. This has been particularly beneficial for countries needing
help to build infrastructure, enhance trade policy, and boost export capacity.

Arguments for the Weaknesses and Failures of the WTO
While the WTO has been pivotal in shaping global trade, the organisation faces challenges in
reaching new multilateral agreements, enforcing its rulings, and addressing the disproportionate
influence of wealthier nations in trade negotiations.
The WTO Has Had Limited Success in Agreeing Multilateral Trade Agreements:
● One of the major criticisms of the WTO is its inability to secure comprehensive multilateral
trade agreements in recent years.
● The Doha Development Round, launched in 2001, aimed to reduce trade barriers and
address inequalities between developed and developing countries. However, after years of
negotiations, it has largely stalled due to disagreements over agricultural subsidies,
intellectual property, and market access.
○ This failure has diminished the WTO’s credibility as a platform for multilateral
cooperation, pushing many countries to pursue bilateral or regional trade
agreements instead, such as the Trans-Pacific Partnership (TPP) and the Regional
Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), which operate outside the WTO
framework.
● As a result, critics argue that the WTO is struggling to maintain its relevance in an era
where countries prefer to bypass its slow negotiation process in favor of quicker, more
focused deals.
The WTO Is Often Unable to Address Major Global Trade Disputes and Its Rulings Are Often
Rejected:
● Although the Dispute Settlement Body (DSB) is one of the WTO’s key mechanisms, its
enforcement ability has been shown to be weak in recent years.
○ Over 20% of trade disputes brought to the WTO in the last decade remain
unresolved.
● High-profile disputes, such as the US-China trade war, illustrate how countries have
increasingly bypassed WTO dispute resolution mechanisms in favor of unilateral actions
like tariffs, which contradict WTO rules.
○ In 2020, the WTO ruled that US tariffs on $360 billion of Chinese goods violated
global trading rules, but the United States rejected the ruling, further undermining
the organisation’s ability to resolve disputes. The Appellate Body, which reviews
appeals, has also been hampered by the US blocking new appointments, rendering
it unable to fully function.
● This erosion of trust in the WTO’s ability to enforce rulings has led to a rise in trade conflicts
and decreased compliance with its rules, diminishing its role as an arbitrator in global trade.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 8
Trade Negotiations Are Still Dominated by Wealthier Nations:
● While the WTO technically operates on a “one country, one vote” principle, in practice,
wealthier nations, particularly the United States and European Union, exert disproportionate
influence over trade negotiations.
● Many developing countries struggle to have their voices heard, lacking the resources and
negotiating power to fully engage in complex, multi-layered trade discussions.
○ For example, during the Doha Round, developed countries pushed for greater
access to developing markets while resisting efforts to cut agricultural subsidies that
harm poorer nations’ farmers.
○ Additionally, issues critical to developing countries, such as technology transfer and
flexible intellectual property rights, are often sidelined in favor of trade rules that
benefit advanced economies.
● This imbalance has led to the perception that the WTO primarily serves the interests of the
Global North, despite its claims to support equitable development.
The G7 and the G20
The G7 (Group of Seven) and G20 (Group of Twenty) are informal forums where the world’s major
economies come together to discuss and coordinate on global economic issues. Unlike IGOs such
as the UN and WTO, which have formal structures and legal frameworks, the G7 and G20 are
informal forums for dialogue and cooperation. They do not have binding decision-making power or
permanent secretariats and they rely more on diplomatic discussions and non-binding agreements.
The Group of Seven (G7)
The Origins and Development Of The G7 Over Time
The G7 was established in the 1970s as an informal forum for the world’s largest advanced
economies to discuss global economic and political issues. Originally known as the G6 when it was
founded in 1975 (with the United States, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Japan, and Italy),
Canada joined in 1976, creating the G7.
The G7 also became the G8 when Russia joined in 1998, but following Russia’s annexation of
Crimea in 2014, its membership was suspended, reverting the group back to the G7. The
European Union also enjoys full membership rights, but doesn’t host meetings.
The Structure Of The G7
● Unlike formal IGOs like the UN and WTO, the G7 lacks binding powers and does not have
a permanent secretariat. Instead, it functions as a forum for dialogue, where agreements
reached are based on political consensus rather than enforceable commitments.
● The G7 meets annually for leaders’ summits to discuss key global challenges. The
presidency of the G7 rotates annually among the member countries, and the host country
sets the agenda for the year.
○ The summits are attended by the leaders of the G7 countries, with additional
meetings held throughout the year by ministers, such as the finance ministers or
foreign ministers of the member nations.
● The G7’s power comes from the economic and political weight of its member countries,
which collectively represent nearly 50% of global GDP. While it does not enforce policies
directly, the G7’s influence shapes global economic governance, often setting the agenda
for formal institutions like the IMF, World Bank, or G20.

What Does the G7 Do?
The G6’s original aim was to address the economic instability caused by the 1973 oil crisis, and the
global recession that followed. Over time, the G7 has met regularly to address the key economic
issues that face them. It plays a very important role in responding to financial crises, such as
during the 2008 global financial crisis when G7 leaders worked closely to coordinate monetary
policy responses and prevent a deeper global economic collapse.
With the Russian Invasion of Ukraine and the growth of China as a strategic competitor to the US,
the G7 is an increasingly important forum for discussing security concerns and coordinating to
protect the interests of the West. Further, the G7 focuses on global challenges including climate
change.
Case Study – The G7’s Response to the COVID-19 Pandemic
During the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, the G7 demonstrated its importance as a global forum for
crisis management by coordinating economic and public health responses. G7 members,
representing the world’s largest economies, came together to address both the health and
economic impacts of the pandemic. This included pledges of over $20 billion to support the
development and distribution of vaccines, treatments, and diagnostics through mechanisms like
the COVAX initiative, aimed at ensuring equitable vaccine distribution.
The G7 Finance Ministers also worked closely to provide economic support to nations struggling
with the fallout from the pandemic. They led efforts to suspend debt repayments for the world’s
poorest countries through the Debt Service Suspension Initiative (DSSI), providing immediate
relief and freeing up funds for nations to focus on healthcare and social welfare.
Case Study – The G7’s Role in Climate Change Initiatives
At the 2021 G7 Summit, hosted in Cornwall by the UK, the group reaffirmed its commitment to
tackling climate change by strengthening its previous pledges under the Paris Agreement. The
G7 nations collectively committed to achieving net-zero carbon emissions by 2050 and pledged
to cut emissions by nearly 50% by 2030.
One of the key outcomes of the summit was the renewed commitment to provide $100 billion
annually in climate finance to help developing countries adapt to and mitigate the effects of
climate change. This funding aims to support the transition to renewable energy, protect
biodiversity, and build resilience against environmental disasters.
The G7 also launched the Build Back Better World (B3W) initiative, a response to China’s Belt
and Road Initiative, to invest in infrastructure in developing countries. B3W focuses on
sustainable and green infrastructure projects, ensuring that developing nations can transition to
renewable energy and meet their climate goals.
Case Study – The G7’s Agreement On Support For Ukraine At The 2024 Summit
At the G7 Summit held in Apulia, Italy in June 2024, the group agreed to provide Ukraine with
$50 billion in loans to support its war effort versus Russia; a key aim of the alliance of Western
states that the G7 represents. These loans were backed by profits from frozen Russian assets
held in bank accounts in G7 countries, the first time this had been done.
This is a good example of how the G7 is important, as it allows world leaders with major power to
come together and make key decisions to promote the interests of the West against key
challengers like Russia and China.

The Group of 20 (G20)
The Origins and Development of the G20 Over Time
The G20 was established in 1999 in the aftermath of the 1997 Asian financial crisis as a forum for
finance ministers and central bank governors from major economies to discuss global financial
issues. The original purpose was to bring together both developed and emerging economies to
improve global economic governance. The G20 was expanded in 2008 to include heads of state,
due to the importance of a coordinated response to the 2008 global financial crisis.
The G20’s membership includes 19 countries plus the European Union, representing a mix of the
world’s largest advanced and emerging economies. Collectively, G20 members account for over
80% of global GDP, 75% of international trade, and nearly two-thirds of the world’s population. Its
broad representation allows the G20 to address a wide range of global challenges, from financial
crises to development and climate change, giving it a more inclusive focus than the G7, which
primarily includes advanced economies.
The Structure of the G20
The G20 is an informal forum with no permanent secretariat or headquarters, similar to the G7, but
it includes a broader set of member states. Its presidency rotates annually among its members,
with the host country setting the agenda for the year’s meetings. The G20 holds an annual summit
where leaders of member countries, along with their finance ministers and central bank governors,
meet to discuss and coordinate responses to key global economic challenges.
In addition to the annual summits, the G20 also organises several ministerial meetings on topics
such as finance, trade, development, and climate throughout the year. These meetings are
attended by ministers and senior officials who collaborate on global policy coordination.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 11
What Does the G20 Do?
The G20 plays a critical role in global economic governance, focusing on issues such as financial
stability, economic growth, climate change, and development. Its most prominent contribution has
been coordinating global responses to financial crises. During the 2008 global financial crisis, G20
leaders met for the first time at the Washington Summit to coordinate a global response, leading to
policies such as fiscal stimulus and banking reforms that helped prevent a deeper global recession.
Since then, the G20 has expanded its scope to tackle challenges like climate change, global
inequality, and sustainable development. The G20 also emphasises improving financial regulation,
promoting trade, and enhancing international tax cooperation, particularly in addressing tax
avoidance by multinational corporations.
The G20 is also often the place where important bilateral meetings between world leaders take
place, such during the G20 meeting in Bali, when US President Biden met with Chinese President
Xi and they promised there would be no ‘new Cold War’.
Case Study – The G20’s Response to the 2008 Global Financial Crisis
The 2008 financial crisis marked a turning point for the G20, elevating its role in global economic
governance. The crisis began in the U.S. housing market but quickly spread globally, leading to a
severe recession and financial instability. At the Washington Summit in November 2008, G20
leaders coordinated a global response, pledging $5 trillion in stimulus packages to prevent further
economic collapse. The summit led to the establishment of the Financial Stability Board (FSB),
which strengthened global financial regulations to prevent future crises. The G20’s collective
efforts, including reforms to banking regulations and capital requirements, are credited with
stabilising the global economy and fostering recovery.
Case Study – G20 Focus on International Tax Reform (2021)
The issue of tax avoidance by multinational corporations has been a long-standing concern,
particularly for developing countries that lose revenue due to tax evasion. In 2021, G20 finance
ministers and central bank governors agreed to a landmark global minimum corporate tax rate of
15%. This agreement, led by the OECD and endorsed by G20 leaders at the Rome Summit,
aims to prevent multinational companies from shifting profits to low-tax jurisdictions, ensuring that
corporations pay a fair share of taxes where they operate. The tax agreement is seen as a
significant step toward addressing tax inequality, as it will generate additional tax revenues for
countries around the world, particularly benefiting economies that have been undercut by tax
avoidance schemes.
The 2024 G20 Summit in Rio de Janeiro:
Example Of How The G20 Promotes The Interests Of The Global South:
● The G20’s November 2024 summit in Rio de Janeiro is a good example of how the G20
can be seen as an effective forum for promoting the interests of the Global South and world
as a whole, rather than just the interests of the West which the G7 is often accused of.
○ This is one of the key strengths of the G20, as it includes both developing and
developed countries, representing 80% of global GDP in total.
● The summit formally included the African Union as a permanent member of the G20 for the
first time, launched a multilateral initiative titled ‘the Global Alliance Against Hunger and
Poverty’ and backed increased infrastructure investment in developing countries.
○ This included a $2.89 billion deal between Brazil’s development bank (BNDES) and
the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) to support projects in transport,
energy, and sanitation in Brazil.

Example Of The Failure Of The G20 To Address Global Crises:
● At the G20’s November 2024 summit in Rio de Janeiro, the group was unable to agree on
any joint sanctions against Russia or even a statement that condemned its invasion of
Ukraine, as attempts by Western countries to do so were blocked by China and Russia.
● This is a clear example of the limitations of the G20 in addressing major global crises. As
the group of the world’s largest economies includes both Western states and authoritarian

The Debate About The G7 and G20
Arguments That The G7 and G20 Are Important and Effective
1. They promote economic stability and manage crises: Both the G7 and G20 play a
crucial role in coordinating responses to global economic challenges. During the 2008
global financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, they acted quickly to stabilise the global
economy. Their role also extends beyond economic crises. In the 2024 G7 summit, the
group called for an immediate ceasefire in Gaza and increased humanitarian aid,
highlighting their role in geopolitical crisis management.
2. They are a key forum for cooperation among key decision makers: The G7 and G20
provide a critical platform for cooperation between the world’s most influential political and
economic leaders who have the real decision-making power. These informal summits bring
together heads of state, finance ministers, and central bank governors from the world’s
largest economies, facilitating direct communication and negotiation. This structure allows
for swift consensus on global challenges, ensuring that agreements reached at these
meetings have real influence on global policy decisions and lead to coordinated action
across nations.
3. The G20 allows for more inclusive cooperation: The G20, in particular, is effective
because it includes both developed and developing economies, representing over 80% of
global GDP and 75% of international trade. This broad representation allows for more
inclusive discussions on key global challenges like trade, development, and climate
change, ensuring a more balanced global economic system.
Arguments That The G7 and G20 Are Unimportant and Ineffective
1. They are limited as agreements are non-binding: Both the G7 and G20 are informal
forums that lack the authority to enforce binding decisions. Agreements made at these
summits are often non-binding, leading to minimal real-world impact. For example, while
the G20 has committed to addressing climate change and other global challenges, many of
these promises remain unfulfilled due to a lack of mechanisms to ensure compliance.
Without enforceable commitments, these meetings often result in broad declarations with
little follow-through.
2. They have been ineffective in resolving recent global crises: Despite their focus on
global economic and geopolitical issues, the G7 and G20 have been ineffective in resolving
key global crises in recent years. During the US-China trade war, both groups failed to
mediate effectively, allowing tensions to escalate and cause significant disruptions to the
global economy. Similarly, the inability of the G20 to coordinate a strong, united response to
the Russia-Ukraine war demonstrates limitations in crisis management, as China and
Russia opposed sanctions on Russia.
3. They exclude the vast majority of countries which are poorer: The G7 and G20 are
exclusive forums, representing only a fraction of the world’s nations. The G7, for example,
includes only seven highly industrialized countries, while the G20 excludes over 80% of the
