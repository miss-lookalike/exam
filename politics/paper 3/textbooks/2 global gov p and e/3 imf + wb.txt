The International Monetary Fund (IMF)
The Origins And Aims Of The IMF
The International Monetary Fund (IMF) was established in 1944 during the Bretton Woods
Conference, held in New Hampshire, USA, as part of a broader effort to create a stable global
financial system after the devastation of the Second World War. The key aim of the IMF was to
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 3
rebuild and bring stability to the global economy to prevent crises similar to the Great Depression
of the interwar period.
The IMF came into formal existence in 1945, with 29 member countries signing the Articles of
Agreement, and its official operations began in 1947. Today, the IMF has 190 members states and
a number of key aims:
1. Promote International Monetary Cooperation: The IMF fosters collaboration on
monetary policies and exchange rates to enhance stability and reduce financial volatility.
2. Facilitate Global Trade: By promoting currency stability and offering policy advice, the IMF
supports trade growth, which leads to higher living standards and employment.
3. Ensure Exchange Rate Stability: The IMF works to maintain stable exchange rates and
prevent competitive devaluations that could trigger trade conflicts.
4. Provide Financial Assistance: The IMF offers loans to countries facing balance of
payments problems, typically requiring economic reforms in return.
5. Reduce Global Poverty: Over time, the IMF has focused more on supporting developing
countries, offering financial aid and policy guidance to enhance economic stability and
reduce poverty.
How The IMF Promotes Neoliberalism
The IMF promotes economic policies rooted in neoliberal economic theory, emphasising free
markets, fiscal discipline, and monetary stability as drivers of growth and development. Key
policies advocated by the IMF include trade liberalisation, encouraging countries to reduce trade
barriers and tariffs to foster global commerce, and currency stability. The IMF also promotes fiscal
austerity, advising governments to reduce public spending and deficits to maintain economic
stability. Additionally, the IMF encourages structural reforms, such as privatisation and
deregulation, to enhance economic efficiency and attract investment.
The Structure Of The IMF
The IMF distributes decision-making power among its 190 member countries, however the exact
distribution is determined by each country’s financial contributions to the IMF, therefore benefitting
richer countries.
● Quota System and Voting Power: The IMF’s decision-making power is tied to a quota
system, where each member contributes financially based on its economic size. Quotas
determine both the amount a country can borrow and its voting power.
○ Larger economies like the U.S. and the EU have greater voting shares, giving them
more influence over decisions.
○ For example, the United States, as the largest economy, holds around 16.5% of the
total voting power, while many developing nations hold less than 1%.
● Board of Governors: The highest decision-making body in the IMF, comprising one
governor from each member country, typically the country’s finance minister or central bank
governor.
○ The Board meets annually and makes key decisions on matters like approving new
members, increasing quotas, and overseeing the general direction of the IMF.
● Executive Board: The Executive Board is responsible for the day-to-day operations of the
IMF. It consists of 24 Executive Directors who represent the member countries. The largest
economies, such as the United States, Japan, China, and major European nations, have
their own representatives, while smaller countries are grouped into constituencies that
share representation.

● IMF Staff: The IMF employs a large professional staff, including economists, statisticians,
and financial experts, who conduct research, prepare policy recommendations, and work
with member countries on technical assistance and economic surveillance.
What Does the IMF Do?
The Central Aims and Work of the IMF
The IMF has three key functions that underpin its role in promoting global economic stability and
development: lending, surveillance, and capacity development. These functions are central to
its mission of maintaining a stable global monetary system, fostering international monetary
cooperation, and reducing poverty.
1. Lending:
● The IMF provides financial assistance to member countries facing balance of
payments problems, where a country cannot meet its international obligations or stabilise
its currency without external support.
○ The goal of IMF lending is to help countries restore macroeconomic stability and
confidence in their economy, often following a financial crisis or severe economic
downturn.
● IMF loans are typically conditional, meaning they require the borrowing country to
implement economic reforms aimed at addressing their financial difficulties.
○ These reforms are rooted in neoliberal thinking and can include fiscal austerity,
currency devaluation, or structural changes in governance and market regulations.
○ While these reforms can be controversial, the IMF argues they are necessary to
stabilise the economy and restore growth.
Case Study - Greece (2010-2018)
In 2010, Greece faced a severe debt crisis, with public debt reaching 146% of GDP, rendering
the country unable to meet its international obligations and on the verge of default. The IMF,
alongside the European Union and the European Central Bank, provided bailout packages
totaling approximately €289 billion.
In exchange for loans, Greece had to implement austerity measures, including public sector cuts,
tax increases, and pension reforms. These included raising VAT rates, reducing public wages by
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 5
20%, and cutting pensions by up to 40%. While the IMF’s intervention helped Greece avoid
default and exit its bailout program in 2018, the conditions were highly controversial, leading to a
prolonged recession, unemployment reaching over 27% in 2013, and significant social unrest
across the country.
Case Study - Argentina (2025)
In April 2025, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) approved a $20 billion 48-month bailout
package for Argentina. This aimed to stabilise the Argentinian economy that was at risk of
financial crisis, due to limited foreign currency reserves.
On the one hand, this can be used as an example of the importance and effectiveness of the IMF
in offering vital financial assistance to countries at risk of financial crisis. The financial support
prevented a looming crisis by bolstering the country’s foreign exchange reserves.
On the other hand, it can also be used as an example of how the IMF pushes neoliberal
economic policies on countries in difficult financial situations, whilst also pushing them into further
debt. The bailout package was provided to Argentina as its President Javier Milei had
implemented a strict austerity agenda since becoming President in 2023, despite the fact that this
attracted widespread protests from a significant part of the Argentinian population.
Further, it can also be used as a clear example of how tackling climate change is only a
secondary priority of the IMF, as Milei won the Presidency on a platform of climate denial.
2. Surveillance:
IMF surveillance involves monitoring the global economy and individual member states to
prevent potential crises and provide policy advice. This function is critical for maintaining
international monetary stability and helps countries improve their economic performance.
● Each member country undergoes regular reviews, known as Article IV consultations, where
IMF experts assess its economic policies, fiscal stability, and growth prospects. These
reviews include discussions with government officials, central banks, and other
stakeholders.
○ The IMF provides recommendations on monetary and fiscal policy, exchange rates,
and structural reforms to help countries maintain stability and avoid economic
crises.
● The IMF also tracks the global economy through reports such as the World Economic
Outlook and the Global Financial Stability Report, identifying potential risks to the
international financial system. It assesses macroeconomic trends, capital flows, trade
imbalances, and systemic risks that could impact the global economy.
Case Study - Italy (2011 Debt Crisis)
During the Eurozone debt crisis, Italy’s high public debt and slow growth raised concerns about
its fiscal stability. Through Article IV consultations, the IMF identified critical weaknesses in Italy’s
labour market, tax system, and pension schemes.
The IMF recommended structural reforms, including improving labor flexibility and raising
retirement ages. Though Italy avoided a bailout, IMF surveillance played a key role in informing
policy changes that helped stabilise the economy during a period of significant risk.

3. Capacity Development:
Another essential function of the IMF is helping member countries strengthen their economic
institutions and policymaking through technical assistance and training. Capacity development
is particularly important for low-income and developing countries, where economic management
systems may be less developed or fragile. As through conditional lending and surveillance,
neoliberal economic reforms are promoted.
● Technical Assistance: The IMF provides expertise in areas such as tax policy, public
expenditure management, monetary policy, financial regulation, and the development of
statistical systems.
● Training: The IMF also offers extensive training programs for government officials, central
bankers, and financial regulators in member countries. These programs cover topics like
managing inflation, designing fiscal policies, and improving public financial management.
Case Study - Cambodia
In the early 2000s, Cambodia sought to modernise its central banking and financial systems. The
IMF provided technical assistance to improve Cambodia’s monetary policy framework and
banking regulations. By enhancing the capabilities of the National Bank of Cambodia and
providing training to local officials, the IMF helped the country stabilise its banking sector,
manage inflation more effectively, and sustain its economic growth. This technical support laid
the foundation for Cambodia’s improved financial stability.
Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs) in Focus
Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs) are economic reform packages imposed by the IMF as
a condition for receiving financial assistance. These programs aim to help countries facing
significant economic challenges, such as balance of payments crises, high debt, or severe fiscal
imbalances, by encouraging long-term economic stability and growth. In line with neoliberal
economic thinking SAPs typically focus on liberalising economies, implementing austerity, and
promoting free markets, but they have also generated significant debate due to their social and
political impact on recipient countries.
Key Components of SAPs:
● Fiscal Austerity: SAPs often require countries to reduce government spending to lower
fiscal deficits. This can involve cutting subsidies, reducing public sector employment, or
reforming pension systems.
● Trade Liberalisation: Countries are encouraged to open up their economies by reducing
trade barriers, such as tariffs and quotas, and promoting exports. The aim is to make the
economy more competitive and integrated into the global market.
● Privatisation and Deregulation: SAPs typically push for the privatisation of state-owned
enterprises and reducing government intervention in the economy. The idea is to create
more efficient and competitive markets by encouraging private investment and
entrepreneurship.
● Monetary and Exchange Rate Reforms: Countries may be required to implement policies
aimed at stabilising inflation, which can include devaluing the currency to make exports
more competitive and controlling money supply to prevent hyperinflation.
Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs) were most heavily used by the IMF from the 1980s
through the early 2000s, particularly in developing countries facing debt crises or severe
economic instability. SAPs became common as many countries in Latin America, Africa, and Asia
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 7
struggled with high external debts, inflation, and balance of payments problems. These countries
had little choice but to implement the SAPs as they required financial assistance.
These programs became controversial because, while they were designed to restore economic
stability, the neoliberal reforms they introduced frequently led to increased poverty,
unemployment, and social unrest. The perceived social costs and loss of economic sovereignty
sparked widespread criticism and protests in many countries.
Case Studies Of SAPs:
Case Study - Jamaica (1970s)
In the late 1970s, Jamaica faced severe economic challenges, including external debt that had
ballooned to over 150% of GDP, inflation rates reaching up to 50%, and stagnant economic
growth. The country turned to the IMF for assistance, agreeing to implement SAPs in exchange
for loans.
The programs involved significant fiscal austerity, including cuts in public services and subsidies,
along with trade liberalisation and privatisation of state-owned companies. Although these
measures stabilised the economy in some respects, GDP growth averaged just 1% annually
during the 1980s, and unemployment reached 25% by the mid-1980s. Poverty rates remained
high, with around 40% of the population living below the poverty line by 1990, while inequality
worsened, leaving a legacy of social hardship and fueling social unrest.
Case Study - Zambia (1990s)
In the 1990s, Zambia faced an economic crisis due to high external debt and dependence on
copper exports. The IMF and World Bank made lending conditional upon implementing an SAP
that included widespread privatisation, deregulation, and cuts in public spending on healthcare
and education. This resulted in mass layoffs, especially from state-owned enterprises, driving
poverty levels from 49% in 1989 to over 80% by the mid-1990s. The reforms did little to help the
economy, which only began to recover 10 years later.
The Flawed Approach and Disastrous Impact Of The IMF’s SAPs:
The IMF’s SAPs have been heavily criticised for their reliance on flawed neoliberal economic
principles that prioritised free markets, privatisation, and austerity at the expense of social
welfare. These programs were forced on developing countries, many of which, still grappling
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 8
with the economic legacies of European colonialism, had little choice but to accept IMF loans
despite opposing the harsh conditions attached.
While SAPs promoted trade liberalisation and reduced state intervention, successful development
in many Asian countries, such as South Korea and Taiwan, demonstrated that state-led industrial
policies and protectionist measures, rather than neoliberal reforms, were key to sustained
economic growth. SAPs deepened poverty by slashing public services, cutting subsidies, and
prioritising debt repayment over social investment, pushing already vulnerable populations into
further deprivation.
Moreover, these policies often served Western interests by ensuring developing countries
maintained export-oriented economies, supplying cheap raw materials and goods to the West
while restricting their own industrial and economic development. In effect, SAPs entrenched a
form of economic dependency that mirrored the exploitative relationships of the colonial
era, maintaining the dominance of richer nations at the expense of long-term, equitable growth in
the Global South.
Seeing the disastrous impact of their policies, the IMF has moved away from the one-size-fits-all
SAPs of the 1980s and 1990s. However, it continues to promote neoliberal economic reforms and
many countries are still feeling the effects of the reforms and paying back the high-interest loans.
The IMF in the Past Decade
1. Response to the Eurozone Debt Crisis (2010s) - The Eurozone debt crisis, which began
in 2010, marked a significant moment for the IMF’s role in advanced economies. Countries
like Greece, Ireland, and Portugal received IMF assistance to manage unsustainable debt.
2. The IMF and Emerging Markets - As emerging markets like China, India, and Brazil grew
in economic significance, the IMF has had to adjust its role to better reflect the shifting
dynamics of the global economy. In recent years, it has placed greater emphasis on debt
sustainability in low- and middle-income countries, helping them to manage debt levels
without sacrificing economic growth or social services, rather than the harsh austerity they
recommended in the late 20th century.
3. The COVID-19 Pandemic Response - The IMF played a critical role in the global response
to the COVID-19 pandemic, shifting away from strict conditionality toward providing rapid,
emergency financial support with fewer conditions. In 2020 alone, the IMF disbursed over
$100 billion in emergency financing to more than 80 countries, focusing on health system
support and economic recovery.
○ Programs such as the Rapid Financing Instrument (RFI) and Rapid Credit Facility
(RCF) were used to deliver swift assistance, especially in low-income and
developing nations.
4. Reforms and Calls For Change - In response to criticism of its traditional approach, the
IMF has made some reforms in recent years, particularly in its governance structure.
Emerging economies have gained slightly more influence in decision-making, with reforms
in voting shares and quotas.
○ However, calls for greater representation of developing countries and more
democratic governance continue, as these countries remain underrepresented
relative to their growing economic influence.
○ Additionally, critics have pushed the IMF to be more transparent and accountable in
its decision-making, as well as more flexible in tailoring its programs to the unique
needs of member countries.
Reforms In Recent Years:

countries. In particular, it increased its long-term annual lending capacity for low-income

The Debate About The IMF
Key Criticisms of the IMF and Arguments for Reform
The IMF has faced significant criticism over the years for the way it operates, particularly in relation
to its conditional lending, governance structure, and impact on developing countries. Many argue
that the IMF’s policies, often based on neoliberal economic principles, have led to increased
poverty, inequality, and social unrest in some countries. Critics also argue that its governance is
dominated by wealthy nations, leading to policies that favor the interests of the Global North over
those of developing countries and fail to address climate change and other global challenges.
The IMF Promotes Austerity Which Deepens Poverty, Harms Development and Undermines
The Sovereignty Of Developing Countries:
● One of the most frequent criticisms of the IMF is its emphasis on austerity measures in
exchange for financial assistance. Conditionality often requires countries to cut public
spending, privatise state-owned enterprises, and reduce social services.
○ While these measures are intended to restore fiscal balance, they frequently lead to
increased poverty, unemployment, and social unrest.
○ In Greece, IMF-backed austerity measures during the Eurozone debt crisis led to
27% unemployment, massive cuts in pensions and healthcare, and widespread
protests, whilst the SAPs of the 1980s had similarly disastrous consequences.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 10
● The IMF’s lending and austerity measures have also been criticised for creating a cycle of
economic dependency in developing countries.
○ Countries that adopt IMF reforms have often found themselves locked into a cycle
of borrowing, unable to generate sustainable growth or diversify their economies.
Trade liberalisation and privatisation, often benefit foreign investors and export
markets, but stifle the long term developments of domestic industries.
○ This has been particularly true in African and Latin American nations, where
IMF-backed reforms have led to long-term economic stagnation and high levels of
inequality.
● The austerity measures the IMF promotes also undermine national sovereignty. Developing
countries, in particular, feel coerced into accepting IMF programs because they have limited
access to other financial resources, even though they go against domestic interests and
political opinion.
○ In the long term, this also results in governments prioritising debt repayment and
foreign investors over the needs of their own people, exacerbating economic
dependency.
The IMF Is Structured To Favour Wealthy Countries:
● The IMF’s governance structure has long been criticised for being undemocratic and
disproportionately favoring wealthy countries at the expense of developing nations.
● Voting power within the IMF is based on financial contributions, or quotas, which are
determined by a country’s economic size. This system gives advanced economies far more
influence over decision-making.
○ For example, the United States holds around 16.5% of the total voting power, which,
combined with the requirement that major decisions need an 85% majority,
effectively grants the U.S. a de facto veto over key IMF policies. Other large
economies, such as Japan and major EU countries, also have outsised influence.
● By contrast, many developing countries, which are often the primary recipients of IMF
programs, collectively hold far less voting power and thus have minimal input into the
decision-making process.
○ This imbalance has led to growing calls for reform, with critics arguing that emerging
markets and low-income nations, which frequently bear the brunt of IMF-imposed
austerity measures, deserve greater representation and a stronger voice in shaping
the policies that directly affect their economies.
● Despite periodic discussions about reforming the quota system, progress has been slow,
further entrenching the perception that the IMF serves the interests of the Global North over
the Global South.
The IMF Priorities Economic Growth At The Expense Of Tackling Climate Change and
Inequality:
● Critics argue that the IMF’s primary focus on macroeconomic stability and economic growth
often comes at the expense of addressing critical global challenges such as climate change
and inequality.
● The IMF’s central focus has always been on economic growth and debt reduction, with
tackling climate change always being a secondary priority.
○ The Paris Agreement and the growing urgency of the climate crisis have prompted
the IMF to begin incorporating environmental risk into its assessments, but critics
argue that these steps are too limited given the scale of the challenge.
○ Further, the IMF is likely to move away from focusing on climate change in the
coming years, as in April 2025 the Trump administration has lobbied both the IMF
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 11
and World Bank to focus less on climate change and more on its “core missions” of
stabilising the global economy and seeking to reduce poverty.
● Similarly, the IMF’s focus on economic growth has historically failed to take into account its
impact on inequality, which its austerity-focused reforms have greatly increased in the SAPs
of the late 20th century and since.
Key Arguments in Support of the IMF and Its Positive Impact
Despite facing criticism, the IMF is also credited with playing a crucial role in maintaining global
financial stability and promoting economic recovery in times of crisis. Supporters argue that the
IMF’s interventions are essential for preventing deeper economic collapse and helping countries
implement necessary reforms to stabilise and grow their economies. Additionally, the IMF has
adapted in recent years to better address global challenges such as debt relief, sustainable
development, and climate change.
IMF Support Is Crucial In Helping Countries Manage Financial Crises:
● One of the IMF’s key strengths is its ability to provide rapid financial assistance to countries
in economic distress, helping them stabilise their economies and avoid default. This role as
a lender of last resort is particularly important during global or regional financial crises.
○ For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the IMF disbursed over $100 billion in
emergency funding to more than 80 countries, helping them manage the economic
fallout of the health crisis.
● By preventing financial crises from worsening or spreading, the IMF plays a vital role in
ensuring global economic stability. Proponents argue that without the IMF’s interventions,
many countries would face deeper recessions, social unrest, or even financial collapse,
which the IMF has helped to avoid in countries such as Greece and Argentina.
Economic Measures Promoted by the IMF Are Necessary:
● Supporters of the IMF argue that the structural reforms and austerity measures it promotes
are often essential for addressing fundamental economic problems. While these reforms
involve short-term pain, such as cutting government spending or implementing fiscal
discipline, they are necessary to restore long-term stability and growth.
○ In cases like Ghana in the 1980s, IMF-backed reforms had some success in
reducing inflation, stabilising the currency, and fostering economic growth.
● Advocates argue that the IMF’s focus on reducing fiscal deficits, liberalising trade, and
privatising inefficient state enterprises helps countries attract investment, boost
competitiveness, and lay the foundation for sustainable economic development.
○ These measures, while sometimes unpopular, are seen as critical for achieving
long-term macroeconomic stability.
The IMF Has Adapted in Recent Years to Promote Debt Relief, Sustainable Development, and
Recognise the Importance of Tackling Climate Change:
● In response to criticisms of its past policies, the IMF has evolved to better address debt
sustainability, inclusive growth, and climate change in the 21st century.
● Programs like the Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust (PRGT) offer concessional lending
to low-income countries with a focus on poverty reduction, and initiatives like the Heavily
Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) program have provided debt relief to dozens of nations,
freeing up resources for social development.
● Furthermore, the IMF has begun integrating climate risk assessments into its economic
analysis and advising countries on sustainable development strategies.

○ For instance, the IMF now encourages the use of carbon pricing and policies to
reduce greenhouse gas emissions while promoting green growth.
The World Bank
The Origins and Aims of the World Bank
The World Bank was established alongside the IMF in 1944 during the Bretton Woods Conference.
Its initial aim was to help rebuild European economies after the devastation of World War II. The
Bank’s first loan, issued in 1947 to France, focused on reconstruction efforts. Over time, however,
the World Bank’s mission has shifted from reconstruction to fostering economic development and
poverty reduction, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Today, the World Bank has 190
member countries.
The Structure of the World Bank
The World Bank’s governance structure, like the IMF’s, gives more influence to wealthier nations
due to the quota system, where member countries contribute financial resources based on their
economic size.
1. Board of Governors: Each of the 190 member countries is represented on the Board of
Governors, typically by the finance minister or central bank governor. The Board of
Governors sets the overarching policies of the World Bank but delegates most decisions to
the smaller Executive Board.
2. Executive Board: The Executive Board consists of 25 Executive Directors, who represent
member countries or groups of countries. The five largest shareholders (the United States,
Japan, China, Germany, and the United Kingdom) each have their own Executive Director,
while other countries are grouped into constituencies. The Executive Board oversees the
Bank’s operations, lending projects, and policy decisions.
What Does the World Bank Do?
The World Bank’s mission is distinct from the IMF, focusing more on reducing poverty and
promoting long-term development, rather than tackling short-term financial crises. The Bank uses
loans, grants and provides expertise to developing countries. It focuses on infrastructure
investment, capacity development, and institutional reforms that help foster economic growth and
improve living standards.

Financing Development Projects
The World Bank finances a wide array of development projects aimed at reducing poverty,
improving infrastructure, and supporting economic growth. These projects range from building
roads, schools, and hospitals to improving access to clean water and sanitation. This is carried out
by its two main lending institutions:
1. The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD): The IBRD lends
primarily to middle-income and creditworthy low-income countries, offering loans at market
rates to finance projects that foster economic growth.
2. The International Development Association (IDA): The IDA provides concessional loans
and grants to the world’s poorest countries, focusing on initiatives that promote equitable
growth and reduce poverty. IDA loans are interest-free with long repayment periods, making
them accessible for low-income nations.
Case Study – India’s Rural Electrification Program (2014-Present)
The World Bank has played a crucial role in expanding access to electricity in rural India. Since
2014, the World Bank has provided $1 billion in loans to support India’s ambitious rural
electrification initiative, which aims to provide reliable electricity to 200 million people living in
rural areas. By funding the construction of transmission lines, distribution networks, and
renewable energy sources, the World Bank has helped improve living standards, foster economic
growth, and promote environmental sustainability.
Technical Assistance and Capacity Building
In addition to financing, the World Bank provides technical assistance and capacity building to help
countries develop the institutions and skills necessary for sustained economic development. This
includes advising on public sector reform, improving tax systems, and enhancing governance.
Case Study – Rwanda’s Education Reform (2010-Present)
In Rwanda, the World Bank has worked closely with the government to reform the education
system and improve access to quality education. The Bank provided $200 million in grants to
support teacher training, curriculum development, and infrastructure improvements. These efforts
have helped increase school enrollment rates, reduce dropout rates, and improve the quality of
education in a country still recovering from conflict.
Promoting Sustainable Development:
The World Bank is increasingly focused on sustainability and the need to balance economic growth
with environmental protection. The Bank finances projects aimed at mitigating the effects of climate
change, supporting renewable energy, and promoting green infrastructure.
Case Study – Morocco’s Solar Power Program (2016-Present)
The World Bank has supported Morocco’s transition to renewable energy through financing for its
solar power projects. The Bank contributed over $400 million to the development of the Noor
Solar Complex, one of the largest solar power plants in the world. This project has helped
Morocco reduce its reliance on fossil fuels, cut greenhouse gas emissions, and position itself as
a leader in renewable energy in the region.

The Debate About The World Bank
The Weaknesses and Negative Impact of the World Bank
The World Bank has faced significant criticism for the way it operates, particularly in relation to its
promotion of neoliberal economic policies, its focus on large infrastructure projects over social
needs, and its governance structure, which is dominated by wealthier nations. Many argue that the
World Bank’s policies have led to increased debt, environmental degradation, and a lack of
representation for developing countries in decision-making processes.
The World Bank Promotes Neoliberalism and Increases the Debt Burden of Developing
Countries
● One of the most common criticisms of the World Bank is its promotion of neoliberal
economic policies, including trade liberalisation, privatisation, and deregulation, which
critics argue often harm developing countries.
○ These policies are typically tied to the loans provided by the Bank, meaning that
countries in need of financial assistance are forced to implement reforms that can
lead to economic instability.
● The World Bank cooperated closely with the IMF in the 1980s and 1990s to promote
structural adjustment programmes that greatly harmed the economies of many countries in
Africa and Latin America.
○ In Bolivia in the 1990s, World Bank-supported reforms pushed for the privatisation
of water services, leading to a dramatic increase in water prices and sparking
widespread protests.
○ In Zambia, the privatisation of copper mines in the 1990s, as part of World Bank and
IMF prescribed reforms, led to mass layoffs and a reduction in government
revenues, further exacerbating poverty levels.
● Furthermore, by providing loans, even at low-interest rates, the Bank contributes to the
growing debt burden of developing nations. Many countries find themselves trapped in
cycles of borrowing, struggling to repay loans while simultaneously needing to take on more
debt to fund basic services.
○ This has been particularly problematic in Africa and Latin America, where countries
such as Kenya and Argentina have been forced to prioritise debt repayments over
social spending, limiting their ability to invest in public services like healthcare,
education, and infrastructure.
○ In Argentina, after taking on World Bank loans in the late 1990s, the country faced
severe economic decline, leading to widespread poverty, high unemployment, and
social unrest.
The World Bank Focuses on Large-Scale Infrastructure Over Social Needs and Has
Contributed to Climate Change:
● The World Bank’s emphasis on large-scale infrastructure projects, such as building roads,
dams, and energy plants, has been another point of contention. While these projects can
drive economic growth, critics argue that the Bank’s focus on infrastructure often neglects
urgent social needs and displaces local populations, who have no agency.
○ For example, in Uganda, the World Bank-backed Bujagali Hydropower Project
displaced thousands of people, disrupted local ecosystems, and led to protests from
affected communities, despite its goal of improving energy access.
● Large infrastructure projects have also contributed to environmental degradation and
climate change, as many of them involve deforestation, displacement of local populations,
and increased carbon emissions.

○ The Tata Mundra coal plant in India, financed by the World Bank’s International
Finance Corporation (IFC), has been criticised for its environmental and social
impacts, including air pollution and harm to local fisheries.
● Additionally, the World Bank has been involved in funding fossil fuel-related projects despite
global efforts to combat climate change. Between 2018 and 2020, the Bank financed $12
billion worth of fossil fuel projects, including coal, oil, and gas infrastructure, which
significantly undermines international climate goals.
● Although the World Bank has started to recognise the importance of sustainable
development and climate change mitigation, such as investing in renewable energy
projects, critics argue that its actions have been insufficient, and many of its projects
continue to have a negative impact on the environment and local communities.
The World Bank’s Governance Favours Wealthier Nations:

● Like the IMF, the governance structure of the World Bank has been criticised for
disproportionately favouring wealthy, Western nations. Voting power within the World Bank
is determined by financial contributions, meaning that richer countries have significantly
more influence over decisions than poor countries, the primary recipients of its loans.
○ The United States remains the largest shareholder, holding about 15.65% of the
voting power in the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD),
which gives it substantial influence over the Bank’s policies.
■ Additionally, the U.S. is the only country with veto power over major
structural changes within the Bank.
○ In contrast, many developing countries, which are the primary recipients of World
Bank loans, collectively hold much smaller shares, with African nations holding
around 7% of the voting power.
■ Indonesia and Pakistan, the two countries in the world with the closest
population size to the US, have just 1.04% and 0.52% vote share
respectively in the IBRD.
● This imbalance has led to concerns that the World Bank primarily serves the interests of the
Global North rather than the Global South, with wealthier nations shaping policies that
benefit them economically and politically.
○ Calls for reform to give emerging markets and developing countries greater
representation have been ongoing, but progress has been slow, reinforcing

perceptions that the World Bank operates in the interests of powerful nations rather
than the countries it aims to help.
The Strengths and Positive Impact of the World Bank
The World Bank’s supporters argue that it has played a pivotal role in addressing global poverty,
supporting long-term economic development, and providing financial and technical support during
global crises. Through its large-scale funding programs and coordination efforts, the Bank has
helped millions of people worldwide by enhancing infrastructure and economic resilience.
The World Bank Has Reduced Poverty and Promoted Long-Term Development:
● The World Bank’s commitment to reducing poverty is at the core of its mission, and its
financing programs have helped transform economies and improve living standards across
the developing world.
● Since it began, the World Bank has provided over $160 billion in concessional financing to
the world’s poorest countries, helping them build essential infrastructure, improve
healthcare, and expand educational opportunities. This financing has helped lift millions out
of poverty and promote long-term growth.
○ In Vietnam, the World Bank’s Rural Electrification Project successfully connected
2.7 million households to electricity, enabling the expansion of local industries,
increasing educational participation, and raising household incomes.
○ In Bangladesh, World Bank-backed programs contributed to reducing poverty from
44% in 1991 to 14% in 2021. Through initiatives such as microfinance programs
and support for social safety nets, the Bank helped expand economic opportunities
and reduce vulnerability in rural and urban areas.
○ In Ethiopia, World Bank funding has supported healthcare, infrastructure, and
agricultural reforms, contributing to sustained economic growth rates of above 8%
annually in the 2010s. The Bank has helped finance critical roads, clean water
projects, and agriculture-focused investments that have increased food security and
bolstered long-term growth.
● These examples highlight the Bank’s focus on addressing structural barriers to
development while fostering inclusive and sustainable growth.
The World Bank Has Enabled Global Coordination to Tackle Major Global Challenges and
Crises:
● The World Bank plays a vital role in coordinating global responses to major crises and
tackling long-term issues such as climate change, ensuring that vulnerable populations
receive both immediate relief and long-term support. By addressing food security, energy
needs, and climate resilience, the World Bank plays a crucial role in helping countries
navigate complex, global-scale crises.
○ In response to the rising global food and energy crisis resulting from the
Ukraine-Russia war in 2022, the World Bank mobilised more than $30 billion to
support countries impacted by rising prices.
○ This funding provided emergency assistance to food-insecure nations, helped
subsidiae basic goods, and supported farmers with essential resources to boost
domestic food production.
■ In Egypt, the support helped the government address food insecurity by
stabilising the grain supply through targeted subsidies and imports.
● Beyond emergency relief, the World Bank has also been at the forefront of coordinating
climate finance initiatives. In 2020, the Bank committed more than $20 billion to
