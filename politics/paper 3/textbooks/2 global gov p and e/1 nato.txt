Introduction to NATO
The Origins and Original Aims of NATO
The North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) was founded on April 4, 1949, in the aftermath of
World War II. Its primary goal was to create a collective defense mechanism to counter the growing
threat posed by the Soviet Union and communism during the Cold War. The signing of the North
Atlantic Treaty in Washington D.C. laid the foundation for this alliance.
The treaty’s key principles were based on mutual defence, outlined in Article 5, which stated that
an armed attack against one member would be considered an attack against all. This provision
aimed to deter aggression from the Soviet Union, providing a security umbrella to the Western
European states that were rebuilding after the devastation of World War II.
The founding members of NATO included 12 countries: Belgium, Canada, Denmark, France,
Iceland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, the United Kingdom, and the United
States. At its core, NATO was formed to:
● Provide collective security to Western Europe against the Soviet threat.
● Encourage political unity and cooperation between North American and European nations.
● Facilitate the economic rebuilding of Europe in collaboration with the Marshall Plan.

While its primary focus was on deterring the Soviet Union from military expansion in Europe, NATO
also aimed to prevent the rise of nationalism and militarism in Europe by fostering cooperation and
preventing internal conflicts among its member states.
The Structure Of NATO
The alliance’s central decision-making body is the North Atlantic Council (NAC), which consists
of representatives from all member states. The NAC is NATO’s highest authority, and it makes
decisions by consensus, meaning that all member states must agree before action is taken. This
being said, the US’s military might means it holds the most sway.
The Secretary General, appointed by member states, serves as the political leader and
spokesperson of NATO. The Secretary General chairs meetings of the NAC and other key NATO
committees, helping to mediate discussions and drive forward the alliance’s agenda.
Secretary General Of NATO, Mark Rutte
NATO relies on contributions from its member states for its military forces. NATO itself does not
have standing armed forces, but it coordinates the contributions of its members, who provide
personnel, equipment, and resources for joint missions and exercises. NATO’s collective defense
commitment is backed by this multinational force, which is organised and deployed as needed for
both peacetime operations and crisis management.
NATO Today
Since its formation, NATO has evolved significantly, both in terms of membership and the scope of
its mission. Originally a 12-member alliance, NATO today includes 31 member countries, with its
most recent additions being Finland in 2023 and Sweden in 2024. NATO has become a central
pillar of global security, engaging in various roles beyond its Cold War purpose.
The organisation’s modern objectives have expanded to include crisis management, cooperative
security, and conflict prevention. In addition to its defense role, NATO now focuses on:
● Combating terrorism.
● Promoting cybersecurity.
● Conducting peacekeeping operations.
● Responding to emerging threats such as hybrid warfare and the proliferation of weapons of
mass destruction.

Though all of its members apart from the US are European, NATO’s reach extends far beyond
Europe and North America. It has engaged in military operations in Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya, and
the Balkans, and has formed partnerships with non-member states and IGOs including the UN,
EU, Interpol and African Union. NATO today is far more than a Cold War relic; it is a dynamic
organisation that plays a significant role in global politics and remains a cornerstone of Western
defence strategy.
The Development of NATO Over Time
NATO During the Cold War
During the Cold War, NATO’s primary purpose was to serve as a deterrent to the Soviet Union and
the spread of communism in Europe. The geopolitical landscape of the time was defined by the
rivalry between the Western bloc, led by the United States and NATO, and the Eastern bloc, led by
the Soviet Union and its Warsaw Pact allies. NATO played a crucial role in maintaining Western
security and promoting the collective defense of its member states during this period.
The Role and Aims Of NATO In The Cold War
Containment of the Soviet Union
NATO was formed primarily to counterbalance the Soviet Union’s growing influence in Eastern
Europe. The alliance adopted a strategy of “containment,” seeking to limit the expansion of
Soviet communism through military and political means.
The creation of the Warsaw Pact in 1955 by the Soviet Union, comprising Eastern Bloc nations,
was a direct response to NATO. This formalised the division between the East and West in
Europe, establishing the two sides of the Cold War.
Collective Defense and Article 5
The cornerstone of NATO’s strategy was Article 5, which emphasised collective defense. An
attack on one NATO member would be considered an attack on all members, obligating all to
respond. Although Article 5 was not invoked during the Cold War, the mere presence of this
clause acted as a powerful deterrent against Soviet aggression.
NATO’s collective defense policy was designed to guarantee that Western European nations,
some of which were recovering from World War II, were shielded from the Soviet military threat,
especially as the USSR had amassed a large army and nuclear capabilities.
Nuclear Deterrence and the Arms Race
NATO adopted a strategy of nuclear deterrence, primarily through the U.S.’s nuclear arsenal, to
counterbalance the Soviet Union’s military advantage in Europe. This led to the deployment of
U.S. nuclear weapons on European soil, beginning in the 1950s.
NATO’s strategic doctrine during much of the Cold War was “flexible response.” This meant that
NATO would respond to any Soviet aggression with an appropriate level of force, including the
possible use of nuclear weapons if conventional forces were insufficient. This approach was
intended to prevent the Soviet Union from believing it could wage a limited war in Europe without
risking a nuclear conflict.

NATO’s Expansion and Changed Aims Following the End of the Cold War
The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 marked a significant turning point for NATO. The alliance,
originally formed to counterbalance Soviet power and communism, had to redefine its purpose and
objectives in a rapidly changing geopolitical landscape. This shift led to an expansion of both
NATO’s membership and its mission, as it sought to play a broader role in global security.
Changing Aims in the Post-Cold War Era
With the dissolution of the Soviet Union and the Warsaw Pact, NATO no longer faced a clear
adversary. As a result, the alliance began to shift its focus away from traditional Cold War-era
collective defense toward new priorities:
● Crisis Management and Peacekeeping: NATO moved toward addressing regional
conflicts and instability, particularly in areas that could affect European and global security.
The 1990s and 2000s saw NATO playing an active role in crisis management and
peacekeeping, signaling a shift in its purpose.
● Promoting Stability in Eastern Europe: One of NATO’s new goals was to stabilise former
communist states and promote democratic governance and market economies in these
regions. This was seen as crucial for ensuring long-term peace and preventing the
resurgence of authoritarianism in Europe.
● Partnerships and Cooperation: NATO sought to build new relationships with other
countries, including former adversaries such as Russia, through initiatives like the
Partnership for Peace (PfP) program, launched in 1994. The PfP offered non-member
countries the opportunity to cooperate with NATO on security issues.
NATO’s Expansion into Former Soviet Territories
NATO’s expansion was one of the most significant and contentious developments after the Cold
War. The inclusion of Central and Eastern European countries, many of which had been part of the
Warsaw Pact, sparked debate about the alliance’s changing role.
● First Wave of Expansion (1999): In 1999, NATO admitted Poland, Hungary, and the
Czech Republic, marking the first time NATO expanded into former Soviet-aligned
territories. This expansion was seen as part of NATO’s broader strategy to promote stability
and security in Central Europe, but it also triggered concerns in Russia, which viewed it as
a threat to its sphere of influence.
● Second Wave of Expansion (2004): In 2004, NATO underwent its largest expansion,
adding seven new members: Bulgaria, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Romania, Slovakia, and
Slovenia. This brought NATO directly to Russia’s borders, particularly with the inclusion of
the Baltic states, which had been part of the Soviet Union. For many of these countries,
joining NATO was a way to ensure security against potential Russian aggression.
The expansion of NATO was controversial, particularly in Russia, where it was seen as a
provocative move. Russian leaders, including Vladimir Putin, viewed NATO’s eastward expansion
as a direct threat to Russian security. NATO’s offer of potential future membership to Ukraine and
Georgia at the 2008 Bucharest Summit further heightened tensions with Russia, contributing to the
Russia-Georgia War later that year.

NATO’s New Missions in the 1990s: The Balkans
NATO’s post-Cold War era saw the alliance take a more active role in international crises,
especially in Europe, where the breakup of Yugoslavia and ethnic conflicts demanded international
attention.
Bosnia and Herzegovina (1995)
NATO’s first major military engagement came during the Bosnian War, part of the wider Yugoslav
Wars in the early 1990s. After years of ethnic violence, NATO launched Operation Deliberate
Force in 1995 to protect civilians and force warring parties to negotiate.
The operation was significant because it marked NATO’s first-ever military intervention outside its
member states. NATO airstrikes against Bosnian Serb forces helped bring the conflict to an end,
leading to the Dayton Agreement that secured peace in the region.
Kosovo (1999)
NATO intervened again in the Balkans during the Kosovo War, launching Operation Allied Force
in 1999 to prevent ethnic cleansing by Serbian forces against Kosovo Albanians. The air
campaign, conducted without UN Security Council authorisation, was controversial but ultimately
led to the withdrawal of Serbian forces from Kosovo. This intervention demonstrated NATO’s
willingness to act on humanitarian grounds, broadening its mission beyond traditional defence.
NATO in the 2000s: Adapting to Global Terrorism and New Security Threats
The 2000s marked another significant shift for NATO, as the alliance adapted to the new global
security environment shaped by the 9/11 terrorist attacks and the rise of international terrorism.
● Invoking Article 5 for the First Time: In response to 9/11, NATO invoked Article 5 of its
treaty for the first time in its history, declaring that an attack on one member was an attack
on all. This led to NATO’s involvement in the War on Terror, particularly in Afghanistan.
● NATO in Afghanistan (2003-2014): NATO took command of the International Security
Assistance Force (ISAF) in Afghanistan in 2003. ISAF’s mission was to stabilise
Afghanistan, rebuild its institutions, and train Afghan security forces.

○ This marked NATO’s first major mission outside Europe and demonstrated its
growing role in addressing global security challenges.
● Global Counter-Terrorism Efforts: NATO also adapted its strategy to include a broader
focus on counter-terrorism. The creation of the NATO Response Force (NRF) in 2002, a
high-readiness, multinational force, reflected NATO’s efforts to respond rapidly to emerging
threats, including terrorism and the proliferation of weapons of mass destruction.
NATO In The 2010s
The 2010s were a decade of both continuity and significant challenges for NATO. The alliance had
to navigate an increasingly complex global security environment, with rising tensions between
NATO and Russia, internal disagreements over burden-sharing, and shifting political dynamics,
particularly due to the election of President Trump.
At the 2010 Lisbon Summit at the start of the century, NATO adopted a new Strategic Concept that
emphasized collective defense, crisis management, and cooperative security. This reflected
NATO’s need to address a broader range of security threats, from terrorism to cyberattacks, while
still maintaining its core focus on collective defence.
The Annexation of Crimea (2014)
One of the most defining moments for NATO in the 2010s was Russia’s annexation of Crimea in
2014, which fundamentally changed the security landscape in Europe.
● In February 2014, following the ousting of Ukraine’s pro-Russian president Viktor
Yanukovych, Russian forces moved into Crimea, and soon after, Russia formally annexed
the region through a disputed referendum.
○ This marked the first time since World War II that European borders were redrawn
by force.
● NATO strongly condemned Russia’s actions, declaring the annexation of Crimea illegal. In
response, NATO increased its military presence in Eastern Europe, particularly in the Baltic
states and Poland, through the Enhanced Forward Presence initiative.
○ This involved stationing multinational battlegroups in those countries as a deterrent
against further Russian aggression.
● The annexation of Crimea significantly worsened NATO-Russia relations. NATO suspended
all practical cooperation with Russia through the NATO-Russia Council, marking a sharp
reversal of the cooperative approach pursued in the 1990s and early 2000s. Russia viewed
NATO’s military buildup near its borders as a threat, further escalating tensions between the
two sides.
Trump Raises The Issue Of Burden Sharing
Another major issue for NATO in the 2010s was the internal debate over burden-sharing, which
came to a head during the first presidency of Donald Trump. The question of how much each
NATO member contributes to defence spending has been a long-standing issue, but it became
particularly contentious with the election of Trump. Trump’s election was part of a broader rise of
populist, nationalist movements across the world which often questioned the value of international
alliances and called for more isolationist or national-focused foreign policies
● Trump’s Criticism of NATO Funding: Throughout his campaign and first presidency,
Donald Trump criticised NATO members, particularly European allies, for not meeting their
defence spending commitments. Trump argued that many NATO countries were not

contributing their fair share, particularly the goal of spending 2% of GDP on defense, as
agreed at the 2014 NATO Summit in Wales.
● “Obsolete” Remarks: Trump’s labeling of NATO as “obsolete” during his 2016 presidential

clarified that NATO was “no longer obsolete,” the comments created uncertainty about the
future of U.S. leadership within NATO and its commitment to collective defense.
● Increased Defence Spending: Trump’s criticism did lead to some shifts in defence

From the beginning of Trump’s presidency through to the present, NATO was also challenged by

NATO In The 2020s
The 2020s so far have been a defining period for NATO as it adapts to the largest land conflict in
Europe since World War II, the expansion of its membership, and continued debates over its role in
global security.
The Russian Invasion of Ukraine (2022) and NATO’s Response
The most consequential event for NATO in decades has been Russia’s full-scale invasion of
Ukraine in February 2022. This conflict has drastically altered the European security landscape
and led to a renewed focus on NATO’s core mission of collective defense.

● Putin’s Justification of the Invasion: Russian President Vladimir Putin justified the
invasion, in part, by accusing NATO of expanding too close to Russia’s borders since the
end of the Cold War, which he argues threatened Russian security. Putin pointed to NATO’s
expansion into former Soviet states like the Baltic countries and its growing relationship
with Ukraine (which sought NATO membership) as provocations.
○ He cited NATO’s enlargement as a key reason for the invasion, describing it as part
of a broader Western encirclement of Russia.
● Immediate NATO Response: When Russia launched its invasion of Ukraine, NATO
immediately condemned the attack as a violation of international law and European
security. While Ukraine is not a NATO member and therefore not covered by NATO’s
collective defense guarantee (Article 5), the alliance provided substantial military, financial,
and humanitarian support to Ukraine.
○ Since 2022, the US and NATO members in Europe have supplied financial support,
advanced weapons systems, intelligence, and logistical support to help Ukraine
resist Russian forces.
● Strengthening Eastern Flank: In response to the Russian aggression, NATO rapidly
bolstered its military presence on its eastern flank. Troops, equipment, and air defense
were deployed to countries bordering Russia and Ukraine, including Poland, Romania, and
the Baltic states, to deter any potential spillover of the conflict into NATO territory.
● NATO’s Reaffirmation of Collective Defense: The invasion of Ukraine has reinforced
NATO’s central mission of collective defense. The threat posed by Russia to European
security has united NATO members in their determination to defend their territory.
○ At NATO summits in 2022 and 2023, member states reaffirmed their commitment to
Article 5 and agreed to further strengthen NATO’s military readiness and
capabilities.
○ This was especially consequential in the context of the US’ increasing focus on
China as the main strategic competitor prior to the invasion of Ukraine, which once
again showed Russia as a key threat to peace and Eastern Europe as a highly
important geopolitical arena.
NATO Expansion: Finland and Sweden Join the Alliance
The war in Ukraine prompted a dramatic shift in the security policies of several European
countries, most notably Finland and Sweden, which sought NATO membership after years of
maintaining military non-alignment.
● Finland’s Accession (2023): Finland officially joined NATO in April 2023, marking a
historic shift in its defencee policy. Finland, which shares a long border with Russia, had
maintained neutrality throughout the Cold War and the post-Cold War era.

○ However, the invasion of Ukraine raised fears of potential Russian aggression,
pushing Finland to seek the protection of NATO’s collective defense.
● Sweden’s Accession (2024): Sweden, another long-standing neutral country, also joined
NATO in March 2024, driven by similar fear of Russian aggression as Finland. Sweden’s
membership further consolidates NATO’s presence in Northern Europe and strengthens its

The accession of Finland and Sweden represents a significant expansion of NATO, bringing two

to defend its north and eastern flanks, given its strategic location along the Russian border. This
expansion has further isolated Russia and heightened tensions between Moscow and NATO.
Increased Military Spending
● The invasion shattered the post-Cold War security assumptions in Europe and underscored
the immediate threat posed by Russian aggression, particularly to NATO’s eastern
members. As a result, it prompted a substantial increase in military spending by European
countries, many of which had been accused by Trump of free-riding for years.
● Germany, in particular, underwent a significant transformation in its defense strategy. For
decades, Germany had maintained a restrained military posture, spending less than the
NATO target of 2% of GDP on defense and focusing on diplomacy and economic power.
○ However, in response to the invasion, Chancellor Olaf Scholz announced a historic
“Zeitenwende” (turning point) in German defense policy.
○ Germany pledged to immediately inject €100 billion into its defense budget and
committed to meeting NATO’s 2% GDP defense spending target, marking a major
departure from its previous stance.
● Already a key NATO frontline state, Poland significantly boosted its defense spending,
committing to reach 3% of its GDP by 2023. It also embarked on a large-scale military
modernisation program, including the acquisition of advanced U.S. and South Korean
weapons systems.
The Questioning Of The U.S.’ NATO Commitment In Trump’s Second Term
● Perhaps the most historically significant shift in U.S. foreign policy in Trump’s second term
so far has been how his actions and rhetoric have led to the questioning by some of the
U.S.’ continued commitment to NATO and Article 5 in particular.
● Though Trump hasn’t explicitly said he doesn’t support Article 5 or that he intends to leave
NATO, his strong criticism of limited European defence spending and refusal to provide a
security guarantee to Ukraine have led many European leaders to question whether the
American commitment to collective defence of Europe can still be trusted.
○ This is likely Trump’s intention, as he wants Europe to increase its defence
spending and take more responsibility for European security.
○ It is also without doubt motivated by (1) his desire to pivot U.S. foreign policy to
focus on confronting China (2) his desire to pursue a rapprochement with Russia to
help achieve this and (3) his desire to cut the spiralling cost of the U.S. deficit by
spending less money on defence in Europe.
● This could be used to suggest that NATO is no longer effective, as it has always been
highly dependent on the United States and it simply doesn’t work as an alliance if the U.S.’
commitment to defending Europe can be questioned.
○ It can also be used to suggest that NATO is outdated, as the U.S. is now much more
focused on challenging China than it is on Russia.

Debates About NATO
Has NATO Lost Its Purpose
Since the end of the Cold War, the relevance of NATO has been a subject of debate. Some argue
that NATO’s original purpose (defending Western Europe against Soviet expansion) has become
obsolete, raising questions about whether the alliance has lost its direction. On the other hand,
proponents argue that NATO has successfully adapted to new global security challenges, making it
as important today as it was during its early years.
Arguments that NATO Has Lost Its Purpose:
● NATO was established in 1949 to counter the Soviet Union during the Cold War. With the
dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991 some argue that NATO’s primary purpose no longer
exists. This leaves NATO without a clear adversary, making its mission less focused.
○ As a result, the alliance has failed to maintain a unified strategic mission, particularly
with the US increasingly concerned by China.
● NATO’s expansion into former Soviet territories (such as the Baltic states and Poland) has
been controversial. Critics argue that this expansion has diluted NATO’s core mission,
adding members who bring security risks and complicating the decision-making process.
○ Russia has cited NATO’s expansion as a key source of tension, leading to
accusations that NATO is provoking conflict rather than preventing it.
○ Further, NATO’s involvement in conflicts outside its traditional European sphere,
such as in Afghanistan and Libya, has led to criticism that NATO is overextending
itself by engaging in operations far from its original purpose of defending Europe.
● The end of the Soviet Union as an immediate danger has arguably contributed to a lack of
commitment from European nation states to funding their militaries and consequent
disagreements with the US over burden sharing.
○ The strategic priorities of NATO members have diverged in recent decades. While
Eastern European members see Russia as a primary threat, Western European
nations and the U.S. are more focused on terrorism and China as a major
geopolitical threat. This divergence complicates NATO’s ability to act coherently.
Arguments that NATO Hasn’t Lost Its Purpose:
● The 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine underscored the continued relevance of NATO and
of the need for a strong collective defence alliance. NATO’s response, including military
support to Ukraine and the deployment of troops to its Eastern European members,
demonstrated its ability to adapt to new threats and its members’ continued commitment to
the alliance. Article 5 remains a crucial deterrent, particularly for nations bordering Russia.
○ The war in Ukraine also prompted historically neutral countries Finland and Sweden
to seek NATO membership, highlighting the enduring appeal of NATO’s security
guarantee.
● While NATO’s original mission may have been centered around collective defence, the
alliance has successfully adapted to modern security challenges that are key threats to its
member states.
○ NATO has taken on roles in combating terrorism, conducting peacekeeping
missions, and responding to cyber warfare. Its involvement in Afghanistan, although
controversial, demonstrated NATO’s willingness to engage in global
counter-terrorism efforts, which remain critical to its member states.
● NATO has expanded its influence beyond Europe through partnerships with non-member
countries and international organisations. These partnerships, including those with Japan,

Australia, and South Korea, allow NATO to continue to play a role in maintaining global
stability and addressing emerging threats.
○ The geopolitics may have changed, but NATO remains central.
Is NATO Effective?
Arguments That NATO Is Effective
Collective Defence Has Been Successful To Prevent Russian Aggression Against NATO:
● NATO’s core principle of collective defense, enshrined in Article 5, has been a powerful
deterrent against aggression from the Soviet Union and Russia, neither of which have
taken military action against a NATO member since they entered the alliance.
○ NATO’s commitment to protect member states has been shown since the invasion
of Ukraine, as it has enhanced its military presence on its eastern flank.
○ The Enhanced Forward Presence initiative deployed multinational battalions to
Poland, the Baltic states, and other Eastern European members to deter further
Russian advances.
● NATO’s ability to respond rapidly to the Ukraine crisis and bolster its eastern defenses is a
clear example of its relevance and effectiveness in ensuring European security. The
alliance’s actions, including military aid to Ukraine and increased troop deployments, have
been crucial in preventing Russia from threatening NATO member states directly.
○ This effectiveness convinced Finland and Sweden to take the risk of joining NATO in
2023 and 2024, despite Russia warning against it.
NATO Has Successfully Tackled Modern Threats In The Post-Cold War Era:
● NATO has successfully adapted its mission beyond traditional state-on-state conflicts to
address the emerging security threats that threaten its members, including terrorism, hybrid
warfare, and cyberattacks.
○ Following the 9/11 attacks, NATO invoked Article 5 for the first time in its history and
led the International Security Assistance Force (ISAF) in Afghanistan, showcasing
its ability to engage in global counter-terrorism efforts.
● In the realm of cyber defense, NATO has developed comprehensive strategies to protect its
members from cyberattacks, which have become a significant component of modern
warfare.
○ The alliance’s Cyber Defence Pledge and the integration of cyber threats into its
collective defense strategy (under Article 5) demonstrate NATO’s evolving
capabilities in addressing non-conventional threats. This ability to adapt to new
forms of warfare has proven its continued relevance.
NATO Has Helped To Promote Peace Across The World:
● Since the end of the Cold War, NATO has played a crucial role in maintaining peace and
stability across Europe, particularly in the volatile Balkans. NATO’s peacekeeping and
stabilisation efforts in Bosnia and Kosovo during the 1990s prevented large-scale conflicts
from spiraling further and have helped secure long-term peace in these areas.
○ By assisting in post-conflict reconstruction and humanitarian relief efforts, NATO has
proven itself a versatile security organization capable of managing a wide range of
global challenges.
● Further, by collaborating with non-member countries including Australia, Japan, and South
Korea, NATO continues to play a key role in maintaining broader global security.

Arguments That NATO Is Ineffective
NATO Has Failed To Prevent Russian Aggression In Europe:
● Critics argue that NATO has failed to prevent Russian aggression in its immediate
neighborhood. Russia’s annexation of Crimea in 2014 and its subsequent invasion of
Ukraine in 2022 highlight NATO’s limitations in stopping non-member states from being
attacked.
○ Further, Putin justified his invasion in 2022 on the grounds that it was triggered by
NATO expanding too close to Russia’s borders since the end of the Cold War, which
he argues threatened Russian security.
○ Putin pointed to NATO’s expansion into former Soviet states like the Baltic countries
and its growing relationship with Ukraine (which sought NATO membership) as
provocations.
● Furthermore, Russia’s continued interference in Eastern Europe, including its support for
separatist movements and cyberattacks on NATO members like Estonia, raises questions
about NATO’s ability to deter hybrid threats.
Lack Of Shared Vision and Commitment To NATO Among Members:
● One significant criticism is that NATO’s diverse membership has led to a lack of a coherent
strategic vision. While Eastern European nations focus on the threat from Russia, Western
European members and the U.S. are often more focused on global issues like terrorism as
well as the rise of China as the key strategic competitor.
● Further, the alliance has appeared divided and weak due to the issue of burden-sharing,
which became a particularly high profile issue under the presidencies of Donald Trump,
who openly criticised NATO as “obsolete” and questioned the US’s continued commitment
to the alliance if European nations did not increase their defence budgets.
○ Though many European countries did increase their spending in relation to Trump’s
pressure and the war in Ukraine, the US still bears a disproportionately large share
of the alliance’s military expenditure and another Trump presidency potentially
threatens the future of the alliance, which is highly dependent on the US.
○ Since Trump’s reelection and policy to end the war in Ukraine quickly on Russian
terms, many are now questioning whether the U.S. commitment to the collective
defence of Europe remains.
NATO Has Overextended Beyond Its Original Mandate:
● Critics argue that NATO’s role has expanded too far beyond its original purpose and
become ineffective as a result. NATO’s interventions in places like Afghanistan and Libya
have drawn criticism, with some viewing these operations as outside the alliance’s core
mission of collective defence against Russia.
○ The long and costly mission in Afghanistan, which ended with the Taliban regaining
control in 2021, is seen by many as a failure of NATO’s capacity to manage
non-conventional conflicts.
● Additionally, NATO’s global reach has raised questions about its strategic focus. By taking
on a wider range of roles, including crisis management, counter-terrorism, and cyber
defense, critics contend that NATO risks diluting its effectiveness.
○ Some argue this lack of focus and clear message contributed to Putin’s willingness
to invade Ukraine in 2022.
