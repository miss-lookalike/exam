Polarity And Its Implications
Unipolarity/Hegemony
What is Unipolarity?
Unipolarity refers to a global system where one state holds the majority of power, both militarily and
economically, over all others. In this system, the dominant state, often referred to as a “hegemon,”
has significant influence over global political, economic, and military affairs, with no near-peer
competitors challenging its supremacy. The unipolar state has the capacity to shape international
norms, enforce its preferred rules, and act as the primary enforcer of global order.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 3
Implications of Unipolarity: Stability or Instability?
Hegemonic Stability Theory:
Proponents of Hegemonic Stability Theory, such as Charles Kindleberger and Robert Gilpin,
argue that unipolarity leads to a stable international order. A dominant power with significant
military and economic resources can enforce global norms, provide public goods (like free trade
and security), and deter potential challengers. The hegemon’s role as a stabiliser ensures that
other states avoid disruptive behaviours, leading to fewer conflicts. For instance, after the end of
the Cold War, the United States emerged as the sole superpower, promoting free markets,
democratic governance, and military alliances that discouraged major wars.
Unipolarity Leads To Challenges and Instability:
Critics argue that unipolarity can generate instability, particularly due to the hegemon’s
tendency to overreach or act unilaterally. Realist scholars like John Mearsheimer contend that
other states, fearful of the hegemon’s overwhelming power, may seek to balance against it by
forming alliances or increasing their own military capabilities and challenge the superpower,
potentially leading to conflict. The hegemon might also face overextension, as it tries to impose its
will globally, leading to imperial overstretch, where its resources are drained by frequent
interventions or economic pressures. This instability is often exacerbated if the hegemon acts in
ways that provoke resentment or opposition from other states.
Case Study: The United States in the 1990s and 2000s
The United States in the 1990s and early 2000s is a clear example of a unipolar power. Following
the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, the U.S. emerged as the sole global superpower. The
U.S. dominated economically, with its share of global GDP around 25%, and militarily, spending
more on defense than the next 10 countries combined. The U.S. played a leading role in
establishing global norms, promoting free trade through the World Trade Organisation (WTO)
and democratic governance through institutions like NATO and the United Nations.
During this period, the U.S. engaged in military interventions in Iraq (1991) and the Balkans
(1999), asserting its dominance in global security. The U.S. also led the War on Terror following
the 9/11 attacks in 2001, launching military operations in Afghanistan and Iraq (2003). While
these actions reinforced U.S. dominance, they also provoked debates about overreach and led to
significant global opposition, particularly to the Iraq War. Although the unipolar world saw fewer
major conflicts between great powers, the unilateral actions of the U.S. in this era raised
concerns about the long-term sustainability of its hegemonic role.
Bipolarity
What is Bipolarity?
Bipolarity refers to an international system where two superpowers dominate global politics and
influence, with other states aligning themselves around these two major poles. In a bipolar system,
global affairs are heavily shaped by the rivalry between the two dominant powers, each trying to
maintain or expand its sphere of influence. The balance of power in a bipolar world is often argued
to be stable, as both powers are aware that direct confrontation could lead to catastrophic
consequences, particularly when both possess nuclear capabilities.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 4
Implications of Bipolarity: Stability or Instability?
Stability Argument (Balance of Power Theory):
Proponents of bipolarity, such as Kenneth Waltz, argue that it can lead to a stable international
system. In a bipolar world, each superpower has a clear adversary, and the rivalry between the
two often discourages direct conflict due to the threat of mutually assured destruction (MAD),
particularly when nuclear weapons are involved. The simplicity of the system—where only two
powers dominate—limits the number of potential conflicts, as the bipolar powers control their
respective spheres and deter smaller states from upsetting the balance. During the Cold War, for
example, both the U.S. and the Soviet Union avoided direct military confrontation, instead
engaging in proxy wars and diplomatic competition, which helped to prevent major global conflicts.
Instability Argument (Escalation and Proxy Conflicts):
Critics argue that bipolarity can lead to instability through proxy wars and escalating tensions.
While the two superpowers may avoid direct confrontation, they often engage in indirect conflicts
by supporting opposing sides in regional conflicts and seek to better the opposing superpower and
increase their power. This phenomenon was evident during the Cold War, where both the U.S. and
the Soviet Union intervened in wars across Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan, and Latin America,
fueling local instability and violence. These proxy wars not only caused widespread devastation in
the affected regions but also carried the constant risk of escalating into broader superpower
conflicts, whilst there was also the very real threat of nuclear war at times.
Case Study: The Cold War (1947–1991)
The Cold War is the clearest example of a bipolar world order. Following World War II, the global
system became dominated by two superpowers: the United States and the Soviet Union. The
U.S. led the Western bloc, which included capitalist democracies such as those in Western
Europe, supported the NATO military alliance. The Soviet Union, meanwhile, led the Eastern
bloc, composed of communist states in Eastern Europe, and supported its allies through the
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 5
Warsaw Pact and other socialist solidarity networks.
The rivalry between the U.S. and the Soviet Union shaped global affairs for over four decades.
While the two superpowers avoided direct military confrontation due to the risk of nuclear
annihilation, they engaged in numerous proxy wars, such as the Korean War (1950–1953), the
Vietnam War (1955–1975), and the Soviet-Afghan War (1979–1989). These conflicts allowed the
two powers to compete for influence in different regions without risking a direct conflict that could
escalate into nuclear war.
The nuclear arms race between the U.S. and the Soviet Union was a central feature of the Cold
War, leading to the development of large nuclear arsenals and the strategy of mutually assured
destruction (MAD), which deterred both sides from launching a first strike. The Cold War also
saw intense diplomatic competition, espionage, and ideological clashes between capitalism and
communism.
Despite the constant tension, many scholars argue that the bipolar structure of the Cold War
helped maintain global stability by creating a predictable balance of power. Each superpower was
constrained by the presence of the other, and major wars between the superpowers were
avoided. The Cold War ended with the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, leading to a shift
toward unipolarity with the U.S. as the sole superpower.
Multipolarity
What is Multipolarity?
Multipolarity refers to an international system where three or more states hold significant power,
with no single country or pair of countries dominating global affairs. In a multipolar world, multiple
great powers balance one another, and global stability is determined by the interactions, alliances,
and rivalries between them. Power is more diffused in this system compared to unipolarity or
bipolarity, leading to a more complex set of relationships where countries form shifting alliances to
counterbalance the power of others.
Implications of Multipolarity: Stability or Instability?
There is broad agreement among scholars that multipolarity leads to instability.
Instability Argument (Realist Perspective):
Many realist scholars, such as Hans Morgenthau, argue that multipolar systems tend to be less
stable than unipolar or bipolar ones because they introduce greater uncertainty. In a multipolar
world, the existence of several competing powers increases the complexity of the international
system, making it more difficult to maintain a stable balance of power. States may miscalculate
threats, form unstable alliances, and engage in more frequent conflicts as they seek to prevent any
one power from becoming too dominant.
Increased Risk of Conflict (Alliance Uncertainty):
Another perspective suggests that while multipolarity can provide short-term stability, it increases
the risk of long-term conflicts due to alliance uncertainty. In a multipolar world, alliances are more
fluid and less dependable. States may engage in bandwagoning (aligning with a more powerful
state) or balancing (aligning against a rising power), but these strategies are less predictable. This
creates a risk of sudden changes in alliances, making the international system more volatile and
prone to miscalculations, which can escalate into wars. This was evident during the lead-up to
World War I, when shifting alliances and entanglements between Germany, Austria-Hungary,
Russia, France, and Britain played a critical role in the outbreak of conflict.

Case Study: Between the World Wars (1919–1939)
The period between the two world wars (1919–1939) is a clear example of a multipolar system.
After World War I, the global balance of power was fragmented, with no single state emerging as
a clear hegemon. The Treaty of Versailles weakened Germany, while Britain and France
struggled to maintain their colonial empires, and the United States retreated into isolationism.
Meanwhile, new powers like Japan and Italy sought to assert themselves on the global stage,
and the Soviet Union began to rise as a revolutionary power.
This multipolar system was characterized by shifting alliances and competing interests. For
example, the League of Nations was established as a mechanism for collective security, but it
lacked the enforcement power to prevent aggression from revisionist states like Nazi Germany,
Fascist Italy, and Imperial Japan. The Appeasement Policy pursued by Britain and France in the
1930s, especially towards Hitler’s Germany, further destabilized the system, as it allowed
Germany to expand its territory unchecked, leading to the invasion of Poland in 1939 and the
start of World War II.
The interwar period demonstrated both the fluidity and dangers of a multipolar world. While some
states attempted to maintain peace through diplomacy, the lack of a strong hegemon or stable
alliances created an environment ripe for conflict. The system’s inherent instability, combined with
economic pressures from the Great Depression, led to the rise of authoritarian regimes and set
the stage for WWII: the most devastating conflict in human history.
Transitions Between Different States Of Polarity
In the study of international relations, transitions between unipolarity and bipolarity/multipolarity are
seen as periods that can lead to significant confliuct. Various theories have been developed to
explain how these transitions impact the international system and how states act and whether they
seek to maximise power. Below are some key theories that it is worth thinking about when
considering the rise of China, how it will act and how it will influence global politics.
The Thucydides Trap
The Thucydides Trap, popularised by political scientist Graham Allison, is rooted in the historical
analysis of the Peloponnesian War by the ancient historian Thucydides. The theory posits that
when a rising power threatens to displace an established hegemon, the likelihood of conflict
increases dramatically, due to fear and miscalculation between the rising power and established
hegemon. Thucydides famously observed that the war between Athens (the rising power) and
Sparta (the established hegemon) was caused by “the growth of Athenian power and the fear that
this caused in Sparta.”
In modern times, the Thucydides Trap is often applied to the relationship between China (the rising
power) and the United States (the established hegemon), with the concern that China’s rise may
lead to heightened tensions or even war. However, Allison notes that conflict is not inevitable. Of
16 historical cases he studied where a rising power challenged an established power, 12 resulted
in war, but 4 did not. This suggests that while transitions in polarity can be highly unstable, conflict
can be avoided through diplomacy, restraint, and managing expectations between powers.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 7
Offensive Realism
Offensive Realism, developed by John Mearsheimer, argues that states are inherently aggressive
and seek to maximise their power in an anarchic international system. In this view, states are never
satisfied with the status quo and constantly strive to dominate the international system to ensure
their security. As a rising power, China, according to offensive realism, will likely pursue policies
aimed at displacing the U.S. and expanding its influence globally, even if it means risking conflict.
Mearsheimer contends that as China grows stronger, it will become more assertive in regional
disputes and challenge U.S. dominance, potentially leading to a security dilemma where both
powers escalate tensions.
Defensive Realism
In contrast to offensive realism, Defensive Realism, associated with Kenneth Waltz, argues that
states seek security rather than domination. According to defensive realists, states are primarily
concerned with maintaining their position in the international system rather than expanding their
power. In this view, China’s rise may not necessarily lead to conflict if China prioritises maintaining
stability and avoiding actions that provoke balancing coalitions or military confrontation. Defensive
realists believe that as long as the U.S. and China focus on defensive strategies, such as
deterrence and diplomacy, they can avoid war even during periods of power transition.
Liberalism
From a liberal perspective, power transitions need not result in conflict if states are embedded in
strong international institutions and global governance mechanisms. Liberals argue that institutions
like the United Nations and World Trade Organisation (WTO) provide frameworks for cooperation
that reduce the likelihood of war by promoting interdependence and dialogue. According to this
view, the rise of China can be managed peacefully if both China and the U.S. work within these
institutions, engaging in multilateral diplomacy, free trade, and conflict resolution mechanisms.
Liberals also emphasise the importance of economic interdependence, where the costs of war
between major powers, especially economically linked ones like the U.S. and China, are extremely
high and prevent war as a result.
The Changing Nature Of The World Order Since 2000
Since the turn of the millennium, the global order has undergone significant transformations,
characterised by the rise of new actors and the challenge they have posed to the United States’
hegemony it had experienced since the end of the Cold War. In the 2000s and especially in the
2010s and 2020s so far, various developments began to challenge U.S. dominance, leading to a

more multipolar, complex and unstable international system. The extent to which these
developments have truly challenged U.S. supremacy is fiercly debated.
The Rise of China
For more detail on the Rise of China, look at the ‘State Power Classifications’ video on the Politics
Explained YouTube Channel and the accompanying section of the Global Politics Updated
Textbook on the Politics Explained website.
● The most significant change in the global order since 2000 has been the rise of China.
Beginning in the late 20th century, China’s economic growth accelerated, with its GDP
growing at an average rate of 10% annually.
○ By 2010, China had surpassed Japan to become the world’s second-largest
economy, and it continues to challenge U.S. dominance in various economic
spheres.
● After a long period of laying low geopolitically, China began to assert itself from the
mid-2010s.
○ It launched The Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) to create economic interdependence
with nations across Asia, Africa, and Europe, enhancing its geopolitical reach.
○ China also launched the Made in China 2025 initiative focusing on making it a
global leader in technology, green energy and other highly geopolitically and
economically significant industries to challenge the US.
○ It has also sought to carve out a sphere of influence in Asia to increase its influence
and protect its trade routes, including militarily by controlling and building islands in
the South China Sea.
The Resurgence of Russia
● Another significant development has been the resurgence of Russia as a major player on
the global stage. Following the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, Russia experienced a
period of decline, but under Vladimir Putin’s leadership, Russia has reasserted itself
militarily and geopolitically.
○ Russia’s military interventions in Georgia (2008), Ukraine (2014 and 2022), and
Syria (2015) demonstrate its willingness to challenge the U.S. and NATO’s influence
in Eurasia and the Middle East.
● Russia has actively sought to challenge the dominance of the United States and the West,
by providing military support to Syria, invading Ukraine to challenge NATO and using its
energy leverage over Europe as one of the world’s biggest oil and natural gas producers.
Increased Cooperation and Alignment Between Russia, China, and Iran
In recent years, there has been a notable increase in cooperation between Russia, China, and
Iran, further altering the global balance of power. This alignment is driven by shared opposition to
U.S. influence and a desire to reshape global governance in a manner that reduces Western
dominance.
● Russia and China have deepened their military and economic cooperation, as seen through
joint military exercises, China’s tacit support for Russia’s war in Ukraine and China’s
reliance on Russian energy to fuel its growing economy.
○ Both countries share a common interest in counterbalancing U.S. dominance,
particularly in Eurasia.

● Iran has strengthened its ties with both Russia and China, particularly in response to U.S.
sanctions. China’s involvement in Iran, including a 25-year strategic partnership and the
fact it buys 90% of Iran’s oil, has bolstered Tehran’s position, while Russia’s support for Iran
in Syria and other regional theaters has further solidified their alignment.
This trilateral relationship has been characterised by their opposition to Western intervention in
Syria and joint economic ventures that challenge U.S. sanctions. This partnership exemplifies a
shift toward a world order that challenges U.S. dominance and significantly bolster’s China’s
influence as the primary competitor to the United States.
The Emergence Of Other Minor Powers With Regional Influence
In addition to China and Russia, other rising powers have emerged. This has made geopolitics
even more fragmented and potentially indicates a shift towards a multipolar world order.
● India has seen remarkable economic growth, becoming the fifth-largest economy by 2024.
With a population exceeding 1.4 billion, India has become a major player in technology,
pharmaceuticals, and defense, while asserting its influence in South Asia and beyond.
● Turkey has strengthened its regional role, particularly in the Middle East and the Eastern
Mediterranean, with assertive foreign policies and military actions, such as its involvement
in Syria and Libya. Under President Erdogan, Turkey has balanced relations with both the
West (through NATO) and Russia, expanding its geopolitical significance.
● Brazil, though facing internal challenges, remains a key player in South America. As part of
the BRICS group (Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa), Brazil has sought to
expand its influence in global economic governance and regional affairs.
● Saudi Arabia, driven by its Vision 2030 reform program, aims to diversify its economy and
reduce its reliance on oil. The kingdom’s strategic importance in global energy markets
makes it a significant actor in global geopolitics.
● Iran continues to challenge the U.S.-led order in the Middle East, particularly through its
influence in regional conflicts in Syria, Yemen, and Iraq, as well as its contentious nuclear
program, which remains a major international concern.

The Rise of Populism in the US and Western Democracies
In the 2010s in particular, populism has surged in Western democracies, challenging the liberal
international order established after World War II. The rise of populism in the U.S. and Europe has
led to increasing skepticism about globalisation, free trade, and multilateralism. This has weakened
Western cohesion, as populist leaders often prioritise national sovereignty over cooperation with
international institutions.
● In the United States, the election of Donald Trump in 2016 marked a sharp turn toward
nationalism and an ‘America First’ foreign policy, which prioritised U.S. interests over global
cooperation.
○ Trump’s approach included withdrawing from key international agreements, such as
the Paris Climate Accord and the Iran Nuclear Deal, and taking a more
confrontational stance on trade with China and the European Union.
● This isolationism and nationalism indicated that the U.S. was now less willing to
assert itself across the world and protect other countries as a ‘global police force’ it
had acted as since the end of the Cold War.
● In Europe, populist movements in countries like Hungary, Poland, and Italy have similarly
challenged the European Union and its policies on immigration and economic integration,
creating tensions within one of the world’s most important multilateral institutions and
creating vulnerability to non-Western actors.
The Impact Of The Current Balance Of Power On
Contemporary Global Issues
As the global balance of power transitions from the post-Cold War hegemony of the United States
to a more contested global order, its effects are felt across various contemporary global issues.
The shift has altered the dynamics and potential for resolution in relation to conflict, human rights,
poverty, and the environment.
Conflict
Increased Multipolar Competition and Regional Conflicts
● The shift to a multipolar world has led to greater competition among major powers,
increasing the likelihood of regional conflicts.
○ While the US dominated militarily during its unipolar moment and this made other
states more wary of starting conflict, today, countries such as Russia and China
have become more assertive in their regions.
● Russia’s interventions in Georgia (2008) and Ukraine (2014, 2022) reflect its determination
to challenge NATO’s influence in Eastern Europe.
○ Similarly, China’s expansion in the South China Sea has heightened tensions with
neighbouring countries and the US, contributing to regional instability.
● The multipolar competition has also resulted in proxy conflicts, such as in Syria, where
powers like the US, Russia, and Iran have been involved, making diplomatic resolutions
harder to achieve.
https://www.politicsexplained.co.uk/ 11
Decline in Global Conflict Mediation
● Under US hegemony, the US played a leading role in mediating conflicts and enforcing
peace agreements, as seen in the Balkans in the 1990s. However, the contested balance of
power has made global conflict resolution more complex.
○ With the US reducing its involvement in global conflicts and international
organisations like the UN Security Council frequently deadlocked due to competing
interests among great powers, the ability to address conflicts swiftly has diminished.
● For example, diplomatic efforts to end the Syrian Civil War and the Israeli-Hamas war have
faltered, in part due to the lack of unified leadership and cooperation among major powers.
Declining US Unilateral Intervention
● During the unipolar period after the Cold War, the US frequently acted alone in military
interventions, most notably in the 2003 Iraq War.
○ This war was launched without broad international support, but the US decided to
ignore international law and pursue it anyway, recognising its supreme power.
● In the current multipolar world, the US has become more cautious about acting unilaterally.
The rise of powers like Russia and China means that US interventions are more likely to
face resistance or counterbalancing actions, as seen in Syria.
● This shift has also been accompanied by a broader move toward isolationism in recent
years, particularly under the Trump administration’s “America First” policy, which prioritised
domestic issues over global military engagements.
○ As a result, the US has scaled back its involvement in conflicts like Syria and
Afghanistan, leaving power vacuums that regional actors, such as Russia and
Turkey, have filled.
○ The reduced US interventionism reflects the more complex and contested global
environment, where unilateral military actions are increasingly difficult to justify or
sustain.

Human Rights
Weakened Protection of Human Rights
● In the post-Cold War period, the US led international efforts to promote human rights
globally, including through humanitarian interventions in Bosnia, Kosovo and Sierra Lione,
many of which were driven through the UN Security Council.
● The US declining reputation as a result of its human rights abuses during the War on Terror
as well as the rise of China and Russia has complicated these efforts, though.
○ China and Russia don’t prioritise liberal democratic values and have pushed back
against the Western conception of human rights, advocating for sovereignty and
non-interference in domestic affairs.
● This has weakened international mechanisms that protect human rights, such as UN
resolutions and sanctions.
○ For example, China has blocked attempts to investigate human rights abuses in
Xinjiang at the UN, showcasing how a multipolar world can dilute efforts to uphold
universal human rights norms.
Poverty
Diverging Economic Models and Development Pathways
● The shift to a more multipolar world has led to the rise of competing economic models,
influencing poverty reduction efforts and giving developing states more choice and
opportunity.
○ Under US hegemony, the neoliberal model, emphasising free markets, was the
dominant approach and much-needed financial assistance from the IMF and World
Bank was often made condition on implementing austerity measures.
● While globalisation led to significant poverty reduction in countries like China and India, it
also deepened inequality in many parts of the world and many of the IMF and World Bank’s
Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs) had disastrous effects on poverty.
● In the multipolar order, China’s state-managed capitalist model, which has lifted hundreds
of millions out of poverty, offers an alternative to the Western neoliberal model and its
financial support an alternative to the IMF and World Bank.
○ China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) provides developing countries with
infrastructure investment, often without the stringent political conditions attached to
Western aid, though critics argue it risks creating debt dependencies.
Weakening of Multilateral Cooperation on Poverty Alleviation
● However, the contest for influence between major powers has weakened coordinated
efforts to address global poverty.
○ Organisations like the World Bank and IMF, previously dominated by the US and
Western allies, now face competition from institutions like the China-led Asian
Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB).
● This fragmentation reduces the cohesion of international efforts to alleviate poverty, as
countries receiving aid or loans often find themselves caught between competing
geopolitical interests.
● Additionally, global trade disputes, such as the US-China trade war, have disrupted global
supply chains, negatively affecting developing economies that rely on international trade for
growth.

Environment
Stalled Global Cooperation on Climate Change
● Addressing environmental issues, particularly climate change, has become more difficult in
a multipolar world.
○ The US played a key role in the establishment of the Paris Agreement in 2015, but
its subsequent withdrawal under President Trump and the fragmented leadership
among global powers have undermined progress on climate action.
● China and India, as major polluters, have committed to addressing climate change but have
prioritised economic growth, recognising it is necessary to increase their power and
challenge US hegemony. Similarly, developed countries have been unwilling to accept the
burden of tackling climate change as doing so would harm their economies and power.
○ This lack of US hegemony has made it more difficult to implement and enforce
ambitious environmental targets abnd enforce binding agreements on emissions
reductions.
New Opportunities for Green Technology Leadership
● Despite these challenges, the rise of other powers also presents opportunities for
environmental progress, as competition among powers has spurred innovations in green
technology.
● China has recognised the geopolitical importance of renewable energy and has become a
global leader, investing heavily in solar, wind, and electric vehicle technologies.
● The European Union, too, has maintained a strong leadership role on climate change,
pushing for ambitious goals like carbon neutrality by 2050.
○ These initiatives indicate that, although global cooperation has weakened, regional
efforts and technological competition may still drive progress on environmental
issues.

Key Recent Examples
The Expansion Of The BRICs in 2024 and 2025 - Example Of The Shift Away From A Unipolar
World Order:
● In 2024 and 2025, the BRICs group significantly expanded its membership, with Egypt,
Ethiopia, Iran, and the United Arab Emirates joining in 2024, and Indonesia becoming the
first Southeast Asian nation to become a member in 2025.
○ This expansion reflects a growing challenge to Western-dominated institutions like
the G7, IMF, and World Bank, with countries more willing to align themselves with
Russia and China who are dominant in the group.
● Along with holding annual conferences, the BRICs group has coordinated a number of
policies that challenged U.S. hegemony, including seeking to move away from dependence
on the dollar, cooperation on artificial intelligence and creating ‘BRICS Pay’; a system to
allow financial transactions without having to rely on Western banks.
● This is a clear example of how U.S. hegemony and the unipolar world order that existed
after the end of the Cold War is being challenged by Russia and China in particular, who
are aligning themselves with a growing number of countries.
Marco Rubio Accepts Multipolar World Order In Interview - Example Of Declining U.S.
Hegemony:
● In a January 2025 interview, U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio acknowledged that the
era of American unipolar dominance after the end of the Cold War was over and that the
U.S. would have to now adapt to a multipolar world order.
○ He said “It’s not normal for the world to simply have a unipolar power. That was an
anomaly…eventually you were going to reach back to a point where you had a
multipolar world”.
● As a result, Rubio suggested that the U.S. should no longer seek to project power in every
area, be the global police force and decide the rules of global politics. Instead, it should
project its national interests and seek to maximise its power in a multipolar world.
● This potentially explains Trump’s justification from stepping back from supporting Ukraine
and is a useful example for their now being a multipolar world order, as this has been
recognised by a key foreign policy official of the U.S.
DeepSeek - Example Of China’s Rise As A Technological and Economic Superpower:
● In early 2025, Chinese AI firm DeepSeek made international headlines after launching a
highly effective AI model that challenged U.S. Ai giants like Nvidia and OpenAI, whilst being
much cheaper to operate. It resulted in a 17% drop in Nvidia’s stock price.
○ This highlighted how China is effectively challenging U.S. technological dominance
in highly important areas of the economy like AI and electric vehicles, partly due to
its economic model where the Chinese government provides major support to
nationally important industries and companies.
○ Further, this will potentially also influence the U.S.’s military dominance, as
DeepSeek is being integrated into China’s military-industrial complex.
● Overall, this is a useful example of how China is a superpower that is providing a highly
effective challenge to U.S. hegemony.

The U.S. vs China Trade War - Example Of Bipolar Competition And How The Transition Away
From A Unipolar World Order Creates Instability:
● Since Trump started his second term as President in 2025, there has been a massive
escalation in U.S. vs China competition, including a major trade war where each country
has placed extremely high tariffs on each other.
○ In April, Trump announced a 145% tariff on Chinese imports, whilst China retaliated
with a 125% tariff on U.S. imports. These rates have since decreased after a deal
was signed on 12 May 2025.
○ This has led to a sharp decline in trade between the two countries and has caused
significant instability to the global economy.
● This suggests that the current world order is becoming increasingly bipolar, with the U.S.
and China in direct economic competition; a major shift away from the previous era of
economic cooperation and integrated supply chains under globalisation.
○ Further, it shows how transitions between different stages of polarity (in this case
from unipolarity to bipolarity/multipolarity due to the rise in China’s power) create
major tensions and instability in the world.
○ This is supported by both offensive realism and the Thucydides Trap.
The Domestic Economic Consequences Of Trump’s Tariffs - Example Of The United States
Being A Declining Superpower:
● In the first few months of his 2nd term as President, Trump’s extremely erratic and reckless
tariff policy (including placing a 10% tariff on all goods - a reduction from previously higher
tariffs a few weeks before - as well as a 145% tariff on imports from China in April 2025)
triggered major instability in the U.S. economy.
○ In late April, there was a shock to the U.S. bond market due to foreign investors
losing confidence in the U.S. economy, whilst the U.S. stock market is now also
significantly lower than it was when Trump won reelection in November.
● The U.S. economy is the bedrock of its power and it can therefore be suggested that
Trump’s reckless economic policies may cause the U.S. to be a declining superpower.
Competition In The Arctic - Example Of A Multipolar World Order Leading To Greater Global
Instability:
● In 2025 especially, the Arctic has become a key point of geopolitical competition between
Arctic states (especially the U.S. and Russia), due to climate change leading to ice caps
melting and the potential for a new sea lane that reduced the travel time for goods between
Europe and Asia, as well as due to the potential for key natural resource reserves in the
Arctic.
○ Trump has spoken both of acquiring Greenland by force and of seeking to make
Canada (another key Arctic state) part of the United States.
○ Russia has increased its military presence in the Arctic, including by launching a
nuclear-powered submarine in the area.
● Together, this is a good example of how there is increasing multipolar competition for key
parts of the world and how the resurgence of Russia and shift away from American
hegemony is leading to greater global instability and potential conflict over parts of the
world.

Is The Current World Order Unipolar, Bipolar or Multipolar?
The Argument That The World Order Is Unipolar
Some argue that the world order remains unipolar, as the rise of China has been exaggerated and
the United States continues to dominate the global order economically, militarily and in terms of
alliances with great powers.
● Economically, the U.S. remains the world’s largest economy in terms of GDP. China
may be a close second, but the US is the most technologically advanced nation and
its economic power has a number of key structural factors that go beyond simple
GDP numbers.
○ The dominance of the U.S. dollar as the world’s reserve currency (accounting for
nearly 60% of global reserves) ensures American influence in global trade and
financial systems.
○ 7 out of the top 10 global brands and 61 out of the top 100 global brands are
American. These include Amazon, Apple, Google and Tesla.
○ The New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and NASDAQ are the largest stock
exchanges in the world, setting the pace for global investment and innovation.
○ The U.S. has the best universities and research institutions in the world.
● Militarily, the United States remains far more powerful than any other military and is
the only country with a truly global military presence.
○ The U.S. has over 700 military bases across the world.
○ Its defense budget of $877 billion in 2023 is larger than the next 10 countries
combined, including China’s which it is four times bigger than.
● The United States’ alliances and structural power in global governance is far more
powerful than China’s.
○ U.S. alliances with the European great powers of the United Kingdom, France and
Germany through NATO are extremely powerful.
○ The U.S. is also a much closer partner than China is to other important great
powers and economies closer to China, in particular India and Japan.
■ The U.S. has reinforced its military alliances in the Indo-Pacific, particularly
through the QUAD (with Japan, India, and Australia) and AUKUS (with
Australia and the UK), aimed at countering China’s influence in the region.
○ U.S.-led institutions like the World Bank, IMF, and NATO continue to uphold the
liberal international order established after World War II, reinforcing U.S. hegemony.
● China’s rise has been exaggerated in a number of ways:
○ The size and ability of its military to project power globally is far smaller than that of
the United States.
○ It lacks a comprehensive network of alliances that is able to challenge the U.S.
○ China lacks the soft power and influence in international institutions of the U.S.
○ Its currency is far less powerful than the dollar, which continues to dominate.
The Argument That The World Order Is Bipolar
The case for bipolarity argues that the global order is increasingly characterised by the rivalry
between two dominant powers: the United States and China. This dynamic is often compared to

the Cold War bipolarity between the U.S. and the Soviet Union, where two superpowers shaped
global alliances, trade networks, and military strategies.
● China’s economy will soon become the world’s largest and it is challenging the
United States in key sectors.
○ China’s nominal GDP is second only to the U.S. is expected to surpass it and
become the world’s largest economy by 2035.
○ China is increasingly competitive in cutting-edge sectors like 5G technology, artificial
intelligence (AI), and electric vehicles (EVs). Chinese companies like Huawei and
BYD are global leaders in these areas.
○ China accounts for 30% of the world’s manufacturing output, making it the largest
producer of goods globally. It has entrenched itself in global supply chains, with
many countries and corporations dependent on Chinese production.
● China has become more militarily strong and geopolitically assertive, with a clear
goal to become a global superpower and challenge the United States.
○ China has increased its defense spending to around $230 billion in 2023, building a
modern and technologically advanced military, with a focus on naval expansion and
missile technology.
○ China has aggressively asserted territorial claims in the South China Sea by
building artificial islands and militarising key areas.
○ China’s President Xi has a clear vision to bring about the rejuvenation of the
Chinese nation as a global superpower. He has pursued this through Made In China
2025 and the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI).
● China is building alliances to challenge the U.S.-led International Order.
○ China has deepened cooperation with Russia and Iran, sharing common goals of
reducing U.S. influence globally, particularly in Eurasia and the Middle East.
○ Through the Belt and Road Initiative, China has cultivated close economic ties with
over 140 countries, creating economic interdependence and providing them with an
alternative to U.S.-led institutions like the World Bank and IMF.
○ China’s influence is growing in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America, regions
where it has become a leading investor and trading partner, challenging traditional
U.S. influence.
● The United States is treating China as a major threat and its main strategic
competitor, showing its growing power.
○ The U.S. Department of Defense explicitly identifies China as the most significant
long-term threat to U.S. global dominance, with a focus on countering China’s
growing influence in the Indo-Pacific region.
○ The U.S. has initiated a trade war with China and imposed tariffs and sanctions on
Chinese technology firms, aiming to limit China’s access to critical technologies,
particularly in semiconductors and telecommunications.
The Argument That The World Order Is Multipolar
Those who argue that the current world order is multipolar believe that power is distributed among
multiple states and regions, rather than being dominated by one or two superpowers. This view
highlights the increasing influence of China, Russia, India, and other emerging powers, which are
challenging the dominance of the United States and creating a more decentralised global order.

● There are multiple great powers including China, India, Russia, Brazil, Saudi Arabia
and Turkey which are asserting themselves and have significant influence in their
respective regions.
○ India, with its growing economy and military, is asserting itself in South Asia and the
Indo-Pacific. It is now the world’s fifth-largest economy and a crucial player in
international affairs, particularly as a balancing power between China and U.S.
○ Russia, despite economic sanctions and challenges, continues to exert influence in
Eurasia and the Middle East, as seen in its interventions in Ukraine and Syria and
its growing ties with China and Iran.
○ Brazil and Saudi Arabia have become key players in the BRICS and OPEC groups,
respectively, and wield significant influence in energy markets and regional politics.
● The United States is no longer willing or able to dominate in every region of the
world at the same time.
○ The U.S. remains a superpower, but the growing power of a number of countries
across the world has meant they are increasingly stretched and no longer able to
dominate in Europe, Asia and the Middle East at the same time.
○ Developing countries no longer see the U.S.-led international order as the only
means of support, with Russia, China and others building alliances and asserting
themselves in Africa and South America in particular.
○ A significant portion of the U.S. population and political class, led by Trump’s
populism, is no longer willing to spend enormous sums seeking to project American
power in every corner of the globe at all times.
■ This has and will continue to create opportunities for other states to exert
power in varied regions.
