Paragraph One
Point Of Criticism
The most important criticism of both the IMF and World Bank is that their promotion of
neoliberal economic policies as the solution to poverty and underdevelopment did far more
harm to developing countries than it helped, as their neoliberal views were based on flawed
economic thinking.
The International Monetary Fund IMF
The IMFʼs Structural Adjustment Programs SAPs), implemented in the 1980s and 1990s,
required countries to adopt austerity measures, deregulation, and privatisation as conditions for
loans.
This was based on the false belief that free market capitalism would promote economic
development, when in reality these policies led to increased poverty and economic
stagnation.
For example, in Zambia during the 1990s, IMF and World Bank-imposed structural adjustment
programs led to widespread privatisation and significant cuts in public spending, particularly inExamine The Main Criticisms Of The International Monetary Fund IMF and World Bank. 12 Politics Explained 12 Marker Plan1
healthcare and education.
This resulted in mass layoffs and drove poverty rates from 49% in 1989 to over 80% by the
mid-1990s, severely worsening living conditions and delaying economic recovery for over a
decade.
The World Bank
Similarly, the World Bankʼs belief in neoliberal capitalism has led to the promotion of
privatisation as a condition for loans. This has often led to reduced access to essential services
for vulnerable populations, as private profit is prioritised over access for all.
For example, in Bolivia, the World Bank-backed privatisation of water services in
Cochabamba in the early 2000s resulted in a tripling of water prices, sparking mass
protests known as the “Water War.ˮ
Paragraph Two
Point Of Criticism
Another significant criticism of is their governance structures, which disproportionately favour
wealthy nations. Voting power in both the IMF and World Bank is tied to financial contributions,
giving wealthy Western states like the US outsized influence.
The International Monetary Fund IMF
In the IMF, voting power is heavily skewed toward developed Western economies, with the
United States holding over 16% of the votes, effectively granting it veto power over major
decisions that require an 85% majority.
This dominance has allowed the U.S. to shape IMF policies to align with its own economic and
geopolitical interests, despite the fact the IMF is supposed to support smaller countries and the
global economy as a whole.
For example, during the Asian Financial Crisis in the late 1990s, IMF policies heavily
prioritised U.S. banking interests, imposing stringent conditions that exacerbated the crisis
in countries like Indonesia and Thailand.
The World Bank
Like the IMF, the governance structure of the World Bank has been criticised for
disproportionately favouring wealthy, Western nations. Voting power within the World Bank is
determined by financial contributions, meaning that richer countries have significantly more
influence over decisions than poor countries, the primary recipients of its loans.
The United States holds about 15.65% of the voting power in the International Bank for
Reconstruction and Development IBRD, whilst African nations collectively hold around 7%
of the voting power.
Indonesia and Pakistan, the two countries in the world with the closest population size to
the US, have just 1.04% and 0.52% vote share respectively in the IBRD.

A final criticism of both the IMF and World Bank is that they undermine the sovereignty of
developing states. The conditionality attached to their loans forces countries to adopt policies
that prioritise the interests of international creditors over local populations.
This has particularly affected developing nations with limited financial alternatives, trapping
many in cycles of debt where they must borrow to repay previous loans and leaving little room
to invest in sustainable development or poverty reduction.
The International Monetary Fund IMF
Conditional lending and the importance of the IMFʼs loans forces governments to cede
significant control over their economic policies to them, which some critics argue amounts to
financial imperialism.
Loan conditions can require changes in governance structures or key national policies even if
these reforms are deeply unpopular, undermining democracy the ability of states to respond to
their citizensʼ needs.
For example, in Pakistan, IMF loan conditions required strict fiscal reforms, including
reductions in energy subsidies, which led to widespread protests as citizens faced rising
utility costs and there was a sense of diminished national control over policymaking.
The World Bank
The World Bank has faced similar criticisms, as its large-scale infrastructure projects often
leave countries burdened with debt, whilst failing to deliver the promised economic benefits to
local communities.
For example, in Uganda, the World Bank-backed Bujagali Hydropower Project displaced
over 8,700 people and cost $902 million, yet delivered electricity prices that were
unaffordable for many citizens, leaving the country with significant debt and limited
economic growth.
