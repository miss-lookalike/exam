Paragraph One
Point Of Difference
An important difference between the beliefs of shallow-green ecology and deep-green ecology
is that the former is anthropocentric, whilst the latter is biocentric.
Shallow-Green Ecology
Shallow-green ecology adopts an anthropocentric view, prioritising human well-being and
economic growth and seeking to tackle climate change within the context of these primary
objectives.
It is this perspective that has influenced national governmentsʼ and the international
communityʼs efforts to tackle climate change without impeding economic growth.
The European Unionʼs Emissions Trading System ETS exemplifies this approach. It caps
greenhouse gas emissions, but allows companies to trade allowances, using market
incentives to reduce environmental impact without disrupting economic growth

By contrast, deep-green ecology is biocentric and emphasises the intrinsic value of the planet
and environment, which must be protected irrespective of how useful they are to humanity.
Deep-green ecologists suggest that climate change and environmental degradation have
been caused by human economic needs always being placed above the health of nature
and argue that this must be flipped in order to meaningfully tackle climate change and
protect the planet.
For instance, the degrowth movement inspired by deep-green ecology advocates for
reducing consumption and shifting away from GDP growth as a measure of societal
success.
Paragraph Two
Point Of Difference
Another key difference between shallow-green and deep-green ecology is how radical their
policy suggestions are, in particular whether they believe that climate change can be addressed
within the existing economic system or whether a fundamental restructuring of the global
economy is required.
Shallow-Green Ecology
Shallow-green ecologists argue that climate change can be tackled effectively within the
current global capitalist system and supports practical, incremental reforms to tackle climate
change.
They support investing in technological innovation to tackle climate change, whilst slowly
shifting to renewable energy and using market mechanisms such as emissions trading
systems to tackle climate change.
Shallow-green principles are reflected in government subsidies for electric vehicles (EVs),
which promote the adoption of cleaner transportation options without disrupting broader
economic systems.
The U.S. Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 allocated billions to EV subsidies, supporting both
climate goals and the automotive industry, without forcing people to immediately shift away
from cars that burn petrol/diesel.
Deep-Green Ecology
Deep-green ecologists strongly disagree and argue that this approach is insufficient, as it fails
to address the systemic overconsumption and exploitation of natural resources that is inherent
to global capitalism.
They instead support transformative policies that challenge industrial and capitalist
systems, calling for a permanent shift away from fossil fuels and towards local, self-
sufficient communities.
Arne Næss, the Norwegian philosopher who coined the term “deep ecologyˮ, argues that a
fundamental shift in values is needed to tackle climate change, rather than incremental change.
Paragraph Three

A final difference between shallow-green ecology and deep-green ecology is the varying level
of success they have had in influencing global efforts and agreements that aim to tackle climate
change.
Shallow-Green Ecology
Shallow-green ecology has been formed the intellectual basis of the most prominent
international efforts and agreements to tackle climate change to date. It has naturally gained
support from governments and corporations who hold the real power and have an interest in
continued economic growth.
This can be seen in the Paris Agreement, which supported a flexible, market-friendly
approach to tackling climate change. It allows countries to set their own Nationally
Determined Contributions NDCs), enabling them to align their climate goals with economic
growth, while encouraging innovation and investment in cleaner technologies rather than
mandating disruptive systemic changes.
Deep-Green Ecology
In contrast, deep-green ecology remains a minority perspective, largely limited to grassroots
activism and smaller environmental movements. While influential in shaping the rhetoric of
youth climate campaigners like Greta Thunberg, its radical proposals have faced resistance
from government, particularly those in the West.
Movements like Extinction Rebellion, for example, have drawn on deep-green principles to
demand drastic measures such as net-zero emissions by 2025, yet these calls have largely
been dismissed as unfeasible by policymakers.
