Paragraph One
Point Of Difference
One important difference is that the member states have ceded some sovereignty over
economic issues to supranational EU institutions, whilst military and security issues remain
intergovernmental.
Economic Power
In the EU, significant economic control has been ceded to supranational institutions like the
European Commission and the European Central Bank ECB. These bodies enforce uniform
economic policies across member states and allow the EU to act as a cohesive economic bloc,
enhancing its economic influence.
The ECB sets monetary policy for the Eurozone, managing interest rates and inflation across
20 member states, ensuring stability but limiting individual countriesʼ flexibility to address
national economic crises.
For example, in response to the Eurozone debt crisis, the ECB purchased government
bonds to stabilise struggling economies including Greece and Italy.Examine The Differences Between The European Unionʼs Economic Power and Its Military/Security Power. 12 Politics Explained 12 Marker Plan1
Military/Security Power
By contrast, military and security matters remain strictly intergovernmental, requiring
consensus among member states for decisions to be made. This ensures that countries retain
sovereignty over national defence and are not compelled to act against their will.
For example, during Turkeyʼs military incursion into northern Syria in 2019, EU member
states were divided, with France and Germany calling for an arms embargo and
condemnation, while Hungary and others prioritiaed maintaining diplomatic and strategic
ties with Turkey as a key NATO ally.
This division prevented the EU from adopting a unified stance, but also prevented any state
having to act against their will.
Paragraph Two
Point Of Difference
Another difference is that the EU is able to have much more global influence on economic
issues in comparison to military/security issues.
Economic Power
The EUʼs status as the largest global trading bloc gives it immense economic influence, allowing
it to shape global trade standards and regulation. In fact the ‘Brussels Effectʼ refers to the fact
that companies and countries often adopt EU standards voluntarily to access its vast single
market.
For example, the EUʼs General Data Protection Regulation GDPR has become a global
benchmark for data privacy laws, influencing legislation in countries such as Japan, Brazil,
and India to align with its stringent standards.
The size of its single market and customs union also ensures it has leverage in global trade
negotiations.
For example, the EU negotiated the EUCanada Comprehensive Economic and Trade
Agreement CETA in 2014, which eliminated 98% of tariffs between the two regions.
Military/Security Power
On the other hand, the EUʼs global influence in military and security matters is far more limited
due to its lack of hard power. The absence of a unified EU military means that it relies on NATO
for major defence efforts, which prevents it from being an effective global security/military
power on par with the United States or China.
While the EU has undertaken missions like Operation Atalanta to combat piracy off the
Somali coast, these efforts are limited in scope and resources compared to the global reach
of powers like the United States.
Without a centralised military force, the EUʼs security influence remains dependent on the
willingness and capacity of individual member states and these member states continue to
prioritise partnership with the U.S. for defence.

A final difference is that internal disagreements and divisions have hampered the EUʼs
military/security cooperation much more than they have its economic cooperation.
Economic Power
Economic policy within the EU benefits from Qualified Majority Voting QMV, which ensures
that decisions can be made without unanimity, reducing the risk of gridlock and allowing for
swift collective action. This framework has enabled the EU to act cohesively on trade and
economic policies, minimising internal divisions.
For example, in 2016, the EU imposed anti-dumping tariffs on Chinese steel to protect its
internal market, despite differing levels of concern among member states. QMV allowed the
EU to overcome dissent and take collective action to protect its industries.
Military/Security Power
By contrast, military and security policy often face significant divisions due to the unanimity
required under the Common Foreign and Security Policy CFSP and the fact that member
states often have different foreign policy views and priorities.
This prevents the EU from presenting a unified stance on important global issues and limits
its global influence on military/security matters in comparison to other international actors.
For instance, during the Iraq War, EU member states were deeply divided, with the UK
supporting the US-led invasion, while France and Germany opposed it, leading to a
fractured EU stance.
