Paragraph One
Point Of Difference
A central division between the developed and developing world on the responsibility to tackle
climate change is whether the responsibility for reversing climate change should be based on
historical or current CO₂ emissions.
Developing Countries
Developing countries argue that the responsibility for tackling climate change should lie
primarily with developed nations, given their historical emissions during industrialisation.
They point in particular to the fact that the United States and European Union countries
together accounted for approximately 50% of global cumulative CO₂ emissions between
1850 and 2019, while Africa contributed less than 4%.

By contrast, developed nations increasingly argue that addressing climate change requires

Paragraph Two
Point Of Difference
Another important division between the views of the developed and developing world in relation
to climate change is whether they believe that developing states should be allowed to continue
to grow economically in a way that harms the planet.
Developing Countries
Developing nations emphasise the need for economic growth to lift their populations out of
poverty, arguing that they have the right to grow economically in the same way that developed
states were able to, even if this means higher carbon emissions initially.
This is an important part of their argument that developed states should currently bear that
majority of the economic burden for tackling climate change.
For example, India has argued that its use of coal and other fossil fuels is necessary to
sustain economic growth and lift millions out of poverty, particularly as its per capita
emissions are only around 1.9 metric tons compared to the United Statesʼ 14.2 metric tons.
Its Nationally Determined Contributions NDCs) under the Paris Agreement allows its
emissions to peak by 2030 rather than immediately.
Developed Countries
Developed states, influenced by the fact they donʼt want to pay to tackle climate change alone,
stress the urgency of global emissions reductions and argue that even developing nations must
take immediate and significant steps to prevent global warming.
They advocate for developing states to transition to renewable energy and adopt
sustainable development practices, often tying climate finance to such commitments.
This tension was evident during the Paris Agreement negotiations, where developed nations
pushed for ambitious commitments from all countries while developing nations sought more
lenient targets.

Point Of Difference

Developing Countries
Developing countries argue that financial and technological support from developed countries
is essential to enable their transition to clean energy without sacrificing economic growth.
Mechanisms like the Green Climate Fund GCF were established to mobilise $100 billion
annually to assist developing states, but the failure to meet this target until 2022 deepened
tensions, especially as many argue far more is needed.
For example, Mia Mottley, the Prime Minister of Barbados, called for significantly greater
financial commitments of at least $350 billion annually, arguing that the current funding
pledges fall far short of what is needed to address the scale of the climate crisis.
Developed Countries
Developed nations, on the other hand, have often been reluctant to increase financial
contributions, citing domestic political pressures and the questioned influence of such funding.
They argue that while they should provide financial support, developing nations must
demonstrate ambitious climate commitments in return.
The establishment of the Loss and Damage Fund at COP27 was a significant step forward,
but questions remain over how it will be financed, with developed nations hesitant to accept
further financial burdens or liabilities for historical emissions.
