Evaluate The View That Globalisation Has Undermined National Sovereignty.

Introduction
Key Definitions
National sovereignty refers to a stateʼs absolute authority over its territory and the people within
it, free from external interference. This includes internal sovereignty, which is a stateʼs authority
to make decisions within its own borders, and external sovereignty, which is the recognition of
their authority from other states and international bodies.
Globalisation today is understood as the complex web of interconnectedness that defines
global politics across multiple dimensions, including the economic, political and cultural
spheres. What happens in one nation state influences and is influenced by decisions and
factors across the world.

Paragraph One ➡ How Globalisation Has Made States
Interdependent
For: Global Issues Force Cooperation and Undermine Statesʼ Ability to Act
Unilaterally
One key argument that globalisation has undermined national sovereignty is that it has removed
statesʼ ability to control issue and tackle challenges without cooperating with other states.
This is in line with the perspective of hyperglobalisers, who argue that globalisation is a
transformative force reshaping the world into a highly interconnected global system, where
nation-states are less relevant and global cooperation is central to tackling political and
economic challenges.
As globalisation has made states increasingly interlinked, a large amount of the challenges that
face states today transcend national borders and require states to cooperate through
international institutions to tackle them, rather than being able to act unilaterally.
Climate change has led to agreements like the Paris Agreement, where countries seek to
collectively tackle the problem by reducing emissions.
Similarly, the COVID19 pandemic highlighted the need for global collaboration in vaccine
distribution, travel restrictions, and economic recovery plans.
Due to global travel and the interconnected nature of economies, a countryʼs individual
efforts at tackling the pandemic could be undermined by other countries.
Liberals view this interconnectedness as a positive thing that leads to greater cooperation
and mutual prosperity. As states must cooperate to be successful, the likelihood of conflict
is decreased.
Economic globalisation intensifies this need for political cooperation, as interconnected
economies rely on shared financial regulations to prevent and respond to crises like the 2008
financial crisis, which required global coordination through institutions such as the International
Monetary Fund IMF and G20.
This interconnectedness pressures states to surrender aspects of sovereignty, shifting
decision-making power to international bodies like the World Health Organisation WHO or the
UN, which enforce collective decisions that can override unilateral national action.
As a result, national sovereignty has become increasingly subordinated to global
governance frameworks.

************************************************************************************************

Against: States Still Have Internal Sovereignty
On the other hand, it can be argued that globalisation hasnʼt undermined national sovereignty,
as states still retain internal sovereignty and the rise of populism across the world has led to a
reassertion of this sovereignty.
There is no doubt that states have to take into account other actors when making decisions,
but they still maintain the ability to control borders, regulate immigration, and protect
national interests in global negotiations.
This perspective is argued by globalisation sceptics, who argue that the narrative of the
nation stateʼs decline is overstated and that countries continue to prioritise their sovereignty
and self-interest over global cooperation.
Australia provides a clear example of how countries can maintain sovereignty through strict
border and immigration policies. The countryʼs ‘Operation Sovereign Bordersʼ aims to prevent
unauthorised illegal immigration by using the Australian Defence Force ADF and border
protection agencies to intercept boats carrying asylum seekers and process asylum claims in
offshore facilities.
This demonstrates that globalisation has not eroded a stateʼs capacity to regulate migration
and protect its territorial integrity.
Additionally, in international negotiations, states can assert their national interests and achieve
favourable terms. Realists argue that IGOs have limited real power to coerce states.
During the renegotiation of NAFTA into the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement
USMCA in 2018, Canada successfully protected its dairy industry by negotiating limits on
U.S. dairy imports, asserting national interest in preserving the domestic market for its dairy
farmers.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, there is no doubt that globalisation has made states more interconnected and therefore
reduced statesʼ ability to act unilaterally without being influenced by other actors. Globalisation
therefore has undermined national sovereignty to some extent.
It hasnʼt completely removed it as hyperglobalisers argue though, as powerful states in
particular retain significant internal sovereignty and the power to assert their interests.

Paragraph Two ➡ The Impact Of Economic Globalisation
For: Economic Globalisation Has Removed State Control Over National
Economies
Perhaps the most important way in which it can be argued globalisation has undermined
national sovereignty is in relation to economics. The global economy is now so interconnected
and interdependent that national governments have limited real power to influence their
economies, with global actors and trends arguably having far more impact.
The rise of MNCs, which operate across multiple borders, allow private entities such as Apple
and Amazon to hold substantial economic power, often exceeding that of the states.
As they provide significant jobs and taxes to countries yet can threaten to move production
elsewhere, MNCs can influence domestic policy in their interests, therefore reducing
government control over their stateʼs economy.
An example of MNCs influencing domestic policy can be seen in the influence of
multinational car companies on UK policy. In 2025, the UK Government made a number of
changes that benefitted multinational car companies including Volswagen and Stellantis, as
they were concerned that the MNCs may stop manufacturing in the UK otherwise and lose
thousands of British jobs as a result.
In particular, the government relaxed the Zero Emission Vehicle Mandate, so that car
manufacturers were no longer forced to ensure that a high percentage of the vehicles
they made in the UK were electric.
Additionally, a stateʼs economic performance is now very dependent on global markets outside
of their control, as national economies are increasingly vulnerable to global fluctuations in
trade, finance, and demand.
For instance, the 2008 financial crisis, originating in the U.S., had far-reaching
consequences for economies worldwide, demonstrating how interconnected markets can
undermine a stateʼs internal sovereignty.
This reliance on global markets also weakens the stateʼs ability to set independent
economic policies, as countries must now cater to international investors and foreign
market forces.
Cultural and political globalisation amplify these challenges to state sovereignty by spreading
consumerism and free market capitalism, which further erode traditional economic structures
and undermine national control over the economy, making states struggle to maintain both
political and economic control.
For example, the IMF and World Bank have frequently made vital economic aid contingent
on countries (often in the global south) moving towards free-market economies.
In this sense, the national sovereignty of weaker states in the Global South has been
undermined more than powerful states. These states were reliant on loans from the World
Bank and IMF and as a result were forced to implement reforms that they knew would have
a detrimental effect.

************************************************************************************************

Against: Economic Globalisation Hasnʼt Undermined Statesʼ Sovereignty
While economic globalisation has increased economic interdependence, it can be argued that
states retain a significant amount of control over their national economies, using policy tools
like tariffs, regulations, and subsidies to protect their national interests.
China, for instance, participates in global markets but maintains firm control over critical
economic sectors such as technology and state-owned enterprises. This control allows
China to shape its economic development according to national priorities while benefiting
from international trade.
The United States has also demonstrated that it can impose tariffs and protectionist
measures, as seen during the USChina trade war, where the U.S. sought to shield its
manufacturing sector from competition.
Many countries including Japan and India provide agricultural subsidies to support
domestic food production and protect key national interests, ensuring sovereignty over key
sectors even in a globalised economy.
These examples show that while states engage in global trade, they retain the power to regulate
and protect their economies.
Crucially, however, this power to protect a countryʼs economy is arguably only held by the
more powerful states, as the majority of states lack the economic power or influence to
impose their will, particularly if this would conflict with the interests of powerful states.
This reflects transformationalistsʼ views that globalisation is a transformative and highly
complex force that affects states unevenly. While some states see their sovereignty
reduced, others can adapt and thrive in the interconnected world.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, economic globalisation has undermined the national sovereignty of the vast majority of
nation states, which have limited power over their economies due to the influence of global
trends, the power of multinational corporations MNCs) and the coercive use of economic
assistance by the IMF and World Bank.
Some powerful states are able to use tariffs and subsidies to protect their national interests,
however even these countries are heavily influenced by global markets.

Paragraph Three ➡ The Impact Of Political Globalisation
For: Political Globalisation Constrains How States Can Act
A final argument is that political globalisation has created international frameworks that
undermine national sovereignty, as IGOs like the UN, ICC and EU set binding rules on issues
such as trade, human rights, and conflict resolution. If states break these rules, their
sovereignty can be justifiably infringed upon in the name of upholding the rules.
This is especially true for weaker states, which canʼt ignore international law and rulings in
the same way that powerful states can. As a result, they are the subject of the majority of
international rulings.
In 2015, Greece was sanctioned by the EU for breaching the Common Agricultural Policy
CAP by providing unlawful subsidies to its farmers. The EU required Greece to repay
€425 million in misused funds, demonstrating how supranational institutions can
infringe upon a state's internal sovereignty.
In 2011, the UN sanctioned a military intervention in Libya under UN Security Council
Resolution 1973 to protect civilians from human rights abuses by the Gaddafi regime during
the Libyan Civil War. The resolution authorised a no-fly zone and “all necessary measuresˮ
to protect civilians, leading to an international military campaign primarily conducted by
NATO forces.
This intervention marked a case where the UN sanctioned the use of force, overriding
Libyaʼs sovereignty to uphold international humanitarian norms and prevent further
atrocities against civilians.
Liberals would strongly argue that this shows that undermining national sovereignty is
highly important to protecting human rights and preventing conflict.
International law therefore challenges state sovereignty by requiring compliance with global
norms, limiting the freedom of states to act independently in pursuit of their national interests.

************************************************************************************************

Against: States Choose To Participate in Political Globalisation and Can Opt Out
On the other hand, it can be argued that political globalisation doesnʼt undermine national
sovereignty, as states can decide to opt out of IGOs. States voluntarily choose to participate in
political globalisation by joining international organisations and institutions, but they retain the
ability to leave or not join.
The UKʼs exit from the European Union through Brexit shows that countries can withdraw
from supranational institutions to reclaim control over domestic policies such as trade,
immigration, and legal frameworks.
Similarly, not all states have ratified the International Criminal Court ICC treaty, with major
powers like the United States, Russia, and China opting not to join, reflecting their
unwillingness to cede sovereignty in matters of international justice.
Powerful states have the power to ignore rulings or recommendations from international bodies
when it is convenient for them. They know they wonʼt be punished/harmed in the same way
weaker states would.
In 2016, China rejected a ruling by the Permanent Court of Arbitration that invalidated its
territorial claims in the South China Sea, continuing to assert control over the region despite
the international tribunalʼs decision, demonstrating its willingness to defy global rulings in
favour of national interests.
This reflects a realist perspective that international organisations hold limited real power
over sovereign nations. This ability to opt in or out of global governance frameworks
underscores the idea that states remain the ultimate decision-makers in the global system,
whilst IGOs are simply tools for powerful countries to advance their own interests rather
than fostering genuine global cooperation.
Further, powerful states can use their economic power to shape global rules and bend
globalisation to their advantage, maintaining control over domestic policy and forcing other
countries to follow global rules that primarily benefit wealthier countries.
For example, the US exerted influence in the WTO through its successful push for
intellectual property protections under the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of
Intellectual Property Rights TRIPS.
The U.S. lobbied for stronger global intellectual property protections, particularly
benefiting American pharmaceutical and technology companies.
This allowed the U.S. to secure favourable rules that protect patents and copyrights,
ensuring that its industries maintain a competitive advantage in global markets,
especially in sectors like pharmaceuticals, where patent protections are crucial for
profitability.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the impact of political globalisation on nation statesʼ sovereignty is very different for
weaker states than it is for powerful states.
The national sovereignty of weaker states is undermined by international institutions and law, as
they lack the influence to ignore international rulings and withstand retaliation if they do so.
Further, these states have limited power in IGOs and in shaping international law itself.
By contrast, powerful states such as the US and China largely retain national sovereignty
despite political globalisation. Not only do they shape global rules and norms due to their
economic might, they also often ignore international rulings that donʼt serve their interests,
knowing they are untouchable due to their power in IGOs and influence over other countriesʼ
economies.

************************************************************************************************
************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, globalisation has certainly reduced the national sovereignty of all countries,
which are now far more interconnected economically and politically. It has only undermined the
national sovereignty of weaker states, though, as a few powerful states including the US and
China have been able to retain significant influence due to their economic might.
In this sense, a transformationalist perspective of globalisation is far more useful than the
binary arguments of hyperglobalisers or globalisation sceptics. This ultimately recognises that
globalisation has had a highly uneven impact. Some states have been able to take advantage of
it and assert control, whilst other states havenʼt.
The national sovereignty of weaker states is undermined as they must abide by international
rulings, they play little part in shaping international rules and their economies are vulnerable
to global market fluctuations.
By contrast, powerful states have been able to retain far more of their sovereignty as they
can ignore international rulings that donʼt serve their interests and can use their economic
might to protect their interests through measures such as tariffs and subsidies.
