regional organisations build trust among member states, create economic interdependence that
discourages conflict and create mechanisms for mediation and collective security that address
regional tensions before they escalate.

It has authorised peacekeeping missions in conflict zones like Somalia and the Sahel and

Similarly, the European Union EU has been highly effective in maintaining peace among its
members, which was one of its founding aims after decades of bloody war on the continent.
The EUʼs strategy of economic integration, coupled with shared political institutions such as
the European Parliament and the European Court of Justice, has created strong incentives
for peaceful cooperation, helping to prevent armed conflict among member states for over
seventy years.
In Southeast Asia, ASEAN provides a platform for dialogue and confidence-building, particularly

Philippines and Vietnam.
Although ASEAN relies on consensus-based diplomacy rather than binding decisions, its
role as a forum for discussion has helped de-escalate tensions and promote regional
stability.
This reflects the liberal perspective that regional organisations create stable frameworks
that encourage states to cooperate and increase their collective influence, rather than
selfishly pursue their own interests which can threaten conflict.
Against: Regional Organisations Havenʼt Been Effective In Reducing Conflict
On the other hand it can be argued that regional organisations are in fact ineffective in reducing
conflict, as some lack the power or ability to enforce decisions when member states are divided
or in conflict.
The Arab League, for instance, has faced significant challenges in resolving conflicts among its
members, due to competing national interests and divergent foreign policy goals. As a
intergovernmental organisation, it relies on the consent of member states and therefore has few
coercive powers.
In the case of the Syrian Civil War, the Arab League initially responded by suspending
Syriaʼs membership and imposing economic sanctions, signalling disapproval of the Assad
governmentʼs actions.
Yet, despite these measures, the League was unable to facilitate a resolution or provide a

While regionalism has the potential to mitigate conflicts, its effectiveness heavily depends on

Judgement
Though regional organisations wonʼt always be able to prevent conflict, on the whole they have
been very effective in doing so. Economic integration makes it much more costly for member

Paragraph Two ➡ Reducing Poverty
For: Regional Organisations Have Been Effective In Reducing Poverty
There is a very strong argument to suggest that regional organisations are highly effective in
reducing poverty by fostering economic cooperation, facilitating trade, and providing funding
and support for development initiatives that target less-developed member states.
In Southeast Asia, ASEAN has been very successful in driving economic growth for its
members. The ASEAN Free Trade Area, established in 1992, reduced tariffs on intra-ASEAN
trade to near zero and significantly increased trade between ASEAN countries as a result, with
intra-ASEAN trade reaching over $800 billion in 2022.
Further, it has also been able to secure significant trade agreements with other countries to
boost ASEANʼs exports. For example, The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership
RCEP was signed with China, Japan and other key trading partners in 2020 and is
projected to increase trade for countries in the agreement by 10.4% annually.
The economic growth delivered by regionalism has been central in reducing poverty in
ASEAN member states. For example, the poverty rate in Cambodia dropped from 47.8% in
2007 to 16.6% in 2021.
The European Union EU has also significantly reduced poverty within its member states
through its Structural and Cohesion Funds, which provide targeted financial support to less-
developed regions in countries like Romania, Bulgaria, and Greece.
Between 2014 and 2020, the EU allocated €367 billion to reduce regional disparities,
resulting in infrastructure improvements, better educational opportunities, and economic
growth, which have contributed to lower poverty rates in many areas.
This shows how regional organisations can actively work towards reducing economic
disparities between member states.
The African Union AU has also made poverty reduction a core priority, through initiatives like
the New Partnership for Africaʼs Development NEPAD, which promotes sustainable economic
growth, improves infrastructure, and boosts human development across the continent.
For example, NEPADʼs support for Rwandaʼs agricultural sector through CAADP initiatives
led to significant improvements, contributing to a 5% annual growth in agriculturalEvaluate The View That Regional Organisations Have Been Effective In Reducing Conflict, Poverty and Promoting Human Rights. 30 Politics Explained

According to the World Bank, the African Continental Free Trade Area AfCFTA also has the

market stimulates economic activity, creates jobs, and provides better access to goods and

Against: Regional Organisations Havenʼt Been Effective In Reducing Poverty
On the other hand it can be argued that regional organisations are ineffective in reducing
poverty, as economic integration can lead to uneven development and benefits wealthier states
more than poorer ones.
The EU has faced criticism for creating economic disparities within the bloc because the
European Central Bank ECB sets monetary policy that tends to favour the economic conditions
of wealthier member states, such as Germany and France, which have stronger economies and
lower debt levels and therefore prefer low interest rates.
This approach has led to challenges for poorer and heavily indebted states like Greece and
Italy, where lower interest rates and stringent fiscal policies have hampered the economic
growth they need.
Following the 2008 financial crisis, Greeceʼs poverty rate surged to 35% by 2015, driven by
austerity measures imposed by the EU, ECB and the IMF as conditions for bailout packages.
These austerity policies led to high unemployment, with youth unemployment reaching
nearly 50% in Greece, highlighting how EU-driven economic policies sometimes
exacerbate poverty rather than reduce it.
In the African Union AU, while initiatives like NEPAD and AfCFTA show promise, limited
infrastructure and implementation challenges have hindered their effectiveness, showing the
difficulties regional organisations face in reducing poverty.
For instance, only 17% of Africaʼs roads are paved, which restricts the AfCFTAʼs ability to
reach the poorest populations by limiting market access in rural areas.
ASEAN also faces challenges in ensuring that economic growth benefits all its member states
equally.
Although Singaporeʼs GDP per capita reached approximately $59,000 in 2022, countries
like Myanmar and Laos lag behind, with GDP per capita at roughly $1,400 and $2,500,
respectively.
Judgement
Overall, regional organisations have been highly effective in reducing poverty. By reducing
tariffs within organisations and negotiating effective trade terms with other countries, regional
organisations across the world have significantly boosted economic growth for their member
states and therefore reduced poverty, whilst also using targeted revenue to do so and reduce
disparities within economic blocs.

On the one hand, it can be argued that regional organisations are effective in promoting human
rights by establishing monitoring mechanisms and encouraging member states to uphold
certain minimum human rights protections.
As member states donʼt want to lose the economic benefits of being a member of a regional
organisation, this makes them less likely to undermine human rights, as this would risk them
being kicked out.
This demonstrates how regional organisations can put pressure on states to uphold norms,
even if they are intergovernmental organisations and arenʼt able to coerce states into action.
The African Union AU, for example, has adopted the African Charter on Human and Peoplesʼ
Rights, a legally binding document that sets out a framework for human rights across the
continent.
It has also established the African Court on Human and Peoplesʼ Rights, which allows
individuals and organisations to bring cases against member states, increasing
accountability for human rights abuses.
Similarly, the European Union EU has embedded human rights within its legal framework and
made them a fundamental component of EU membership criteria.
EU member states are required to comply with the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, which
covers rights ranging from freedom of expression to non-discrimination, and the European
Court of Justice has the authority to rule on cases where member states violate these
rights.
For example, Hungaryʼs 2018 ‘Stop Sorosʼ law, which criminalised aid to asylum seekers,
was found by the ECJ to breach the EU Charterʼs rights related to asylum and freedom of
association. This ruling required Hungary to amend or repeal the law, reinforcing the EUʼs
commitment to human rights and non-discrimination.
In Southeast Asia, ASEAN has also made strides towards promoting human rights through the
establishment of the ASEAN Intergovernmental Commission on Human Rights AICHR, which,
although limited in enforcement powers, provides a platform for discussing and encouraging
adherence to human rights principles among member states.
Against: Regional Organisations Havenʼt Been Effective In Promoting Human
Rights
On the other hand, it must be recognised that regional organisations arenʼt always effective in
promoting human rights. Regional organisations often face internal divisions and lack the
enforcement mechanisms necessary to hold member states accountable.
The Arab League, for example, has a mixed record on human rights, and its member states
have diverse political systems, political interests and human rights practices that make
consensus difficult.
While the League has made statements condemning human rights abuses in conflict zones,
such as Syria and Yemen, it lacks a binding human rights charter or enforcement body,
limiting its ability to take concrete action.

For example, despite concerns over human rights abuses in Ethiopia and Sudan, the AU
struggled to intervene decisively due to its principle of non-interference in the internal
affairs of member states and the influence of powerful nations within the organisation that
prioritised stability over confronting human rights violations.
ASEAN also faces challenges in promoting human rights effectively; its Intergovernmental
Commission on Human Rights operates on a non-binding, advisory basis, which restricts its
ability to hold member states accountable for abuses.
ASEANʼs response to the Rohingya genocide in Myanmar underscored its limitations, as the
Intergovernmental Commission on Human Rights could only issue non-binding
recommendations and calls for dialogue. This restricted ASEANʼs ability to hold Myanmar

These examples demonstrate that while regionalism can create frameworks for human rights,
its impact often depends on member statesʼ willingness to prioritise human rights over national
sovereignty and political alliances.
Judgement
Overall, regional organisations have a mixed record in promoting human rights. Because the
EUʼs court ECJ is a supranational institution, it has been successful in promoting human rights
as it has the power to reverse member state actions.
Other regional organisations are intergovernmental, though, and have been unsuccessful in
promoting human rights as they lack the power to enforce sanctions against states that abuse
human rights.
Overall Argument
In conclusion, regional organisations across the world have been largely effective in reducing
conflict, reducing poverty and promoting human rights.
By reducing trade barriers between states, they have not only been able to promote economic
growth and reduce poverty, they have also created significant economic interdependence
between member states that reduces the likelihood of conflict.
Further, regional organisations have established important mechanisms to deal with and
sanction human rights abuses and conflicts. Though the EUʼs supranational institutions are the
most effective at doing so, as they have real enforcement mechanisms, intergovernmental
regional organisations such as the AU have also been effective in putting pressure on state to
uphold norms and coordinating action to promote peace.
