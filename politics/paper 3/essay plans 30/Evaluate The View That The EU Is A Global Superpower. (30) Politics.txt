Against: The EU Isnʼt A Global Superpower
It can be argued that the EU isnʼt a global superpower, as its economic power it increasingly
challenged on the global stage as it is caught between two dominant economic powers: China
and the United States.
Unlike the US and China, which have centralised control over their economic policies, the EUʼs
economic power is constrained by internal divisions and a lack of unified strategic direction.

technology and semiconductors.
However the EU isnʼt able to because it faces restrictions on state aid and doesnʼt have
a unified industrial policy pooled between member states, which has reduced its ability
to compete on the global stage.
Germany in particular is struggling as a manufacturing powerhouse with an important
automotive sector that is being outcompeted by China when it comes to electric

The trade war between the US and China has also left the EU member states vulnerable to
disruptions, particularly those which are dependent on exports to the US or China, such as

In early 2025, despite being a major economic player, it wasnʼt exempt from the U.S. tariffs,
receiving a 10% tariff on all its exports to the U.S. (lowered from 20% a couple of weeks
earlier).
The EUʼs inability to protect these member statesʼ interests demonstrates how the EU isnʼt
an economic superpower and is struggling to have influence in a global economy
dominated by the USChina rivalry.
For: The EU Is A Global Superpower
On the other hand, it can be argued that the EU is a global economic superpower, as its
economic might allows it to negotiate effective trade deals with countries across the global and
to effectively compete with China and the United States.
The EU is the worldʼs second-largest economic bloc, with the EU representing around 15% of
global GDP and is the largest trading partner for over 80 countries. This gives it significant
leverage in the global economy.
Firstly, this economic mights enables the EU to negotiate on behalf of its 27 members and
secure favourable trade agreements that support economic growth across its member
states. With an internal market of over 450 million consumers, it has significant leverage.
For instance, the EUCanada Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement CETA
eliminated tariffs on 98% of goods traded between the two regions, benefiting EU
exporters and reducing costs for businesses.
Similarly, the EUSouth Korea Free Trade Agreement has increased EU exports to South
Korea by 76% since its implementation, benefiting member states with strong export
sectors, such as Germany in machinery and Ireland in pharmaceuticals.
Secondly, the EUʼs size enables it to set global standards in industries ranging from
environmental regulation to digital privacy, influencing production and regulatory practices
worldwide.
The EUʼs General Data Protection Regulation GDPR has had a particularly notable
impact, with countries like Japan and Brazil adopting similar privacy laws in response to

The EU hasnʼt only used its economic might to secure favourable trade deals, it has also
protected its member statesʼ economies against the threat of China and the United states,
therefore helping them to compete effectively.
For example, in October 2024 the EU protected its member statesʼ automotive industries by
imposing additional tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles (EVs), with rates ranging from 7.8%
to 35.3%, citing concerns over unfair subsidies provided to Chinese manufacturers.
Additionally, in the renewable energy sector, the EUʼs aggressive carbon reduction targets
and green technology investments have made it a global leader, with member states like
Germany and Denmark leading in wind and solar energy innovations.
These efforts help EU businesses compete with China in particular, with EU renewable
energy exports worth over €72 billion in 2020.
Judgement
Overall, the EU has the economic might to be a global superpower and saw significant success
when the global economy was dominated by free trade.
In the era of USChina competition, however, it is a great power not a superpower. This is due
to the fact that its internal structure and divisions prevent it from implementing a unified and
effective industrial strategy that is needed to compete with China and the United States and
assert itself in an era of bipolar competition where both of these states are aggresively pursuing
their interests.
Paragraph Two ➡ Geopolitical/Military Power
Against: The EU Isnʼt A Global Superpower
The strongest argument for the EU not being a superpower is that it is unable to compete
effectively with the United States and China in geopolitical/military terms, in particular due to its
lack of centralised military force and hard power capabilities.
Unlike the United States, which has the worldʼs most powerful military, or China, which is
expanding its military presence globally, the EU lacks the independent military strength to
project power beyond its borders.
This limitation is especially apparent in high-stakes geopolitical areas, like the South
China Sea, where the EUʼs diplomatic appeals are largely overshadowed by US and
Chinese military manoeuvres.
The EUʼs lack of a unified, centralised military force means that it is very dependent on
NATO and the United States in particular to have global influence and to respond to major
security crises. This arguably relegates it to a great power that is within the USʼ sphere of
influence rather than a superpower in its own right.
This was seen clearly in relation to Ukraine after Trumpʼs return to the Presidency in
2025. Trump pushed strongly for a ceasefire and swift peace negotiations, but these
negotiations were held between the U.S., Russia and Ukraine, with no EU leaders
invited.
The EU also found itself sidelined as it was unable to offer a credible military guarantee
to Ukraine when the U.S. refused to do so, due to its collective lack of military strength

A central reason for the EUʼs inability to project major influence geopolitically is its internal
divisions over foreign policy, which prevent it from taking a strong, unified stance and weaken
its geopolitical impact.
Member states have diverse interests and priorities in foreign policy, which can lead to stark
disagreements on how to approach global issues.
For example, during the US-led War on Terror, EU member states took opposing
stances: the UK and Poland joined the US-led coalition in Iraq, while Germany and
France strongly opposed the intervention.
More recently, this has undermined efforts to find an effect way to project European power
in the context of the USChina rivalry, with disagreement on whether to align more closely
with Washington or pursue a more balanced stance.
France and Germany have expressed concerns about becoming overly dependent on
the US, with French President Macron calling for “strategic autonomy.ˮ However,
Eastern European countries, particularly those bordering Russia, generally support
closer alignment with the US, fearing Russian aggression.
This indecision and the EUʼs lack of hard power have led to it being overlooked in key
strategic decisions and alliances, such as in Asia-Pacific security arrangements.
For: The EU Is A Global Superpower
On the other hand, it can be argued that the EU is a global superpower as it wields extensive
soft power and is increasing its coordination on security and military issues in order to increase
its power.
The EU wields extensive soft power, positioning itself as a global leader in promoting
democracy, human rights, and sustainable development.
The EU and its member states collectively account for over 50% of global development aid,
providing approximately €80 billion annually. This assistance is strategically allocated to
support economic development, poverty alleviation, and governance reforms, especially in
Africa, the Middle East, and the EUʼs eastern neighbourhood.
For example, through the European Development Fund EDF, the EU has provided
billions of euros to Sub-Saharan Africa to improve education, healthcare, and
infrastructure, often tying aid to commitments on human rights and good governance.
As a result of these efforts, the EU has a strong global reputation as the worldʼs champion of
liberal values such as democracy and human rights, particularly as the US looks likely to
once again step back from doing so under a second Trump presidency. This gives it
significant soft power across the world.
For example, after the 2013 Rana Plaza disaster, the EU pushed for labour reform by
threatening to remove Bangladeshʼs preferential trade access under the “Everything but
Armsˮ program if safety standards werenʼt improved.
This led to significant improvements in factory conditions and workplace safety through
the creation of the Accord on Fire and Building Safety, positively affecting millions of
workers.
Further, the EU is increasingly coordinating its foreign and security policies in order to increase
its global influence. This is especially driven by French President Macron, who has long arguedEvaluate The View That The EU Is A Global Superpower. 30 Politics Explained Essay Plan4
that the EU needs to “avoid being followersˮ and build an independent geopolitical strategy
separate from the US.
The EUʼs initial response to Russiaʼs 2022 invasion of Ukraine, for example, was very strong.
It imposed multiple rounds of sanctions targeting Russiaʼs financial system, energy exports,
and key industries, cutting off critical funding sources for Moscow.
This economic pressure combined with EU support for Ukraine in the form of military
aid and humanitarian assistance, has had a significant positive impact on Ukraineʼs
chances of winning the war and demonstrated the EUʼs ability to articulate a strong
foreign policy in defence of liberal values.
In 2017, the EU also launched the Permanent Structured Cooperation PESCO, which
facilitates deeper defence cooperation among 25 EU member states, fostering joint projects
such as new military technologies, cyber defence initiatives, and coordinated rapid
response units.
Judgement
Overall, the EU is clearly not a superpower in geopolitical/military terms. Though it does have
significant soft power, its lack of hard power and a centralised military force limits its ability to
project power globally and ultimately makes it dependent on the United States. It is a great
power within the United Statesʼ orbit, rather than being a global superpower in its own right.
Paragraph Three ➡ Structural Power
For: The EU Is A Global Superpower
Finally, it can be argued that the EU is a global superpower due to the significant structural
power it has in global trade and in international governmental organisations IGOs).
The EUʼs structural power in trade and regulation makes it one of the most influential players in
the global economy, where it often acts as a “regulatory superpower.ˮ As the worldʼs largest
single market, the EU leverages its economic size to set global standards in areas such as
environmental protection, consumer safety, and data privacy.
This phenomenon, often referred to as the “Brussels Effect,ˮ occurs when multinational
companies and even other countries adapt their practices to comply with EU regulations to
access its market of over 450 million consumers.
A prime example is the EUʼs General Data Protection Regulation GDPR, which has set
an international standard for data privacy. Countries including Japan, Brazil, and South
Korea have adopted similar data privacy laws in response, illustrating the EUʼs far-
reaching regulatory influence.
Additionally, the EU plays a central role in the World Trade Organization WTO, where its
collective weight allows it to negotiate and advocate for fair trade practices and high
standards.
In the WTO, the EU led efforts to ban hormone-treated beef, successfully advocating for
stricter food safety standards that influenced global agricultural practices.
The EU can also be argued to be a superpower, as its structural power is supported by its active
participation in major IGOs, such as the G7, G20, International Monetary Fund IMF, and World
Health Organisation WHO.Evaluate The View That The EU Is A Global Superpower. 30 Politics Explained Essay Plan5
Within these institutions, the EU uses its economic clout to influence policies on issues
ranging from financial stability to global health and climate action.
For example, the EU played a critical role in shaping the Paris Climate Accord by
pushing for binding commitments to limit global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius, setting
an ambitious precedent for other countries and upholding its importance even when the
US pulled out under Donald Trump.
As a prominent G7 and G20 member, the EU represents its collective economic interests,
working alongside other global powers to shape responses to crises such as the COVID19
pandemic and the 2008 financial crisis.
Against: The EU Isnʼt A Global Superpower
On the other hand, it can be argued that the EU isnʼt a global superpower, as its internal
structure undermines its ability project power across the globe, particularly in the context of
growing divisions and tensions since the 2010s.
Crucially, the EUʼs structural power is limited by its dependence on member states for both
decision-making and enforcement, particularly in areas where national sovereignty and identity
play significant roles.
Since the 2008 financial crisis, this has significantly limited the EUʼs ability to act
cohesively, as there have been major divisions within the EU over key issues such as how to
respond to the rise of China and the migration crisis.
Since the early 2010s, populist and eurosceptic parties have gained traction in various
EU countries, driven by concerns over economic inequality, immigration, and a
perceived loss of national sovereignty. Some have even come to power, such as Fidesz
in Hungary and the Party for Freedom in the Netherlands.
This has led to a significant problem for a regional organisation that is committed to
liberal values, with divisions over a range of policy areas undermining its ability to
act effectively.
During the 2015 migration crisis, Hungary and Poland refused to implement the
EUʼs migrant relocation plan, arguing that national sovereignty took precedence
over shared EU policies, leading to an inconsistent approach to asylum seekers
across the Union.
In dealing with China, Hungary joined the Belt and Road Initiative in 2015,
supporting closer ties with China, which has led to internal EU divisions as other
member states advocate for a more cautious approach, prioritising concerns
over human rights and strategic autonomy from Chinese influence.
Crucially, this rise of populism also reduced the EUʼs influence as it led to the UKʼs
withdrawal in June 2016. This not only reduced the EUʼs economic influence as the
UK is the worldʼs 6th largest economy, it also had a significant impact on the EUʼs
geopolitical influence.
Further, even when the EU can agree on policies, its implementation relies heavily on
member states, leading to inconsistencies in enforcement, especially in areas like

For example, Italy has repeatedly exceeded the EUʼs 3% GDP deficit limit, including in
2019, when the Italian governmentʼs budget plan projected a deficit of 2.4% but ended
up exceeding 3%, leading to disputes with the European Commission over Italyʼs
commitment to fiscal discipline.
Judgement
Overall, the EU does have important structural power within global trade and IGOs and gives
member states far more influence than they would have on their own, however this does not
make it a superpower.
Within these IGOs, it isnʼt able to exert as much influence as the US or China and central to this
is its internal structural challenges and divisions which prevent it from acting cohesively.
Overall Argument
In conclusion, it is clear that the European Union is not a global superpower and is not able to
effectively compete with China and the United States despite being the worldʼs second largest
economic bloc. This is for two main reasons.
Firstly, its internal structure and divisions between member states prevent it from agreeing on
and implementing an effective strategy to increase and project its power globally. This has
meant it has failed to develop an industrial strategy to compete with China and the United
States economically.
Secondly, it may have significant soft power but it lacks hard power and a centralised military
force, which makes it dependent on the United States for its security.
Instead of being a superpower, the EU remains a great power that is largely in the USʼ orbit for
these security reasons.
