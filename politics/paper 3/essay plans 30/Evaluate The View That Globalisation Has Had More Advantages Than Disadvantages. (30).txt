Evaluate The View That Globalisation Has Had More Advantages Than Disadvantages. (30) 

Introduction
Key Definitions
Globalisation today is understood as the complex web of interconnectedness that defines global
politics across multiple dimensions, including the economic, political and cultural spheres. What
happens in one nation state influences and is influenced by decisions and factors across the world.

Paragraph One ➡ Economic Globalisation
For: Globalisation Has Had More Advantages Than Disadvantages
One of the key arguments that globalisation has more advantages than disadvantages is that it
has driven the expansion of international trade, allowing countries to access larger markets and
thus increase economic output and job creation.
Between 2000 and 2024, the global economy has nearly tripled in size, with nominal global
GDP increasing from about $33 trillion in 2000 to approximately $104 trillion by 2023,
according to data from the World Bank and IMF.
This particularly benefited emerging economies like China, which was able to use the
competitive advantage of its cheap labour costs to become a global hub of manufacturing
and drive its rapid economic growth.
In less than 40 years, China experienced rapid industrialisation and saw over 800 million
people lifted out of poverty as a result.
This argument is supported by modernisation theory, which suggests that globalisation has
advanced the global economy and reduced poverty by spreading capitalism and free
market principles.
As nations integrate into the global economy, they adopt free market economic
practices such as deregulation, privatisation, and market liberalisation.
These reforms attract foreign direct investment FDI, promote entrepreneurial activity,
and drive technological innovation, which stimulates job creation and development. This
further enables developing countries to move away from reliance on agriculture and raw
materials to industrialised and service-based sectors.
The free flow of technology and knowledge facilitated by globalisation has also helped
developing nations access advanced technology, enabling them to leapfrog traditional stages
of industrialisation and improve productivity.
After India opened up its economy to global markets in the 1990s, it experienced rapid
growth averaging 67% annually, especially in sectors like information technology IT and
services. India became a global IT hub, with cities like Bangalore and Hyderabad emerging
as major tech centres.
This growth helped reduce extreme poverty from 45% in 1993 to around 6% by 2024,
according to World Bank data.
Globalisation has also allowed technology vital to tackling climate change, including solar
panels and electric cars, to spread rapidly across borders, helping countries reduce their
reliance on fossil fuels.
Beyond economic growth, globalisation has increased consumer access to a wider range of
goods and services, driving down prices and improving living standards worldwide.

************************************************************************************************

Against: Globalisation Hasnʼt Had More Advantages Than Disadvantages
One major disadvantages of globalisation is that it has deepened global inequality, with
wealthier nations and multinational corporations benefiting disproportionately from
globalisation, while poorer countries remain reliant on exporting raw materials and low-wage
labour.
The rise of global supply chains has led to a “race to the bottom,ˮ where countries compete
by offering cheap labor, leading to poor working conditions and limited worker protections,
particularly in industries like textiles and electronics.
As Bangladesh integrated into global supply chains, particularly in the garment industry,
millions of unskilled workers were employed as it became a hub for low-cost production
due to its cheap labour. However, this resulted in workers being trapped in cycles of
poverty, earning as little as $95 per month and often very dangerous factory conditions.
The Rana Plaza collapse in 2013 killing over 1,100 workers, illustrating the
consequences of the “race to the bottomˮ in labour standards.
Economic globalisation has also enabled the dominance of MNCs, which often exploit
weaker states by pressuring governments to enact favourable regulations by threatening to
leave the country and move jobs away.
This erosion of state sovereignty, combined with the concentration of wealth and power
in fewer hands, has worsened inequality and left many countries dependent on external
forces, limiting their ability to independently shape their economic futures.
In 2021, the richest 1% of the global population controlled 38% of global wealth,
while the poorest half held less than 2%.
For example, Apple has pressured countries like Ireland to maintain low corporate tax
rates, which align with the companyʼs interests but have sparked conflict with the EU
and other states prioritising tax fairness and revenue generation.
As Apple is important to Irelandʼs economy, it has significant sway.
Another disadvantage of economic globalisation is that it has left many countries vulnerable to
global market fluctuations, as seen during the 2008 financial crisis, where interconnected
financial markets collapsed, triggering economic downturns worldwide, disproportionately
affecting smaller economies such as Ukraine and Jamaica.
Further, the rapid industrialisation and expansion of global trade have increased carbon
emissions, deforestation, and resource exploitation, contributing to climate change and
environmental degradation.
A report released in late 2024 revealed that global deforestation continued to increase
across 2023, surging to 6.4 million hectares despite the fact that 140 countries pledged in
2021 to end deforestation by 2030. This was driven by global demand for beef, soy and
palm oil in particular.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, it is clear that the economic growth driven by globalisation has come at the expense of
economic instability and poor working conditions for many. This being said, it arguably still has
more advantages than disadvantages as it has brought over a billion people out of poverty and
allowed China and India in particular to hugely improve living standards for their citizens as a
result.

Paragraph Two ➡ Political Globalisation
For: Globalisation Has Had More Advantages Than Disadvantages
A key argument that political globalisation has more advantages than disadvantages is that
IGOs like the UN and WHO have facilitated collaboration to tackle global challenges and
established global norms that protect the environment and human rights.
Political globalisation has been important in fostering global cooperation to tackle climate
change. International institutions like the UN and treaties like the Paris Agreement have brought
nations together to commit to reducing carbon emissions.
The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, commits countries to keep the global temperature
rise this century well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels. 196 countries have signed the
agreement, representing nearly the entire world.
Political globalisation and cooperation through the UN in particular has also been crucial in
promoting human rights across the globe and tackling poverty.
Political globalisation fostered a global commitment to poverty reduction. The United
Nations, World Bank, and IMF launched initiatives aimed at reducing poverty and enhancing
development.
The Millennium Development Goals MDGs), established in 2000, targeted halving
global poverty by 2015, a goal that was met ahead of schedule.
The Sustainable Development Goals SDGs), launched in 2015, aim to eliminate extreme
poverty by 2030.
The SDGs take an approach to tackling poverty that addresses key structural issues
including inequality, climate change and access to education & healthcare.
Political globalisation has been instrumental in the development of international human
rights law, establishing frameworks that hold governments and individuals accountable for
human rights abuses.
The Universal Declaration of Human Rights UDHR, adopted by the United Nations in
1948, laid the foundation for modern international human rights standards.
Institutions including the European Court of Human Rights ECHR, and the International
Criminal Court ICC have also created mechanisms to protect human rights on a global
scale.
These international legal bodies have helped standardise human rights protections
across borders, increasing accountability for violations and providing recourse for
victims. The creation of these institutions shows how political globalisation has
fostered global human rights governance.
The ECHR, which covers 46 countries in Europe, ensures the protection of human
rights for over 700 million people. In Oliari v. Italy 2015, for example, the court
found that Italy violated the rights of same-sex couples by not providing legal
recognition for their relationships.

************************************************************************************************

Against: Globalisation Hasnʼt Had More Advantages Than Disadvantages
On the other hand, it can be argued that political globalisation has more disadvantages than
advantages as organisations like the UN and IMF wield significant influence and impose policies
that primarily benefit powerful nations, leaving weaker states with little influence and forced to
accept them.
This reflects the fact that wealthier nations have more power within IGOs and the
international system and can therefore shape them in ways that benefit them, whilst poorer
nations have less power and are forced to accept policies driven by Western ideology, in
particular neoliberalism. In reality, they have less sovereignty within the global politics.
In Sub-Saharan Africa, free market reforms imposed by the IMF and World Bank under
Structural Adjustment Programs SAPs) in the 1980s and 1990s deepened poverty and
dependence on external financial aid. Receiving much needed financial assistance was
made conditional on accepting these reforms, which included privatisation, deregulation,
and reduced public spending on essential services.
In Zambia, this resulted in mass layoffs, especially from state-owned enterprises,
driving poverty levels from 49% in 1989 to over 80% by the mid-1990s, whilst also
continuing the countryʼs dependence on the export of copper, rather than helping it to
develop.
Additionally, IGOs have been criticised for eroding state sovereignty, forcing nations to comply
with rules that do not necessarily align with their national interests.
For example, in 2015 Greece was sanctioned by the EU for breaching the Common
Agricultural Policy CAP by providing unlawful subsidies to its farmers, which it was trying
to support.
The EU required Greece to repay €425 million in misused funds, demonstrating how
supranational institutions can infringe upon a state's internal sovereignty and punish it
for pursuing things in the national interest.
This has also been the case with interventions in the name of protecting the human rights of
civilians, such as the UN sanctioned a military intervention in Libya under UN Security
Council Resolution 1973 to protect civilians from human rights abuses by the Gaddafi regime
during the Libyan Civil War.
Finally, realists would argue that the framework of IGOs and global governance driven by
political globalisation in fact have little power to influence states and uphold peace or human
rights, as shown in Israelʼs war in Gaza and Russiaʼs invasion of Ukraine, both of which have
violated international law in different ways.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, political globalisation has had more advantages than disadvantages. The IMF and
World Bank have at times caused more harm to Subsaharan African countries than they have
helped, but have since moved away from SAPs and IGOs provide a vital means for countries to
cooperate on issues that require collaboration to tackle, in particular upholding human rights
across the world and tackling climate change.

Paragraph Three ➡ Cultural Globalisation
For: Globalisation Has Had More Advantages Than Disadvantages
An important argument that cultural globalisation has had more advantages than disadvantages
is that it has promoted cultural diversity, where different cuisines, fashion, art, and music have
spread globally. This has allowed people to experience and appreciate cultures beyond their
own, breaking down cultural barriers.
For example, K-pop from South Korea has gained immense global popularity, connecting
different cultures through entertainment, whilst Hollywood and Bollywood stars are known
across the world.
Furthermore, the widespread use of social media platforms like Instagram and YouTube has
accelerated the spread of social and political movements, including those which advocate for
social justice and environmental awareness. This allows global collaboration on pressuring
governments to address the issues that matter to people, especially young people.
Fridays for Future, started by Greta Thunberg, which united young people worldwide to
address climate change and pressured governments to put climate change to the front of
the political agenda.
Similarly, the #MeToo movement gained traction internationally and sparked conversations
about gender equality and sexual harassment across cultures.
It can also be argued that the spread of English as a global language, which has been driven by
globalisation, has improved communication and collaboration across nations, aiding education
and business.
Globalisation has enriched education by enabling cross-cultural exchanges through
programs like Erasmus, allowing students to study abroad and experience diverse cultures
firsthand.
Finally, cultural globalisation has also been instrumental in spreading values like democracy,
human rights, and environmental sustainability, seen through initiatives like the United Nationsʼ
Sustainable Development Goals SDGs).

************************************************************************************************

Against: Globalisation Hasnʼt Had More Advantages Than Disadvantages
On the other hand it can be argued that cultural globalisation has more disadvantages than
advantages because in reality globalisation erodes local cultures and replaces them with a
homogenised, capitalist culture. As MNCs and media conglomerates dominate global markets,
local traditions, languages, and customs are often overshadowed by the more dominant
American/Western culture.
The spread of fast food chains like McDonaldʼs and Hollywood movies has led to a dilution
of traditional culinary practices and local entertainment industries.
Further, as global media conglomerates have gained influence, local artists and cultural
creators have lost some control over how their culture is represented. Cultural narratives
and traditions are increasingly shaped by global media corporations rather than by local
communities themselves, resulting in misrepresentation and cultural dilution.
For example, Disney films Pocahontas and Moana have been criticised for
misrepresenting indigenous cultures, commercialising their traditions for global
audiences while oversimplifying their histories and beliefs.
Similarly, African cultural symbols and art are often commodified and sold in global
markets by non-African companies, stripping these works of their deeper cultural
significance and exploiting them for profit.
It can further be argued that cultural globalisation is essentially a form of Americanisation, as
American culture—through music, movies, and consumer goods—has disproportionately
shaped global cultural norms.
This dominance of Western, particularly American, values contributes to the marginalisation
of indigenous cultures and to the loss of cultural diversity.
Finally, cultural globalisation has contributed to the decline of less commonly spoken
languages, as global languages like English dominate international business, media, and
education. This leads to the gradual extinction of indigenous and minority languages,
weakening cultural heritage.
Despite revitalisation efforts, the Maori language has struggled due to the dominance of
English in media, education, and public life, leading to a generational gap in fluent speakers
in New Zealand.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
On the whole, cultural globalisation has had more disadvantages than advantages. Rather than
breaking down cultural barriers, it has in reality promoted an Americanised monoculture that
has eroded and coopted local cultures.

Overall Argument
In conclusion, globalisation has had both major advantages and major disadvantages, but on
balance there are marginally more advantages.
The benefits of political and economic globalisation have come at the expense of workersʼ
conditions and the imposition of some flawed reforms. However, the massive impact of
globalisation on poverty reduction and giving countries a framework to tackle its most pressing
challenges shouldnʼt be understated.
Over a billion people have been brought out of poverty by the economic growth driven by
globalisation and the efforts of IGOs, whilst IGOs have been and will be vital in tackling issues
such as climate change and human rights, which canʼt be effectively tackled without
international cooperation.
