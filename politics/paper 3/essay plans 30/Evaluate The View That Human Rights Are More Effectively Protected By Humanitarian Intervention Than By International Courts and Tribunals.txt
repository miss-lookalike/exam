Paragraph One ➡ The Ability Of Humanitarian Interventions To
Prevent Immediate Human Rights Violations
For: Human Rights Are More Effectively Protected By Humanitarian
Interventions Than International Courts

In situations where human rights violations are imminent, interventions are the only way to
protect vulnerable populations—an approach that courts cannot achieve as they are unable
to enforce binding decisions.
The end of the Cold War, marked by unipolarity and the U.S. championing liberal democracy,
created a global environment in which humanitarian interventions became frequent, highlighting
the international communityʼs commitment to immediate human rights protection.
A notable example is the NATO-led intervention in Kosovo in 1999, launched specifically to
prevent mass violence against ethnic Albanians by Serbian forces. Despite bypassing a
Security Council veto, NATOʼs intervention successfully halted large-scale violence and
forced Yugoslav forces to withdraw, saving civilian lives that would likely have been lost
without direct intervention and successfully establishing a de facto independent Kosovo
under international supervision.
Similarly, the successful INTERFET intervention in East Timor 1999 demonstrated how
rapid, well-coordinated intervention can uphold human rights. Backed by UN authorisation,
Australian-led forces restored order, facilitated East Timorʼs independence, and established
a foundation for lasting peace. This stopped violent action and human rights abuses by pro-
Indonesian militias that was facilitated by the Indonesian military.
Beyond providing immediate protection, humanitarian interventions also protect human rights as
they serve as a powerful deterrent to potential perpetrators, signalling that sovereignty is
conditional upon a stateʼs willingness to protect its population.
Under the Responsibility to Protect R2P doctrine, which was formally endorsed by the UN
in 20025, if states fail in this duty, the international community has a collective responsibility
to act.
NATOʼs 2011 intervention in Libya, authorised by the UNSC, prevented a massacre of
civilians in Benghazi and contributed to the overthrow of Gaddafiʼs regime. This reinforced
R2Pʼs capacity to intervene where necessary.
Unlike international courts, which often struggle to enforce rulings, interventions like these
can swiftly circumvent state consent to directly protect civilians and deliver immediate
security and stability.

Against: Human Rights Are More Effectively Protected By International Courts
Than Humanitarian Interventions
While humanitarian interventions can protect human rights in theory, they are becoming
increasingly rare and it can therefore be argued they are limited in their ability to protect human
rights in the 21st century. This decline is due to the growing influence of China and Russia, both
strong opponents of humanitarian interventions that override state sovereignty, and the shift
toward isolationism in the U.S.
Over the past 15 years, the rise in power of China and Russia has significantly impacted the
global willingness and capacity to engage in humanitarian interventions.
Both countries have been strong supporters of the principle of state sovereignty and have
opposed any international actions that could be perceived as violating this norm, even to
protect human rights under the R2P doctrine.
They have used their UNSC vetoes to block resolutions that could authorise humanitarian
action, especially when such interventions might challenge their own strategic interests or

those of their allies.
For example, Russia has repeatedly vetoed resolutions aimed at addressing the crisis in
Syria, shielding its ally Bashar al-Assad from international military responses despite
evidence of widespread human rights abuses, including the use of chemical weapons.
This assertive use of veto power has created an environment where major humanitarian
interventions are increasingly difficult, if not impossible, to pursue.
The decline in humanitarian interventions has also been influenced by the United Statesʼ
rejection of its previous role as the global police force and its shift instead towards isolationism,
which began in the late 2000s and has continued into the 2010s and 2020s.
The costly and prolonged military campaigns in Iraq and Afghanistan shifted U.S. policy
away from foreign intervention and toward domestic priorities, a trend that began with
Obamaʼs presidency and intensified under Trumpʼs “America Firstˮ policy.
The U.S. withdrawal from Afghanistan in 2021 under Biden underscored this shift, marking
an ongoing reluctance to commit U.S. military forces and lead humanitarian missions,
particularly where strategic interests are not clearly at stake.
This lack of commitment to humanitarian interventions has made the impossible to conduct and
allowed human rights crises across the world to continue.
While some of these like the Uyghur Genocide in Xinjiang would not have attracted
intervention in the 1990s due to occurring in powerful states or those backed by major
powers like Russia and China, others such as the Rohingya Genocide in Myanmar or the
widespread casualties and victims of sexual violence in the Sudanese civil war may have.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, it is clear that humanitarian interventions used to play a central role in protecting human
rights, as they could override state sovereignty to do so.
In the present day, however, the changed international environment means that they are now
unable to protect human rights. Strong opposition by China and Russia and the increasing
isolationism of the United States means that R2P no longer acts as a deterrent and human rights
abuses are being carried out across the world without fear.
Though international courts are also flawed and have limited powere, this means that they are
more effective in protecting human rights.

Paragraph Two ➡ The Ability Of International Courts To Uphold
Human Rights And Hold Perpetrators To Account
Against: Human Rights Are More Effectively Protected By International Courts
Than Humanitarian Interventions
An important argument that international courts and tribunals are more effective than
humanitarian interventions in protecting human rights is that they are often able to convince
states to change their behaviour through legal rulings, without infringing upon state
sovereignty.
This is supported by a liberal perspective, which views the rules-based international order
and the moral pressure of the international community as having real power and the ability
to uphold human rights effectively.
The International Court of Justice ICJ encourages compliance with human rights by settling
disputes between states, whilst the European Court of Human Rights ECHR enables
individuals to directly bring cases against states for alleged violations of their rights.
The ECHRʼs judgements have not only forced states to respect the human rights of
individual complainants, they have also promoted legal and policy reforms in member states
to protect the human rights of wider groups. This demonstrates how courtsʼ moral pressure
can effect legal reforms within sovereign states that protect human rights.
For example, in Goodwin v. United Kingdom 2002, the Courtʼs ruling that the UKʼs
failure to legally recognise a transgender womanʼs gender reassignment violated her
rights to respect for private life Article 8 led the UK to enact the Gender Recognition
Act, allowing transgender individuals to obtain legal recognition of their gender.
The ICJ is also able to protect human rights by settling disputes between states and
creating pressure on states to comply, especially as states consent to the jurisdiction of the
ICJ and are supposed to accept its judgements as binding.
For example, in Democratic Republic of Congo v. Uganda 2022, the ICJ ordered
Uganda to pay $325 million in reparations to the DRC for its military intervention during
the late 1990s that included human rights abuses and the illegal exploitation of natural
resources. Uganda complied with the judgement and has made the first payment.
Furthermore, the International Criminal Court ICC and specialised international tribunals are
effective protecting human rights as, unlike humanitarian interventions, they can prosecute
perpetrators directly hold individuals accountable for severe human rights abuses.
In March 2025, former President of the Philippines Rodrigo Duterte was arrested and
transferred to the Hague for charges relate to his administrationʼs brutal ‘War on Drugsʼ
during which thousands of alleged drug dealers and users were killed without trial between
2016 and 2019.
This shows how the ICC can hold high ranking politicians to account for crimes against
humanity, including leaders outside of Africa where the vast majority of previous ICC
cases were focused on.
Similarly, the the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia ICTY was very
effective in holding to account those most responsible for war crimes during the Balkan
conflicts of the 1990s. It convicted 90 war criminals, including former President of the
Republika Srpska Radovan Karadžić, who was sentences in 2016 to 40 years in prison for
orchestrating atrocities, including the Srebrenica massacre, where over 8,000 Bosniak men
and boys were killed.

For: Human Rights Are More Effectively Protected By Humanitarian
Interventions Than International Courts
On the other hand, it can be argued that international courts are less effective than
humanitarian interventions in protecting human rights, as the enforcement of their rulings
depends heavily on state cooperation, which means that state sovereignty hinders their
effectiveness in many cases.

This is supported by a realist perspective that argues states are primarily driven by self-
interest and will prioritise their own sovereignty and interests rather than implementing an
international courtʼs decision that goes against them.
For realists, this means that courts lack true enforceability/power to protect human rights,
as they cannot compel states to comply with their decisions.
The ICJ is a clear example of this, as states must consent to its jurisdiction for disputes to be
heard and for rulings to be enforced. If a state refuses to recognise the Courtʼs authority, it is
unable to hear a case let alone enforce a judgement.
No case against the United States has ever been brought to the ICJ, despite widespread
reports of human rights abuses during the War on Terror, including torture and indefinite
detention at facilities such as Guantanamo Bay and Abu Ghraib prison.
Further, even when states do participate and recognise the ICJʼs jurisdiction, they often
ignore rulings that conflict with their strategic interests.
In the 2024 case of South Africa v. Israel the ICJ issued a ruling ordering Israel to
prevent genocidal acts and let humanitarian aid into Gaza, whilst late ordering Israel to
immediately halt its military offensive in Rafah in May 2024.
Israel didnʼt immediately halt its offensive in Rafah and has stopped humanitarian aid
from entering Gaza on numerous occasions since the ruling.
This demonstrates how the ICJ is ineffective due to its limited enforcement power
and reliance on state compliance for enforcement, as the UN doesnʼt have its own
police force.
The ECHR also faces challenges in enforcing human rights standards, as its decisions rely on
the political will of member states to implement them. Although ECHR judgments are legally
binding, enforcement is often inconsistent, with states such as Russia and the United Kingdom
resisting compliance on politically sensitive issues.
Before its expulsion from the Council of Europe in 2022, Russia repeatedly ignored ECHR
rulings on human rights violations in a number of cases related to political prisoners,
freedom of expression, and human rights abuses in Chechnya.
In the UK, prisoners are still not able to vote despite the ECHRʼs 2005 ruling in Hirst v.
United Kingdom, which found that a blanket ban on prisoner voting violated human rights.
States can therefore selectively comply with international courtsʼ rulings and ignore them
when they conflict with the governmentʼs domestic priorities and interests. This shows how
state sovereignty is a significant obstacle to international courts upholding rights, as courts
have no enforcement power.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
There is no doubt that state sovereignty limits international courtsʼ ability to uphold human
rights a lot of the time.
This being said, they are still more effective at protecting human rights than humanitarian
interventions in the present day, as they can put moral pressure on states to uphold rights,
whilst the ICC and international tribunals are able to hold the direct perpetrators of human rights
abuses to account.

Paragraph Three ➡ How Powerful States Can Undermine The
Ability Of International Courts To Uphold Human Rights
Against: Human Rights Are More Effectively Protected By International Courts
Than Humanitarian Interventions
A final argument that humanitarian interventions are more effective in upholding human rights
than international courts is that international courtsʼ rulings and enforcement disproportionately
impact weaker states while more powerful nations evade accountability. This undermines the
principle of human rights, which are supposed to be universal and upheld for all.
Human rights law often proves effective in influencing less powerful states due to
international pressure and the potential repercussions of not accepting the jurisdiction of
international courts or accepting their judgements, including sanctions or diplomatic
isolation.
However, more powerful states can ignore these pressures without consequence and
decide not to accept the jurisdiction of international courts or accept their rulings. As a
result, they are able to retain their sovereignty.
This is seen most clearly in the ICC, where 70 states including powerful nations such as Russia,
China and the United States havenʼt signed the Rome Statute, whilst the majority of the worldʼs
poorer and weaker states have been pressured by liberal states to sign the Rome Statute.
This means that the ICC doesnʼt have jurisdiction over these 70 countries and canʼt act
against individuals and leaders from these nations.
For example, it hasnʼt been able to investigate those responsible for genocide and
crimes against humanity by China against the Uyghur Muslim population in Xinjiang, as
China doesnʼt accept its jurisdiction.
Not only has this meant that the ICC has only been able to act on a very small number of
cases since its founding, it has also meant that it has disproportionately targeted leaders
from weaker or developing countries, especially within Africa, despite human rights abuses
occurring globally.
Up until 2023, all 31 individuals publicly indicted by the ICC were from African nations,
including Uganda, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Sudan. As a result, many
African Union states have threatened to withdraw from the ICC due to allegations of
bias.
This shows how the liberal ideal that international human rights law protects all citizens equally
is fundamentally flawed, as powerful states face less accountability and are able to use their
sovereignty to protect themselves, whilst weaker states are pressured into accepting the
jurisdiction of international courts and therefore have less sovereignty.

************************************************************************************************

For: Human Rights Are More Effectively Protected By Humanitarian
Interventions Than International Courts
On the other hand, it can be argued that international courts are in fact more effective than
humanitarian interventions in protecting human rights, as humanitarian interventions have
caused major civilian casualties and have sometimes been used selectively by the West as
justification for military actions that are not genuinely aimed at protecting human rights.
Military interventions, even those justified by humanitarian motives, inherently carry the risk
of collateral damage that can undermine the very human rights they aim to protect.
For example, NATO bombings in Kosovo 1999, while aimed at protecting the ethnic
Albanian population from violence by Serbian forces, mistakenly targeted civilian
infrastructure, including hospitals, schools, and residential areas, leading to the deaths
of hundreds of civilians.
Despite the strategic and security-driven motives of the War on Terror, the U.S. government
frequently used humanitarian language to justify its military interventions, including rhetoric
about liberating oppressed populations, promoting democracy, and protecting human
rights.
In reality, the Iraq War in particular involved widespread human rights abuses and the
death of an estimated 200,000 Iraqi civilians.
This demonstrates how interventions that are supposedly humanitarian can cause far more
damage to human rights than protection in many cases. The victims of these human rights
abuses are all in weaker states, as interventions havenʼt taken place against U.S. allies such
as Israel or against major powers such as China and Russia.
This is despite current reported human rights abuses by the Chinese government in
Xinjiang, by the Russian government in Ukraine and by the Israeli government in Hamas,
which the U.S. funds militarily, therefore showing that humanitarian interventions donʼt
uphold human rights equally for citizens of different states.
Further, it can be argued that international courts are more effective in upholding human rights,
as in 2023 and 2024, the ICC demonstrated it doesnʼt just pursue cases in Africa and is
therefore able to uphold human rights, even against powerful states.
In 2023, the ICC issued an arrest warrant for Russian President Vladimir Putin and for
alleged war crimes related to the conflict in Ukraine. It was able to as Ukraine, where the
alleged crimes took place, has signed the Rome Statute.
Additionally, the ICC has applied for arrest warrants against Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin
Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant, as well as former Hamas leader
Yahya Sinwar, for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity in Gaza.
It was able to do so as the Palestinian Territories have signed the Rome Statute.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, it is clear that both humanitarian interventions and international courts are unable to
prevent human rights abuses that are perpetrated by leaders of powerful states or their allies.
This undermines the protection of human rights globally as it means that in reality they are not
universal.
However, as humanitarian interventions seem highly unlikely in the present state of global
politics and international courts are effective in upholding human rights in some ways in some
countries, international courts are more effective in upholding human rights.
