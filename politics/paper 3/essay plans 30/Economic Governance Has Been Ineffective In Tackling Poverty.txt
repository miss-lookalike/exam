Evaluate The View That Global
Economic Governance Has Been Ineffective In Tackling Poverty. (30)

Paragraph One ➡ The International Monetary Fund and World
Bankʼs Promotion Of Neoliberal Capitalism
Against: Economic Global Governance Has Been Effective At Tackling Poverty
One of the most significant roles played by IGOs in trying to tackle poverty is that done by the
IMF and World Bank. Both are financial institutions that use lending to help countries escape
financial crises and develop economically.
Crucially it can be argued they are effective in tackling poverty, as they place conditions on
these loans that require countries to move towards austerity and free market economies,
which they see as central to promoting economic development.
The conditions imposed by the IMF often include liberalising trade, privatising inefficient state
enterprises and reducing fiscal deficits to help countries attract investment, boost
competitiveness, and lay the foundation for sustainable economic development.
Even though these measures have been unpopular, particularly the Structural Adjustment
Programmes SAPs) of the 1980s and 1990s, advocates argue that they are essential for
addressing fundamental economic problems. While the reforms involve short-term pain,
such as cutting government spending or implementing fiscal discipline, they are necessary
to restore long-term stability and growth.
In cases like Ghana in the 1980s, IMF-backed reforms had some success in reducing
inflation, stabilising the currency, and fostering economic growth.
Crucially, this argument is in line with Classical Economic Development Theory, which suggests
that when countries in the Global South open their economies to trade and investment, they can
specialise in producing goods where they have a comparative advantage. This specialisation
leads to greater efficiency and productivity, helping countries grow wealthier over time.
By engaging in global capitalism, nations can benefit from increased capital flows, access
to technology, and new markets for their products.
For example, after India opened up its economy to global markets in the 1990s, it
experienced rapid growth averaging 67% annually, especially in sectors like information
technology IT and services. India became a global IT hub, with cities like Bangalore and
Hyderabad emerging as major tech centres.
This growth helped reduce extreme poverty from 45% in 1993 to around 6% by 2024,
according to World Bank data.

************************************************************************************************

For: Economic Global Governance Has Been Ineffective At Tackling Poverty
On the other hand, perhaps the strongest argument that global economic governance hasnʼt
been effective at tackling poverty is that the neoliberal, free market reforms promoted by the
IMF and World Bank have in fact consistently deepened poverty in countries that were already
the worldʼs poorest. These countries had little choice but to accept austerity conditions as they
required financial assistance and it had disastrous effects.
Free market reforms imposed by the IMF and World Bank under Structural Adjustment
Programs SAPs) in the mid-late 20th century deepened poverty and dependence on external
financial aid.
Receiving much needed financial assistance was made conditional on accepting these
reforms, which included privatisation, deregulation, and reduced public spending on
essential services, including cuts to education and healthcare.
In line with dependency theory, this entrenched African nationsʼ position in exploitative global
economic structures, forcing them to remain dependent on exporting raw materials while
importing expensive manufactured goods.
As of 2023, approximately 38% of subsaharan Africa remains in extreme poverty and the
region remains heavily dependent on exports of raw materials, with 75% of its exports
consisting of unprocessed goods like oil and minerals, making it highly vulnerable to global
price fluctuations.
The free market reforms were based on flawed economic thinking and imposed despite
resistance by many in subsaharan African countries.
In Nigeria, an SAP was introduced in 1986, leading to privatisation, deregulation, and
devaluation of the currency. These reforms caused inflation to rise, public services to
deteriorate, and living standards to decline, particularly for the poor.
By the early 1990s, poverty had increased from 28% to 66%, as real wages fell and the cost
of living soared, making essential goods unaffordable for many.
In Zambia, the SAP resulted in mass layoffs in the 1990s, especially from state-owned
enterprises, driving poverty levels from 49% in 1989 to over 80% by the mid-1990s, whilst
also continuing the countryʼs dependence on the export of copper, rather than helping it to
develop.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, it is clear that the World Bank and IMFʼs policies have done far more harm to developing
countries than they have helped and they are therefore ineffective at reducing poverty.
SAPs werenʼt supported by the majority of the populations in subsaharan Africa that were
subject to them, but their governments had to accept them as they had little other choice and
they had disastrous effects and significantly increased poverty in many countries, whilst
furthering their dependence.
Until these institutions stop believing in free market capitalism and Classical Economic
Development Theory, they will not be effective in reducing poverty.

Paragraph Two ➡ The World Trade Organisationʼs Promotion Of
Free Trade
Against: Economic Global Governance Has Been Effective At Tackling Poverty
Another important argument that economic global governance has been effective in tackling
poverty is how the WTOʼs promotion of free trade and globalisation has driven global economic
growth and poverty reduction as a result.
The WTOʼs primary aim is to promote free trade and prevent against protectionism, ensuring
that global trade flows as smoothly, predictably, and freely as possible. It achieves this by
providing a forum for negotiating trade agreements and settling disputes between
members.
The Bali Trade Facilitation Agreement 2013, for example successfully reduced red tape
and simplified customs procedures globally. It is estimated to reduce global trade costs
by 14.3% on average, helping small businesses and landlocked countries participate
more effectively in international trade.
This has clearly been successful, as between 2000 and 2024, the global economy has
nearly tripled in size, with nominal global GDP increasing from about $33 trillion in 2000 to
approximately $104 trillion by 2023, according to data from the World Bank and IMF.
Increased global trade and investment have allowed countries to grow their GDP, particularly by
using cheap labour as a competitive advantage to mass produce goods, and poverty rates have
fallen in regions integrating into the global economy.
According to World Bank data, extreme poverty rates globally have dropped from 35% in
1990 to 9% in 2019.
Chinaʼs transformation is one of the most significant examples of how this promotion of free
trade has sustained economic growth and poverty reduction. China gradually opened its
economy to global markets from the 1980s, but after its accession to the World Trade
Organisation WTO in 2001, it integrated deeply into global supply chains and its growth
skyrocketed.
China used its cheap labour to became the worldʼs manufacturing hub. As a result, it
became the worldʼs second-largest economy, with GDP growth averaging nearly 10%
annually over three decades.
This economic growth lifted more than 800 million people out of extreme poverty, making it
the largest poverty reduction effort in history. The poverty rate fell from 88% in 1981 to less
than 1% by 2019.

************************************************************************************************

For: Economic Global Governance Has Been Ineffective At Tackling Poverty
On the other hand, it can be argued that economic global governance hasnʼt been effective at
tackling poverty, as free trade and global trade have simply perpetuated global capitalism,
which is inherently unequal for developing nations and entrenches their poverty and
underdevelopment, which benefits the worldʼs richest countries.
Structural theory argues that wealthier, industrialised countries (the core) dominate global
markets, while poorer, less developed nations (the periphery) are dependent on exporting
raw materials.
As a result, the North-South Divide remains and countries in the Global South remain stuck
in a cycle of exporting low-value commodities while importing expensive manufactured
goods, keeping them in a state of underdevelopment.
For example, crude oil and gas account for around 92% of Nigeriaʼs exports and about
65% of government revenue. These revenues are extremely volatile and this structural
dependence traps Nigeria in poverty, with approximately 40% of Nigerians living below
the poverty line.
The WTO, IMF and World Bank present free market economics and austerity reforms as the
clear path to economic development and poverty reduction, in line with Classical Economic
Development Theory.
However in reality the countries that have experienced significant economic growth and
poverty reduction were driven by significant state investment whilst embracing global trade.
China and South Korea, among other countries, saw huge economic growth and poverty
reduction in the past few decades, with China alone bringing 800 million people out of
poverty. They didnʼt do so by simply opening their economies to market forces, though.
Instead, their governments actively guided development through strategic industrial
policies, investment in education and infrastructure, and state-led export strategies. The
stateʼs role in managing and directing economic growth was critical, particularly in
protecting nascent industries and steering investment into key sectors.
Despite this, the ideas of Classical Economic Development Theory continue to drive the
thought of economic global governance, perhaps as it serves the interests of wealthier
nations as structural theory suggests.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, there is no doubt that the integration of developing economies into global trade has
significantly reduced poverty and the WTO has played a significant role in this and therefore
has been effective in reducing poverty.
This doesnʼt mean that this poverty reduction was due to free market capitalism spreading,
however, as in reality the impressive economic growth of many East Asian countries was
driven by effective state intervention rather than simply leaving the market alone.

Paragraph Three ➡ The United Nationʼs Efforts To Bring
International Focus To Tackling Poverty
Against: Economic Global Governance Has Been Effective At Tackling Poverty
A final argument that economic global governance has been effective in tackling poverty is that
key IGOs have fostered a global commitment to poverty reduction. Institutions such as the
United Nations UN, the World Bank, and the International Monetary Fund IMF have launched
initiatives aimed at reducing poverty and enhancing development, making them central to their
mandates and aims.
The Millennium Development Goals MDGs), established by the UN in 2000, targeted
halving global poverty by 2015. This goal was met ahead of schedule, driven by the UN
driving collaboration through the Economic and Social Council in particular.
The Sustainable Development Goals SDGs), launched in 2015, now aim to eliminate
extreme poverty by 2030. Crucially, they take an approach to tackling poverty that
addresses key structural issues including inequality, climate change and access to
education & healthcare.
International Governmental Institutions have also recognised how climate change will
disproportionately affect developing countries and how this has the potential to further poverty.
As a result, the United Nations and developed countries have committed to supporting
developing countries financially.
At the 2024 United Nations Climate Change Conference COP29 in Baku, developed
countries strengthened their commitment to tackling climate change by committing to
provide at least $300 billion annually into developing countries by 2035 to support their
efforts in dealing with climate change.

************************************************************************************************

For: Economic Global Governance Has Been Ineffective At Tackling Poverty
On the other hand, it can be argued that economic global governance has been ineffective in
tackling poverty as their decision making is largely dominated by the worldʼs wealthiest
countries which continue to be primarily motivated by their economic success, rather than by
tackling poverty or climate change.
For example, both the IMF and World Bank are structured in a way that disproportionately
favours wealthy, Western nations. Voting power both the World Bank and IMF is determined
by financial contributions, meaning that richer countries have significantly more influence
over decisions than poor countries, the primary recipients of their loans.
The U.S. is the only country with veto power over structural changes within the World
Bank and holds around 16.5% of the total voting power in the IMF, which, combined with
the requirement that major decisions need an 85% majority, effectively grants the U.S. a
de facto veto over key IMF policies.
The worldʼs rich countries have consistently failed to put helping the worldʼs poorest countries
develop and reduce poverty above their own economic interests.
This reflects Worldʼs Systems Theory, which argues that the world economy operates as a
single system, where wealth is continually extracted from the periphery (developing
nations) to benefit the core (developed nations) and the developed nations have no interest
in disrupting this.
This can be seen in the WTOʼs Doha Development Round, which aimed to create a
multilateral free trade agreement that reduced trade barriers and addressed inequalities
between developed and developing countries.
However, after a decade of negotiations, it failed as developed countries refused to stop
subsidising their farmers even though this significantly harmed the developing worldʼs
exports and kept millions in poverty.
This shows that even though developed countries and IGOs claim to support free trade
and reducing poverty, their highest priority is always their own countryʼs economic
success.
Further, even though developed countries first committed to giving $100B annually to
developing nations at COP15 in 2009 and have reaffirmed this commitment at COP26 and
the G7 in 2021, there has still been little follow through to meet this commitment. This shows
that they may claim to prioritise tackling poverty and climate change, but in fact they donʼt.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, the United Nations does play an important role in bringing states together to tackle
poverty and the SDGs arenʼt unimportant, however it would be wrong to argue that economic
global governance is effective in tackling poverty as it is dominated by developed countries
which continue to prioritise their own economic success over poverty reduction and the
economic growth of developing nations.

************************************************************************************************
************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, economic global governance has had mixed results in tackling poverty and is
therefore somewhat effective. The World Trade Organisation has facilitated global trade and
this has irrefutably helped hundreds of millions in Asia out of poverty, particularly due to Chinaʼs
integration into global trade.
However, economic global governanceʼs promotion of free market capitalism as a solution to
poverty and the underdevelopment of the Global South is false and fails to account for how the
export of raw materials traps countries in poverty and how state intervention was key in East
Asian countriesʼ development.
As the IMF and World Bank aggressively pushed free market capitalism as a solution, they
did great harm to countries through SAPs and increased rather than tackled poverty.
Until it global economic governance recognises that Classical Economic Development
Theory is flawed, it will not be effective in tackling poverty.
