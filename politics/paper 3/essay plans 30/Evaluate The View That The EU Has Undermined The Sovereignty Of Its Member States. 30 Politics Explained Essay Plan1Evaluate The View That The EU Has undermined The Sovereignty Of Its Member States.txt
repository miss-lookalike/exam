Introduction
Key Definitions
Sovereignty is the authority of a state to govern itself and determine its own future, making and
enforcing laws independently of external control.
Paragraph One ➡ The Influence Of The EU On National Policy-
Making
For: EU Law Overrides National Law
A central argument that the EU has undermined the sovereignty of its member states is that it
has removed the ability of their parliaments to legislate on key policy areas, as EU law overrides
national law.
Evaluate The View That The EU Has Undermined The Sovereignty Of Its Member States. 30 Politics Explained Essay Plan2The principle of EU law supremacy means that, in cases of conflict, EU law takes
precedence over national legislation, significantly limiting member statesʼ control over
policy areas that the EU controls.
This principle was firmly established in the Costa v ENEL 1964 ruling by the European
Court of Justice ECJ, which declared that EU law is supreme and binding on all
member states.
This supremacy affects various policy areas, including competition law, trade, environmental
standards, and consumer protection, where EU regulations must be adopted even if they
contradict national preferences.
For example, member states cannot introduce laws to aid important sectors of their
economy through tariffs, subsidies or other rules, as this discriminates against other
countries/industries and the EU single marketʼs laws designed to protect fair competition
have supremacy.
In 2019, France introduced a law to limit pesticide use specifically to protect the wine
industry, but in 2021, the European Court of Justice ECJ ruled it as non-compliant with
its agricultural standards and internal market rules.
France was subsequently forced to withdraw the law, demonstrating how EU
agricultural policies can override local environmental and industry-specific
initiatives.
This has also meant that countries with struggling sectors can be harmed, such as Italy
which has struggling automotive and steel industries and may have wished to support
them in the context of global competition to aid their competitiveness and prevent job
losses. However, they are unable to due to EU law.
For instance, EU environmental directives set binding targets for emissions and renewable
energy that member states must meet, restricting their flexibility to develop their own
environmental policies, even though there may be domestic political pressures to do so.
The EUʼs environmental standards and climate targets require Poland to adopt strict air
quality and emission targets, even though Poland heavily relies on coal for energy,
showing how EU law can force states to adopt policies that are against their interests
and that they otherwise wouldnʼt.
Poland unsuccessfully sought to prevent these ambitious standards being
introduced in 2020 but was overruled under Qualified Majority Voting, which meant
it no longer had a veto.
This supremacy of EU law, enforced by the ECJ, therefore member statesʼ sovereignty,
requiring them to align with EU standards in areas critical to national economies and social
policies, which would otherwise fall under sovereign control.
Against: Member States Retain Control Over Key Areas Of Policy
On the other hand, it can be argued that the EU doesnʼt significantly undermine the sovereignty
of its member states, as member states maintain full control over critical policy domains.
In areas including national defence, health, education, and taxation, the EU canʼt decide a policy
without agreement from all member states. This gives them a veto and means they maintain
sovereignty over the most important powers of a state.
Evaluate The View That The EU Has Undermined The Sovereignty Of Its Member States. 30 Politics Explained Essay Plan3Each countryʼs military remains fully under national control, with no EU-led standing army or
compulsory EU military requirements, allowing countries like France and Poland to conduct
independent defence operations.
Similarly, in education, member states set their own curriculums and funding levels,
preserving cultural and educational autonomy.
For example, France maintains a highly centralised education system where curriculum
and standards are set at the national level, while Germany allows each federal state to
determine its own curriculum and education policies, reflecting regional preferences
and cultural differences.
The principle of subsidiarity further protects national sovereignty by ensuring that decisions are
made at the most local level possible, meaning the EU can only act if objectives cannot be
effectively achieved by individual member states.
This principle is designed to limit the EUʼs authority to areas where collective action is
genuinely necessary, allowing member states to retain sovereignty in areas where they are
best positioned to act independently.
This demonstrates how the EU seeks to maintain national sovereignty as much as possible.
Further, when new EU treaties are introduced, member states can negotiate opt-outs from
specific EU policies, which allow them to maintain control in areas they consider sensitive or
essential.
These opt-outs allow member states to participate in EU integration selectively, ensuring
that they can safeguard sovereignty in areas they deem crucial, demonstrating that EU
membership does not necessarily override national sovereignty in fundamental policy
areas.
Denmark, for example, opted out of adopting the euro and continues to use the Danish
krone, preserving its independent monetary policy.
Further, EU membership is ultimately voluntary, and countries have the sovereign right to
leave the Union if they feel their sovereignty or national interests are compromised, as
demonstrated by the United Kingdomʼs decision to withdraw via Brexit.
The process, clearly outlined in Article 50 of the Lisbon Treaty, provides a legal pathway
for any member state to exit the EU, preserving each countryʼs ultimate sovereignty over
its membership.
If a country leaves, they regain full control over all areas of policy, including immigration
and trade policy, as can be seen with Brexit.
This reflects the realist perspective that regional organisations are only used by
states to bolster their own power. They are fundamentally intergovernmental and
states will leave if it is their interest.
Judgement
Overall, there is no doubt that EU membership reduces the sovereignty of member states in
relation to trade policy and regulation in particular. However, the more persuasive argument is
that it hasnʼt undermined the sovereignty of member states, as they still retain control over the
most important areas of policy - taxation and foreign policy - and can ultimately secure opt outs
for future EU treaties or leave the EU to regain their sovereignty.

Evaluate The View That The EU Has Undermined The Sovereignty Of Its Member States. 30 Politics Explained Essay Plan4Paragraph Two ➡ The EUʼs Commitment To An Ever Closer Union
For: The EU Is Committed To An Ever Closer Union
Another key argument that the EU undermines the sovereignty of member states is that it is
committed to an ever closer union and has gradually reduced the sovereignty of its member
states over time, as seen in particular with the growing importance of qualified majority voting.
The EUʼs commitment to “ever closer union,ˮ is stated in the Treaty of Rome 1957 and
reaffirmed in the Maastricht Treaty 1992, committing member states to a vision of deeper
integration, which would require the further erosion of member statesʼ sovereignty.
Key EU leaders, such as former European Commission President Jean-Claude Juncker and
French President Emmanuel Macron, have advocated for deeper EU integration and a
stronger centralised governance structure, with Macron often promoting the idea of
“strategic autonomyˮ for Europe.
This ideological commitment to “ever closer unionˮ suggests there will be a continued shift
toward federalisation, where national sovereignty will be further diluted as more decision-
making power is transferred to supranational EU institutions.
The commitment to an “ever closer unionˮ can be seen in the increasing use of Qualified
Majority Voting since the EU was founded, which undermines member statesʼ sovereignty as
they can be forced to accept decisions they disagree with.
QMV enables EU legislation to be passed by a ‘double majorityʼ in the Council of the
European Union rather than requiring unanimity, which means that countries can be
outvoted and cannot veto critical policies affecting their national interests.
A double majority requires 55% of member states to vote in favour, whilst these
member states must represent at least 65% of the EU population.
Over the years, QMV has been extended to include areas like immigration, trade, and
climate policy, resulting in more decisions where individual states cannot exercise veto
power. The Lisbon Treaty 2009 expanded QMV to over 40 new areas, including judicial
cooperation and energy policy, further reducing member statesʼ sovereignty over important
policy domains.
In 2015, under QMV, the EU approved mandatory migrant relocation quotas, requiring
countries like Hungary, Poland, and the Czech Republic to accept a share of refugees,
despite their strong opposition.
Poland was again overruled in 2020 when the EU set more ambitious climate targets
under QMV, mandating a reduction in carbon emissions by at least 55% by 2030,
despite Polandʼs concerns about the impact on its coal-dependent economy.
Against: The EU Increases The Overall Influence Of Member States Due To
Pooled Sovereignty
On the other hand, it can be argued that this move to an “ever closer unionˮ hasnʼt in fact
undermined the sovereignty of member states. Instead, it has pooled their sovereignty together
to give them much greater collective influence overall.
By pooling their sovereignty with the EU, member states are given much greater global
influence than they otherwise would have, including in relation to trade, geopolitics and climate

and protection in global affairs far beyond what they could achieve alone.
Acting as a bloc representing 15% of global GDP, the EU has negotiated major trade
agreements that benefit all member states, such as the EUCanada Comprehensive
Economic and Trade Agreement CETA which eliminated tariffs on 98% of goods traded
between the two regions, and the EUJapan Economic Partnership Agreement.
These agreements offer member states access to new markets, strengthened
intellectual property protections, and lower tariffs, enhancing economic growth for both
large economies like Germany and smaller ones like Estonia.
Without being a member of the EU, smaller states in particular wouldnʼt have been able
to negotiate these agreements and get such favourable trade terms.
Further, in global organisations, such as the World Trade Organisation WTO, International
Monetary Fund IMF, and World Health Organisation WHO, the EUʼs collective bargaining
power allows member states to influence international standards on climate, digital policy,
and health.
For example, the EU played a critical role in shaping the Paris Climate Accord by
pushing for binding commitments to limit global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius, setting
an ambitious precedent for other countries and upholding its importance even when the
US pulled out under Donald Trump.
It must also be recognised that it seems unlikely that member states will cede more sovereignty
to the EU, given the rise of right wing populism in countries such as Poland, Hungary and the
Netherlands. Leaders in these countries have promoted the idea of “taking back controlˮ from
Brussels and strongly opposed giving more control over immigration in particular to
supranational EU institutions.
Judgement
Overall, the EUʼs commitment to an ever closer union has reduced member statesʼ sovereignty
but it has not undermined it. The increased use of qualified majority voting has reduced
member statesʼ sovereignty over time by removing their veto over more areas of policy,
however member states remain sovereign over the most important areas of policy and they
have been able to increase their influence over global trade and climate policy especially by
pooling their sovereignty together.
Paragraph Three ➡ The Influence Of The EUʼs Core Institutions
On State Sovereignty
For: The EU Has A Number Of Supranational Institutions That Limit Member
Statesʼ Sovereignty
A final important argument that the EU undermines the sovereignty of its member states is that
supranational institutions have significant control within the European Union. This limits member
statesʼs sovereignty as they donʼt have a veto over decisions made within these institutions.
Supranationalism is when member states agree to transfer some sovereignty to a central
body that can make binding decisions on behalf of all member states, therefore removing
the ability for a member state to veto any of their decisions.
Evaluate The View That The EU Has Undermined The Sovereignty Of Its Member States. 30 Politics Explained Essay Plan6The two most important EU supranational institutions are the European Central Bank ECB
and the European Commission.
The ECB dictates monetary policy for the 20 Eurozone countries, setting interest rates and
managing inflation. This can be argued to have undermined the sovereignty of member states,
as it removed a critical tool of economic control from national governments.
Countries including Italy, Germany and Greece cannot independently adjust their monetary
policy, despite having very different economies with very different needs.
This lack of sovereignty was shown most clearly after the Eurozone Crisis, when Greece
was forced to accept stringent austerity measures, imposed by the IMF, EU and European
Central Bank as a condition for bailout packages.
This was despite the fact they were deeply unpopular measures that inevitably led to
long-term economic hardship, including a 25% decrease in GDP and youth
unemployment rates exceeding 50% at the height of the crisis.
The European Commission also has extensive authority to propose and enforce legislation
across the Union, including related to trade, environmental standards and competition. It is the
EUʼs executive body, responsible for proposing and enforcing legislation, implementing
decisions, and managing the EUʼs budget.
As a supranational institution, it is empowered to take action against member states that
violate EU rules.
For instance, Poland and Hungary have faced sanctions and funding cuts from the
European Commission over alleged rule-of-law violations, highlighting the Commissionʼs
power to impose penalties on non-compliant member states.
This shows how supranational institutions limit sovereignty, as they can remove funding
from states and impose sanctions on them without their consent.
Against: The Most Powerful EU Institutions Are Intergovernmental
On the other hand, it can be argued that the EU hasnʼt undermined member statesʼ sovereignty
as its most powerful institutions where the most critical decisions are made are in fact
intergovernmental bodies that preserve the sovereignty of each individual member state.
The European Council, comprising the heads of state or government from each member state,
is the primary decision-making body for key EU policies and its strategic direction, particularly
in areas like foreign policy and security.
Decisions within the Council require unanimity in almost all cases, meaning that every
country has a veto in areas that could deeply impact national sovereignty, such as tax
policy, foreign policy or constitutional matters.
In 2015, Ireland vetoed proposed EU changes to corporate tax rules, protecting its
competitive low-tax regime from alteration by other member states.
During negotiations over EU responses to major crises, such as the 2008 financial crisis or
the COVID19 pandemic, the European Council plays a central role in shaping the Unionʼs
strategy,.
For example, during the COVID19 pandemic, the European Council coordinated a
historic recovery fund, the Next Generation EU, to support member statesʼ economic
recovery through a €750 billion package.

debates between fiscally conservative states like the Netherlands and Austria, which
advocated for loans rather than grants, and heavily affected countries like Italy and
Spain, which argued for substantial financial support to recover from the crisis.
Rather than some states having to accept completely the other sides view, the
intergovernmental structure meant that this compromise was reached and the
sovereignty of member states was maintained).
Similarly, the Council of the European Union allows ministers from each member state to
participate directly in legislative processes, voting on policies that affect their national interests.
Whilst it does use Qualified Majority voting in most areas, on sensitive issues such as
taxation and defence, decisions in the Council still require unanimity, meaning that no
member state can be forced into policies that contradict its fundamental interests.
Judgement
Overall, there is no doubt that the EUʼs supranational institutions mean that member states
arenʼt fully sovereign. This being said, there sovereignty has only been reduced rather than
completely undermined as they still have a veto over the ultimate strategic direction of the EU
and major policy changes, due to the fact that the intergovernmental European Council is the
most important EU institution.
Overall Argument
In conclusion, the EU has reduced the sovereignty of its member states, but it hasnʼt
undermined it. The EUʼs supranational institutions and increased use of qualified majority voting
have reduced sovereignty over trade and regulation policy in particular.
This being said, in these areas pooling sovereignty has increased member statesʼ overall
influence, whilst they still retain a veto and full sovereignty over the EUʼs strategic direction and
the most important areas of policy, in particular taxation and foreign policy/defence.
This means that member states remain largely sovereign, especially given the fact they
have only granted their sovereignty to the EU on a temporary basis and they can fully regain
their sovereignty by leaving the EU at any point, as shown by Brexit.
