Evaluate The View That Globalisation Has Been Effective In Reducing Poverty. (30) 

Introduction
Key Definitions
When looking at globalisationʼs effect on poverty, this essay considers both the effects of the
economic growth generated by globalisation and the impact of political globalisation;
international governmental organisationsʼ IGOs) efforts to reduce poverty globally.

************************************************************************************************
************************************************************************************************

Paragraph One ➡ The Impact Of Economic Growth
For: Globalisation Has Led To Economic Growth, Bringing People Out Of Poverty

A key argument that globalisation has been effective in poverty reduction is that it has driven
global economic growth. Globalisation has facilitated trade liberalisation, expanded markets,
and increased foreign direct investment FDI, which in turn has boosted industrialisation and
job creation, particularly in developing economies.
Between 2000 and 2024, the global economy has nearly tripled in size, with nominal global
GDP increasing from about $33 trillion in 2000 to approximately $104 trillion by 2023,
according to data from the World Bank and IMF.
Increased global trade and investment have allowed countries to grow their GDP, particularly by
using cheap labour as a competitive advantage to mass produce goods, and poverty rates have
fallen in regions integrating into the global economy.
According to World Bank data, extreme poverty rates globally have dropped from 35% in
1990 to 9% in 2019.
Chinaʼs transformation is one of the most significant examples of how globalisation can drive
sustained economic growth and poverty reduction. China gradually opened its economy to
global markets from the 1980s, but after its accession to the World Trade Organisation WTO in
2001, it integrated deeply into global supply chains and its growth skyrocketed.
China used its cheap labour to became the worldʼs manufacturing hub. As a result, it
became the worldʼs second-largest economy, with GDP growth averaging nearly 10%
annually over three decades.
This economic growth lifted more than 800 million people out of extreme poverty, making it
the largest poverty reduction effort in history. The poverty rate fell from 88% in 1981 to less
than 1% by 2019.

************************************************************************************************

Against: Globalisation Has Led To The Exploitation Of Labour
On the other hand, this economic growth has come at the expense of very bad working
conditions for much of the worldʼs poor.
Globalisation has led to a “race to the bottomˮ in labour standards, where companies move
production to countries with cheaper labour, often resulting in low wages and poor working
conditions that perpetuate poverty and sometimes constitute human rights abuses.
This exploitation undermines the potential for long-term poverty reduction, as workers
remain trapped in cycles of poverty despite employment. According to the International
Labour Organization, about 1.5 billion workers worldwide are in vulnerable employment,
often in precarious jobs created by global supply chains.
For example, as Bangladesh integrated into global supply chains, particularly in the garment
industry, millions of unskilled workers were employed as it became a hub for low-cost
production due to its cheap labour. However, this resulted in workers being trapped in
cycles of poverty, earning as little as $95 per month and often in very dangerous factory
conditions.
The Rana Plaza collapse in 2013 killing over 1,100 workers, illustrating the consequences
of the “race to the bottomˮ in labour standards.
Similarly, in December 2024 it was revealed that both Nestlé and Starbucks used coffee
farms in Yunnan province, China where there was both child labour and where workers
were forced to work excessive hours without proper contracts.
As cheap, manual labour has been outsourced to the ‘global southʼ, rich Western countries such
as the United States have been able to capture the high value-add parts of global supply
change in order to maintain economic supremacy and higher wages.
Though globalisation has brought many out of poverty, it therefore hasnʼt significantly
altered the dominance of the West in the global economy, with countries such as America -
and MNCs including Apple and Amazon - benefitting greatly from global supply chains and
cheap, exploited labour.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, there is no doubt that the free market capitalism spread by globalisation is deeply
flawed, with poor labour conditions a direct consequence. This being said, the poverty
reduction in China in particular since its integration into the global economy has been
remarkable and it is therefore clear that globalisation on the whole has been effective in
reducing poverty.

Paragraph Two ➡ The Spread Of Free Market Capitalism
For: Globalisation Has Improved The Economy By Spreading Free Market
Capitalism

Another key argument in favour of globalisation being effective in poverty reduction, is that it
has spread free market capitalism, which proponents of modernisation theory argue is
necessary for development.
Modernisation theory argues that globalisation has advanced the global economy and reduced
poverty by spreading capitalism and free market principles.
As nations integrate into the global economy, they adopt free market economic practices
such as deregulation, privatisation, and market liberalisation.
These reforms attract foreign direct investment FDI, promote entrepreneurial activity,
and drive technological innovation, which stimulates job creation and development. This
further enables developing countries to move away from reliance on agriculture and raw
materials to industrialised and service-based sectors.
For example, after India opened up its economy to global markets in the 1990s, it
experienced rapid growth averaging 67% annually, especially in sectors like information
technology IT and services. India became a global IT hub, with cities like Bangalore and
Hyderabad emerging as major tech centres.
This growth helped reduce extreme poverty from 45% in 1993 to around 6% by 2024,
according to World Bank data.
These reforms have been strongly promoted by the IMF and World Bank, including by making
financial assistance to struggling countries conditional on implementing free market reforms.

************************************************************************************************

Against: Globalisation Has Deepened Inequality And Marginalisation
On the other hand, it can be argued that modernisation theory and free market capitalism are
flawed and donʼt benefit the poorest in the world. Globalisation may have lifted many out of
poverty and contributed to global economic growth, but is has also deepened inequality within
and between nations.
Whilst free market capitalism is inherent to globalisation, the rapid economic growth
experienced by countries including China and South Korea in fact often involved significant
state intervention.
Economic globalisation has led to a rise in high-paying jobs for skilled workers, while wages for
unskilled labour have stagnated or declined.
In 2021, the richest 1% of the global population controlled 38% of global wealth, while the
poorest half held less than 2%.
The urbanisation and industrialisation encouraged by globalisation has also led to displacement
of rural communities as land is repurposed for commercial, industrial, or agricultural ventures.
This has led not only to the destruction of rural ways of life, it has also pushed them further
into poverty, as they are unable to access the benefits of globalisation in the same way as
those who are urban, skilled workers.
For example, in India, more than 50 million people have been displaced due to development
projects since independence, with many of these linked to industrial and infrastructure
projects aimed at integrating into the global economy.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, globalisation has been effective in reducing poverty, but this has not been an inevitable
consequence of free market capitalism as modernisation theory suggests. The key factor was
integration of countries into the global economy, but in reality free market capitalism increases
inequality within countries and the rapid poverty reduction and development seen in many
Asian countries was heavily influenced by state intervention.

************************************************************************************************

Paragraph Three ➡ The Impact Of Political Globalisation
For: Political Globalisation and International Cooperation Have Reduced Poverty
Globally
A final argument that globalisation has been effective in reducing poverty is that political
globalisation has fostered a global commitment to poverty reduction. Institutions such as the
United Nations UN, the World Bank, and the International Monetary Fund IMF have launched
initiatives aimed at reducing poverty and enhancing development, making them central to their
mandates and aims.
The Millennium Development Goals MDGs), established by the UN in 2000, targeted
halving global poverty by 2015. This goal was met ahead of schedule, driven by the UN
driving collaboration through the Economic and Social Council in particular.
The Sustainable Development Goals SDGs), launched in 2015, now aim to eliminate
extreme poverty by 2030. Crucially, they take an approach to tackling poverty that
addresses key structural issues including inequality, climate change and access to
education & healthcare.
IGOs focusing on global economic governance, in particular the IMF and World Bank, have also
sought to reduce poverty by providing financial assistance to countries, as well as advice on
how to run and reshape their economies.
For example, in the early 2000s, Cambodia sought to modernise its central banking and
financial systems. The IMF provided technical assistance to improve Cambodiaʼs monetary
policy framework and banking regulations.
By enhancing the capabilities of the National Bank of Cambodia and providing training to
local officials, the IMF helped the country stabilise its banking sector, manage inflation more
effectively, and sustain its economic growth.

************************************************************************************************

Against: IMF Imposed Reforms Have Deepened Poverty In Subsaharan Africa
On the other hand, it must be recognised that the free market neoliberal reforms promoted by
the IMF and World Bank have deepened poverty in many Subsaharan African countries, with
the Structural Adjustment Programmes SAPs) of the 1980s and 1990s failing to adapt to the
particular circumstances of each country and having disastrous consequences as a result.
Free market reforms imposed by the IMF and World Bank under Structural Adjustment
Programs SAPs) in the mid-late 20th century deepened poverty and dependence on external
financial aid.
Receiving much needed financial assistance was made conditional on accepting these
reforms, which included privatisation, deregulation, and reduced public spending on
essential services, including cuts to education and healthcare.
In line with dependency theory, this entrenched African nationsʼ position in exploitative global
economic structures, forcing them to remain dependent on exporting raw materials while
importing expensive manufactured goods.
As of 2023, approximately 38% of subsaharan Africa remains in extreme poverty and the
region remains heavily dependent on exports of raw materials, with 75% of its exports
consisting of unprocessed goods like oil and minerals, making it highly vulnerable to global
price fluctuations.
The free market reforms were based on flawed economic thinking and imposed despite
resistance by many in subsaharan African countries.
In Nigeria, an SAP was introduced in 1986, leading to privatisation, deregulation, and
devaluation of the currency. These reforms caused inflation to rise, public services to
deteriorate, and living standards to decline, particularly for the poor.
By the early 1990s, poverty had increased from 28% to 66%, as real wages fell and the cost
of living soared, making essential goods unaffordable for many.
In Zambia, the SAP resulted in mass layoffs in the 1990s, especially from state-owned
enterprises, driving poverty levels from 49% in 1989 to over 80% by the mid-1990s, whilst
also continuing the countryʼs dependence on the export of copper, rather than helping it to
develop.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, political globalisation provides an important and potentially highly useful framework for
tackling poverty and will potentially have a major impact in the future, but due to the disastrous
policies forced on countries by the IMF and World Bank in the last 20th century so far it has
worsened poverty. Political globalisation has the potential to be effective in reducing poverty,
but it has not been effective to date.

************************************************************************************************
************************************************************************************************

Overall Argument
In conclusion, the most persuasive argument is that globalisation has been effective in reducing
poverty.
This is not because free market capitalism or political globalisation have been effective in
reducing poverty. In fact, the IMF and World Bank have deepened poverty in many countries
due to their prescription of free market capitalism as a solution to poverty, when in reality it is
often a cause of greater poverty.
Instead, the main reason why globalisation has on balance been effective in reducing poverty is
that the opening up of the global economy to many Asian countries, especially China, led to
massive reductions in the poverty rates of these countries, as a consequence partly of the
effective intervention of their states.
Chinaʼs economic growth alone has lifted over 800 million people out of extreme poverty and it
is therefore extremely difficult to argue that globalisation has been ineffective in reducing
poverty.
