Introduction
Key Definitions
Sovereignty is the authority of a state to govern itself and determine its own future, making and
enforcing laws independently of external control.
Paragraph One ➡ Supranational Institutions
For: Supranational Institutions Undermine National Sovereignty And Impose
Decisions On States
An important argument that regional organisations undermine state sovereignty is that many of
them have supranational institutions, which can lead to decisions being imposed on states even

The European Union EU exemplifies this through institutions like the European Commission
and the European Court of Justice, which hold significant power to enforce regional policies
and legal rulings across all member states.
The European Commission has extensive authority to propose and enforce legislation
across the Union, including related to trade, environmental standards and competition. It is
the EUʼs executive body, responsible for proposing and enforcing legislation, implementing
decisions, and managing the EUʼs budget.
As a supranational institution, it is empowered to take action against member states that
violate EU rules.
For instance, Poland and Hungary have faced sanctions and funding cuts from the
European Commission over alleged rule-of-law violations, highlighting the Commissionʼs
power to impose penalties on non-compliant member states.
This shows how supranational institutions limit sovereignty, as they can remove
funding from states and impose sanctions on them without their consent.
The ECJ also undermines sovereignty and reduces the influence of EU member states by
striking down national laws if they contradict EU law in areas such as competition law, trade
and environmental standards.
For example, member states cannot introduce laws to aid important sectors of their
economy through tariffs, subsidies or other rules, as this discriminates against other
countries/industries and the EU single marketʼs laws designed to protect fair
competition have supremacy.
This has meant that countries with struggling sectors can be harmed, such as Italy
which has struggling automotive and steel industries and may have wished to support
them in the context of global competition to aid their competitiveness and prevent job
losses. However, they are unable to and have lost influence due to EU law.
Similarly, in the African Union AU, the Peace and Security Council has occasionally deployed
peacekeeping forces in conflict zones, even in cases where the affected state has shown
reluctance.
In Sudan, AU peacekeeping forces were deployed in the Darfur region to contain the
violence, despite resistance from the Sudanese government.
In Somalia, the AUʼs mission AMISOM was critical in stabilising the country, yet it required
significant AU intervention that overrode Somali sovereignty to some extent.
Although the AU operates primarily as an intergovernmental body, its ability to impose
security measures highlights how regional organisations can limit member statesʼ
sovereignty over domestic affairs in the interest of regional stability.
Against: The Majority Of Regional Organisations Are Intergovernmental
On the other hand, it can be argued that the majority of regional organisations donʼt undermine
sovereignty as, unlike the EU, they operate on an intergovernmental basis where member states
maintain a high degree of autonomy and control over decision-making.
In ASEAN, decision-making is based on consensus and the core principle of non-
interference in each countryʼs internal affairs, meaning that no policy or initiative can be

This consensus model allows ASEAN countries, such as Indonesia, the Philippines, and
Thailand, to engage in regional cooperation while retaining full control over policies
affecting their sovereignty.
Similarly, the Arab League is structured as a loose cooperative alliance that prioritises
sovereignty and national autonomy, focusing on coordination rather than imposing binding
policies.
Member states like Egypt and Saudi Arabia can pursue their foreign policies
independently, even when these differ from regional priorities, such as during the Gulf
statesʼ diplomatic shift toward Israel.
Furthermore, even in highly integrated regional organisations with supranational institutions like
the EU, member states retain complete control over core areas of national interest, such as
taxation, defence, and foreign relations. It can therefore be argued that their sovereignty and
influence has been largely maintained.
EU member states maintain independent defence forces and make sovereign decisions on
military matters, with NATO serving as the primary defence alliance in Europe rather than
the EU itself.
France, for example, has conducted military interventions in Africa independently, as
seen in its operations in Mali, showing that EU countries do not have to coordinate
military actions through the EU.
This is also seen in other regional organisations:
In the African Union, member states retain authority over their defence policies, with
countries making autonomous decisions about their military engagements, even though
the AU provides a platform for cooperation.
ASEAN operates under a non-interference policy that allows countries to independently
manage foreign relations and defence, which is crucial given the diversity of political
and security alliances among members, such as Thailandʼs military cooperation with the
US and Cambodiaʼs ties with China.
Judgement
Overall, the supranational institutions of the EU in do reduce the sovereignty of its member
states, however only to a small extent and the EU as a whole doesnʼt undermine the sovereignty
of member states as they retain significant control and can ultimately leave the alliance if they
wish.
As other regional organisations are integovernmental, they donʼt undermine the sovereignty or
reduce the influence of states at all.

Overall Influence?

Nation Stateʼs Interests

and mean that agreements signed arenʼt specifically designed to serve the interests of one
state.
Furthermore, these agreements and free trade areas integrate economies further into the
globalised economy, making each stateʼs economy far more dependent on other countries
and reducing the governmentʼs control.
In ASEAN, for example, the ASEAN Free Trade Area AFTA facilitates the reduction of tariffs
and barriers within the bloc, but this also increases dependency among member states on
regional trade rather than fostering truly diversified, self-sustaining economies which states can
better control.
Countries like Myanmar and Laos, with smaller, less competitive economies, can find
themselves at a disadvantage within ASEAN, as the interests of stronger economies like
Thailand and Singapore shape regional economic policies.
Similarly, in the African Union smaller economies arguably struggle to influence trade policies
and agreements with other countries, as they are naturally dominated by bigger economies
such as Nigeria, South Africa and Kenya. These economies have more to offer other countries
and therefore have more leverage in negotiations.
For example, in trade discussions with the European Union, Nigeria was able to negotiate oil
export agreements that included favorable tariff reductions for crude oil, benefiting its
dominant oil industry. Smaller West African economies, such as Benin and Togo, which rely
more on agricultural exports, found their interests overlooked, as the focus on energy
exports didnʼt cater to their primary economic needs.
The supranational nature of the European Central Bank ECB and its pooled monetary policy
means that EU member states have even less sovereignty over economic policy, particularly
poorer countries that struggle to influence strategic decisions on monetary policy.
During the eurozone crisis, for example, Greece was required to implement strict austerity
measures and economic reforms in exchange for financial support from EU institutions,
even though these measures were highly unpopular domestically and led to severe
economic hardship.
Against: Regionalism Enhances Statesʼ Overall Influence
On the other hand, it can be argued that regional organisations in fact increase statesʼ influence
by enabling them to act collectively on the global stage, amplifying their voice in international
forums and negotiations with more powerful countries.
This protects the sovereignty of smaller states in particular, which would otherwise have
little power in negotiations with powers such as Russia, China and the United States.
African countries have historically been marginalised in international relations, however the
African Union has helped them to unite and increase their voice by presenting a unified stance
on global issues like climate change and trade reform.
For example, the AU has been a member of the G20 since 2023 and at the 2024 Summit in
Rio de Janeiro, it represented the interests of African states by raising the importance of
debt restructuring and calling for access to financing so that African states could achieve

Crucially, regional organisations also increase statesʼ influence by allowing them to secure
much more favourable trade agreements with other countries than they would otherwise be
able to.
ASEAN signed The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership RCEP with China,
Japan and other key trading partners in 2020 and is projected to increase trade for
countries in the agreement by 10.4% annually.
The regional organisation has been highly successful in using its collective economic
weight to maintain favourable trade partnerships with both the US and China but not to
be dictated to by either.
This demonstrates how regional organisations in fact increase the sovereignty and
influence of small states, which would otherwise be vulnerable to exploitation by larger
economies that dictate the rules of trade and investment in a globalised economy.
Judgement
Overall, by pooling sovereignty with neighbouring states, regional organisations do reduce
statesʼ control somewhat. However, this is only to a small extent and is far outweighed by the
increased influence it gives small states in the global politics and global economy. As a result,
regional organisations increase state influence and sovereignty, rather than undermining it.
Paragraph Three ➡ Political Regionalism
For: Regional Organisations Can Erode Countriesʼ Independent Cultural and
Political Identity
Finally, it can be argued that regional organisations reduce the sovereignty and influence of
nation states, as they can lead to the erosion of cultural and political identity, as countries adapt
to shared norms and standards that may not align with their unique values, traditions or political
cultures.
In the EU, for instance, member states must comply with liberal policies and attitudes on issues
like immigration, human rights, and social welfare, which may conflict with local customs or
political preferences.
Countries such as Hungary and Poland have resisted EU mandates on migration and judicial
reforms, arguing that these policies clash with their national values, yet they remain bound
by EU rules and face sanctions for non-compliance.
In 2015, for example, the EU imposed mandatory migrant relocation quotas on Hungary,
Poland, and the Czech Republic despite their opposition, forcing them to accept a share of
refugees.
Countries may feel forced to remain within regional organisations and accept changes they
disagree with due to the economic benefits that free trade areas bring.
For example, Malaysia has sometimes opposed stricter ASEAN-wide environmental
regulations that could impact its palm oil industry, a major economic sector.
However, Malaysia continues to adhere to ASEANʼs collective framework that seeks to
tackle climate change due to the substantial economic benefits of being part of ASEANʼs

Against: States Can Leave Regional Organisations To Regain Complete
Sovereignty
On the other hand, it can be argued that this argument is weak, as states can simply leave
regional organisations to regain full sovereignty if they want to, demonstrating how sovereignty
is never permanently lost to regional organisations and countries will only stay in them if they
serve their interests.
This reflects the realist perspective that regional organisations are only used by states to
bolster their own power. They are fundamentally intergovernmental and states will leave if it
is their interest.
In 2020, the United Kingdom exercised its right to leave the EU under Article 50 of the
Lisbon Treaty, after a referendum determined that a majority of British citizens wanted to
regain full control over issues such as immigration, trade, and regulatory autonomy.
This precedent highlights that even in highly integrated organisations like the EU,
membership is ultimately voluntary, and states can choose to leave if they believe the
organisation no longer aligns with their national interests.
Similarly, ASEAN and the African Union do not impose binding membership; while no
ASEAN member has ever exited the blocs, the flexibility to withdraw reinforces the
sovereignty of each state, ensuring that participation remains a strategic choice rather than
a permanent obligation.
It must also be noted that the intergovernmental nature of all regional organisations apart from
the EU means that member states canʼt be forced into decisions they disagree with/decisions
that are against their strategic interests. Their sovereignty is therefore maintained.
For example, in 2016 a ruling by the Permanent Court of Arbitration PCA in favour of the
Philippines invalidated Chinaʼs broad claims in the South China Sea under international law.
However, ASEAN didnʼt formally endorse the ruling because Cambodia has strong ties with
China and didnʼt want to damage its relations with the country.
This shows how intergovernmental institutions maintain the sovereignty of member
states, even the smallest and weakest states, as they ultimately retain a veto over any
decisions.
Judgement
Overall, regional organisations donʼt reduce state sovereignty or influence as any control ceded
to them is only temporary and can be fully regained at any point by leaving the organisation, as
Brexit demonstrated.
Overall Argument
In conclusion, regional organisations donʼt undermine state sovereignty at all or reduce state
influence. Member states of the EU in particular do lose direct control over some areas of
policy, however they donʼt lose sovereignty permanently as Brexit demonstrated that they can
leave and fully regain their sovereignty.
States decide to enter regional organisations as, by pooling their sovereignty with neighbouring
states, they increase their overall influence rather than diminishing it. If it reduced their overall
