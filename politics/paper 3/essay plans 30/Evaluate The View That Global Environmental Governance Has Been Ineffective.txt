Evaluate The View That Global Environmental Governance Has Been Ineffective. (30) 

Paragraph One ➡ The Tragedy Of The Commons vs Sustainable
Development
For: The Tragedy Of The Commons Has Made Global Environmental
Governance Ineffective
One of the most important arguments that global environmental governance has been
ineffective is that the tragedy of the commons has prevented states from effectively coming
together to tackle climate change, because it is against each statesʼ national interest to do so.
The tragedy of the commons theory argues that when individuals act in their self-interest to
maximise resource use, they often deplete or degrade the shared resources upon which
they all depend.
In the context of the environmental, this therefore suggests that because resources like the
air, oceans, and biodiversity are collectively owned, they will be overused because
individual actors—whether countries, corporations, or individuals—each lack sufficient
incentives to limit their exploitation for the common good.
The past two decades have shown how the tragedy of the commons has prevented effective
international action to tackle climate change, as countries have continued to prioritise their
short-term economic interests over the long-term sustainability of the planet. Despite
international agreements like the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement, global greenhouse
gas emissions have continued to rise and many countries have failed to meet their targets.
Despite the Paris agreement setting a target of 1.5°C and the the Intergovernmental Panel
on Climate Change IPCC warning in a 2018 Special Report that a 2°C rise in global
temperatures would substantially increase the damage of climate change, the current
commitments of countries place the world on a trajectory for a 2.7°C to 3.1°C rise in
temperatures by the end of the century.
Fossil fuel-dependent countries like Saudi Arabia and Russia have historically resisted
international efforts to reduce carbon emissions due to the economic benefits of oil and gas
exports.
Similarly, many industrialised nations have continued to have high emissions, as they have
resisted the substantial upfront costs and economic restructuring that transitioning to
cleaner energy requires.
For example, Australia proposed a target to reduce emissions by 5% below 2000 levels
by 2020 under the Copenhagen Accord, however it failed to meet this target due to a
continued reliance on coal and its emissions in fact increased by 20%.
The deep-green ecology perspective, supported by activists such as Greta Thunberg, argues
that the international community will be unable to tackle climate change unless there is a
fundamental change to the current economic and industrial systems, which are unsustainable.
Without transitioning away from capitalism, this perspective argues that states will continue
to pursue their national economic interests and the tragedy of the commons will prevail and
lead to the destruction of the planet.

************************************************************************************************

Against: Global Environmental Governance Has Overcome The Tragedy Of The
Commons Through Sustainable Development
On the other hand, it can be argued that environmental global governance has been effective as
it has brought the international community together to tackle climate change through
sustainable development.
Sustainable development suggests that protecting the environment and economic
prosperity can reinforce each other, rather than needing to fundamentally change the
economic system away from capitalism.
This is supported by the shallow-green ecology perspective, which is grounded in the idea
that environmental protection can coexist with economic development and that there are
often market-based solutions to reduce environmental impacts, such as emissions trading
schemes, pollution controls, and investment in cleaner technologies.
Sustainable development has been the basis of international efforts and frameworks to tackle
climate change, as it has encouraged countries to transition to renewable energy sources and
invest in green technology, whilst continuing to pursue their economic interests and seek
economic development.
International agreements including the Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement show this
reflected this.
The former included carbon trading mechanisms, a market mechanism that allowed
countries to meet emissions targets through investment in emissions-reduction projects
in developing countries, rather than just through domestic reductions.
The latter commits each country to submit its own climate action plan, known as a
Nationally Determined Contribution NDC, detailing its emissions reduction targets and
strategies. This has allowed countries to make changes that align with their specific
economic interests and driven ambitious policy reforms to tackle climate change.
For example, the European Unionʼs Green Deal commits EU member states to
achieve net-zero emissions by 2050, with intermediate targets to cut emissions by
at least 55% by 2030.
To achieve this, the EU has introduced a series of initiatives, including the
European Climate Law, which legally binds member states to these targets, and
the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, which places a carbon tariff on
imports.
There has been a significant rise in renewable energy use in the past two decades as a
result, with renewable energy source such as solar and wind accounting for 29% of global
electricity generation in 2020.
Some countries have successfully transitioned to renewable energy sources, whilst
continuing to grow economically. For example, Costa Rica regularly generates over 98%
of its electricity from renewable sources and has used its success to promote eco-
tourism, enhancing its economy while preserving its natural resources.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, global environmental governance has committed states to taking measures to tackle
climate change, however it has ultimately been ineffective as the tragedy of the commons
means that these measures arenʼt radical enough to significantly reduce global warming, with
global temperatures estimated to rise by 2.7°C to 3.1°C by the end of the century.
Both developed and developing status are unwilling to sacrifice economic growth and power to
tackle climate change and without a fundamental restructuring of the economic system, the
tragedy of the commons will therefore prevent environmental global governance from being
effective.

Paragraph Two ➡ The Effectiveness Of The UNFCCC and
International Agreements
Against: The UNFCCC Has Been Effective In Getting The International
Community To Tackle Climate Change
Another important argument that environmental global governance has been effective is that
the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change IPCC and United Nations Framework
Convention on Climate Change UNFCCC have been effective in stressing the urgency of
tackling climate change and bringing the international community together to commit to action.
The IPCC is an international panel of scientists focused on climate change. Every six years, it
produces an Assessment Report on the current progress of climate change and of international
efforts to tackle it.
These reports have convinced the public and governments of the urgency of tackling
climate change and played an important role by informing international agreements and
national policies.
The UKʼs Climate Change Act of 2008, which set legally binding targets for emissions
reductions, was strongly influenced by IPCC findings on the link between carbon
emissions and global warming.
Its assessment reports, including its fourth in 2007 and sixth in 2021/22 have
consistently stressed the importance of immediate action to limit global warming to
1.5°C. This influenced the Paris Agreementʼs target to limit global warming to “well
belowˮ 2°C, ideally 1.5°C.
The UNFCCC, established in 1992 at the Rio Earth Summit, is the primary international forum for
climate action. It has institutionalised climate discussions through annual conferences, known
as Conferences of the Parties COPs), which have resulted in major agreements including the
Kyoto Protocol 1997, the Copenhagen Accord 2009 and the Paris Agreement 2015. These
agreements have been effective in committing states to reduce their environmental damage.
The Kyoto Protocol was the first time that countries agreed to binding greenhouse gas
GHG emissions targets. It required 37 industrialised countries, including EU members,
Japan, and Canada, to reduce GHG emissions to 5% below 1990 levels during the first
commitment period 20082012.
The EU, Russia, Japan and other countries successfully achieved reductions beyond
their targets as a result.
The Paris Agreement was adopted at COP21 in 2015 and brought together 194 countries,
including major emitters like the United States, China, and India, to pledge to limit global
warming to well below 2°C, with efforts to stay within 1.5°C.
Though its emissions targets arenʼt binding it is particularly significant as it requires all
countries to take part in emissions reduction efforts, including developing states, and
includes a “Global Stocktakeˮ every five years to assess collective progress towards
meeting the goals of limiting global warming to 1.5°C or 2°C.
Additionally, the 2021 COP26 conference in Glasgow saw 190 countries agreeing to phase
down coal and 130 countries pledging to halt deforestation by 2030—both landmark
commitments that were facilitated by the Paris Agreement.

For: Sovereignty Has Prevented Global Environmental Governance From Being
Effective
On the other hand, it can be argued that state sovereignty has prevented global environmental
governance from being effective.
The importance of sovereignty within the international system means that international
climate agreements have had to rely on voluntary commitments by states rather than being
able to create legally binding commitments.
This reliance on voluntary commitments has been a significant obstacle to effective climate
action, as it allows nations to scale back or delay their goals based on domestic priorities or
economic pressures without facing international consequences.
The Kyoto Protocol set legally binding targets, however this often wasnʼt successful, as states
are ultimately sovereign and able to ignore international law.
For example, Japan initially committed to emissions reductions under the Kyoto Protocol,
but struggled to meet its targets after the 2011 Fukushima nuclear disaster, as it shifted from
nuclear to fossil fuels.
Further, the Kyoto Protocol was significantly weakened by the fact that the U.S. Senate
refused to ratify the protocol, as it argued that the Protocol unfairly exempted developing
countries from emissions reductions and could harm the United Statesʼ status as the worldʼs
largest economy.
Both of these examples demonstrate how environmental global governance has no
enforcement power to enforce commitment to tackling climate change, due to sovereignty
being a significant obstacle.
The Copenhagen Accord encouraged countries to set their own emissions reduction targets
and report on them, however these targets were also not met by many countries as they
continued to prioritise their economic interests.
For example, Australia committed to reducing emissions by 5% from 2000 levels by 2020,
but its reliance on coal and natural gas production resulted in it failing to do so and
continuing to be one of the highest per capita emitters globally.
Though it is relatively recent, the Paris Climate Agreement has also struggled to get states to
commit to ambitious changes that would enable it to meet its goal of reducing global warming
to 1.5°C, with analysis suggesting that current NDCs place the world on a trajectory for a 2.7°C
to 3.1°C rise in temperatures by the end of the century.
China, the worldʼs largest emitter, pledged to peak its emissions before 2030 and reach
carbon neutrality by 2060, yet continues to approve new coal-fired power plants and faces
rising emissions as it continues to prioritise economic growth.
The United States once again withdrew from the Agreement in 2025 after Trumpʼs
reelection. Trump is a climate sceptic and has promised to boost domestic fossil fuel
production under the mantra “drill baby drillˮ.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, global environmental governance has been ineffective, as it has failed to get states to
commit to the significant emissions reductions that would be necessary to slow down global
warming and the impacts of climate change.
Agreements have set ambitious targets, however these have been consistently missed due to
participating states prioritising their own economic growth and the lack of enforcement
mechanisms held by international institutions as a consequence of state sovereignty.

Paragraph Three ➡ Efforts To Commit Developed States To
Support Developing States
For: Global Environmental Governance Has Been Ineffective Due To The
Division Between The Developed and Developing World Over Responsibility
A final argument that global environmental governance has been ineffective is that is has failed
to get developed states to meaningfully accept responsibility for climate change and support
developing states.
Between 1850 and 2019, the United States and European Union countries together
accounted for approximately 50% of global cumulative CO₂ emissions, while Africa
contributed less than 4% and South Asia only 3%.
As a result, developing nations believe that developed states are responsible for climate
change and should pay to tackle it, whilst developing nations should not be expected to
compromise their economic growth, which is essential to lifting millions out of poverty and
meeting basic human needs.
International agreements have emphasised the need for developed countries to support
developing nations in climate mitigation and adaptation efforts. However, this support has fallen
short of expectations, as developed states have prioritised their own economic interests.
In 2010, developed countries pledged to mobilise $100 billion annually in climate finance by
2020 to assist developing nations under the UNFCCCʼs Green Climate Fund GCF, but this
target was repeatedly missed and only met by 2022.
In 2019, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development OECD reported
that only $79.6 billion was mobilised, leaving a $20 billion gap.
Nations in Sub-Saharan Africa, highly vulnerable to climate impacts, have struggled to
fund necessary adaptation measures, such as building resilient infrastructure and
securing water resources, due to limited financial support.
Furthermore, even the funds that are allocated often fall short of addressing critical needs. For
example, while the Green Climate Fund has provided financing for renewable energy projects
and disaster resilience in countries like Kenya and Bangladesh, the scale of assistance remains
inadequate relative to the challenges faced.
In 2021, the Prime Minister of Barbados Mia Mottley called out developed countries for their
limited contributions, arguing that climate finance must go far beyond the $100 billion
pledged to address both mitigation and adaptation adequately.

************************************************************************************************

Against: Global Environmental Governance Has Been Effective In Committing
Developed States To Support Developing States
On the other hand, it can be argued that global environmental governance has in fact been
effective in getting developed states to accept responsibility and committing them to support
developing states financially.
International climate agreements have placed a strong emphasis on the historical responsibility
of developed nations to support developing countries in mitigating and adapting to climate
impacts. This is based on the Common but Differentiated Responsibilities CBDR principle,
which recognises that all countries share the obligation to address climate change, but
suggests that developed countries should take the lead because of their historical emissions
and greater financial resources.
The Kyoto Protocol only committed to developed states to reduce their emissions, as
developing countries successfully argued that their low historical emissions and economic
needs should exempt them from binding targets.
The Paris Agreementʼs Indiaʼs Nationally Determined Contribution framework allows for
flexibility in emissions targets, so that developing states could commit to less ambitious
targets that would prevent economic growth and poverty reduction.
For example, Indiaʼs Nationally Determined Contribution NDC under the Paris
Agreement allows it to increase emissions until it reaches its peak by 2030, balancing its
developmental needs with its climate commitments.
The 2024 COP29 Conference in Baku also included a highly signifiant commitment to
provide at least $300 billion annually into developing countries by 2035 to support their
efforts in dealing with climate change, a significant increase from the $100 billion annual
commitment that was met in 2022.
This will mean that vital projects will continue across the developing world, such as in
Morocco, where the the Green Climate Fund funded a $98 million project to develop the
worldʼs largest concentrated solar power plant, the Noor Ouarzazate complex, which
has significantly boosted the countryʼs renewable energy capacity and reduced its
reliance on fossil fuels.
Importantly, at COP27 developed states also finally agreed to a Loss and Damage Fund, which
will compensate countries vulnerable to extreme weather for the irreversible impacts of climate
change.

************************************************************************************************

Judgement
Overall, it is clear that there has been significant progress in getting developed states to
support developing states. Particularly in the past few years, when the $100 billion annual
target has been met, the Loss and Damage Fund has been agreed and a higher $300 billion
annual target has been set.
This being said, environmental global governance has still been ineffective as developing states
and especially developed states arenʼt making radical enough changes to meaningfully tackle
climate change and reverse its impacts.

Overall Argument
Overall, it is clear that global environmental governance has been ineffective, because it has
been unable to convince states to act against their economic interests due to the tragedy of the
commons and because it has been able to enforce emissions reductions on states due to state
sovereignty.
The UNFCCC and the agreements it has led to have certainly mobilised the international
community behind tackling climate change and have convinced developed states that they
must support developing states to do so.
This being said, states remain unwilling to put their economic interests aside and prioritise
tackling global warming, which has resulted in moderate commitments that the IPCC
predicts will lead to the devastating result of a 2.7°C to 3.1°C rise in temperatures by the end
of the century.
